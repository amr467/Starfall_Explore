Chapter 10: What the Fire Took

They had finished Plan A an hour after sunrise, and everyone was pleased with it.

It was a good plan. It had a shape and an order, and every person in it knew their place. Figo had said his part back one last time, with his eyes open, which was new, and Trista had told him, “Well done,” in the voice she used on children and horses, and he had glowed. The light came through the seams of the hauler in thin gold wires, and for a few minutes it felt like any other morning.

Then James changed.

It wasn’t much. The smile stayed where it was. But the rest of him came up out of its slouch, the way a man straightens when the bill arrives, and his eyes went out of focus for a moment, the way they did.

“What,” he said slowly, “if Plan A fails?”

They all looked at him.

“It won’t,” said Vael. “I’ll make sure of it.”

“I appreciate that. Truly, I do.” James met his eyes and did not look away. “But we need a Plan B.”

For a while no one said anything. A flake of rust ticked down the inside of the hull.

“Then we fight it with everything we’ve got,” said Figo at last. “With or without this so-called Jack.”

Trista had been looking at the walls. The scorched plates, the dents, the old rivets that had once held a siege-hauler together and now held nothing. She looked at them for a long time, as though she were memorizing a room. Then she looked at the three of them, and she smiled, and her eyes were bright, and she was holding something back behind them with both hands.

“If we die,” she said, “I don’t have any regrets. I’m glad I got to make this journey with you.” She breathed in. “As long as we beat this thing.”

Figo let out a long huff, like a bellows, and pulled Sheela into his lap and held her tight. “We’re hunters,” he said. “We go to sleep every night ready for the next day to be the last one. And as long as we have brothers and sisters, we’re never really dead. I’ve got no regrets either. We finish this thing, once and for all.”

“Same goes for me,” said James. He turned the cask over in his hands and didn’t open it. “I’ve always been the cursed child. I’d like it to end for something good, for once.”

Vael looked at all three of them, and he was angry. He had known he would be. It had been waiting at the bottom of the conversation, and now it came up all at once, hot and shaking, because they were talking about it as if it were already settled. As if it were weather.

“Plan B will never happen,” he said.

He stood. No one argued.

“That’s enough talking. Let’s prepare.”

The swipe was perfect, which was the worst of it.

Nobody saw the Dracol get up. They had been busy being alive. Jack had gone out of the world a few breaths ago, and in those few breaths every crack the white fists had made had closed, obsidian running back into obsidian, the split in the shoulder knitting, the pale wing thickening, until the thing on the stones might never have been touched. It rose without a sound. It took the measure of the plaza with one flat look. Then the tail came across, not the whip-end this time but the thick of it, low and fast, at the height of a man’s waist, and finished exactly where it began.

Figo was still standing where he had been standing, with Sheela lifted over his head in one fist and his mouth open on the end of his shout. He looked puzzled. He looked down.

The tail had gone through him at the belt. For a moment he was only a man with a line drawn across him. Then the line opened.

Armor and leather and man parted together, and what was inside him came out onto the stones in a rush that steamed in the sun. The top half of him fell, and his pauldrons rang as they struck, and the sound of the rest was wet and flat and went on much too long. The legs stood a moment longer, because nobody had told them. Then they folded.

He was not dead. That was the part Vael would never be able to put down. Figo lay on his back with one arm flung out, and his chest labored, and his eyes went looking for the thing that had been in his hand. Sheela had come down on the stones a little way off, and her haft, which had taken the Dracol’s wing and not complained, split down its whole length where she struck. She lay with her blade on its side and Little’s knot of grass still tied beneath it, green against the black. His fingers closed on nothing, and opened, and closed.

“Sheela,” he said.

It came out mild and surprised, the way a man says his wife’s name when he comes in and finds the kitchen empty. She had come back to his hand every time he had ever thrown her. She lay where she lay.

“Sheela…”

Then the hand opened, and did not close again.

Vael did not move. Trista, beside him, did not move either. It was the only thing either of them could do. The sound was still in the air, short and flat and wet, like a door shut in another house, and the smell had begun to arrive, hot iron and something under it.

“Figo?” said Trista.

Then: “FIGO!”

She was on her feet, and the scream tore out of her, and then it broke, and what came after wasn’t a word. “No… no, no, no…”

On the east wall, a long way off, James had stopped waving. He stood at the rail with his hands slack at his sides, and his face had gone the color of paper. He had not needed his eyes to unfocus. He had seen it coming a breath before it came, and had told himself it was only a number.

“Fate,” he whispered to the empty gallery. “Has got us.”

The Dracol turned its head. It looked at Trista, and at the one on the ground beside her, and it looked for a long moment, with something in its throat that was neither a growl nor a breath. Vael looked back. There were words in his mouth, a great many of them. He couldn’t find the first one.

The red along its throat began to climb.

It went from the banked glow of coals through orange to white, lattice by lattice, the molten map lighting from the jaw down to the chest, all of it but one dark channel between two plates. Trista saw it first. She did not scream. She had no time left. She put both hands against the middle of Vael’s chest, exactly where she had put them a few minutes ago to start his heart again, and shoved.

He went backward across the stones. He saw her as he went, and she wasn’t afraid. Her mouth was open. She had something to tell him; he could see it gathered behind her teeth, the last of a long list.

The beam took it.

It was not fire. Fire was something that happened to things. This was a river let off its leash, a bar of white and orange as thick as an old tree, and the paving beneath it ran to glass. It crossed the plaza in a breath. For one heartbeat she was still there: a shape on its knees, black as a burnt log, with its arms still out as if to push and the shape of its mouth still open on whatever it had meant to say. Then the heat shook the air, and the shape fell inward, and was ash.

The smell reached him a moment later. He would not be able to name it, and he would know it for the rest of his life.

Two scraps of white cloth came drifting down out of the hot air, fine as paper. One of them had a hem the color of rust.

James had watched all of it.

He looked at the Dracol, and then, because it was the only thing left to do, he looked at Vael and let his eyes go out of focus. The world printed its small type. Vael’s number was a thin, shivering thing, hardly a number at all, and it was the last one left.

He had said it himself, that morning in the hull: numbers settle, and only a shock shakes them loose. Two shocks had come and gone, and he read the board for what they had bought. It was almost nothing. The boy at the center of the board had stopped. He sat in the dust with his legs gone and his mouth open, and a man in that state summons nothing. It was not the system that needed shaking now. It was Vael.

James turned over the thought of staying at the rail, and the number did not move. He turned over the drop, and it rose a hair. He turned over the way Vael would look at him on the way down, and it rose again.

His gut was never wrong. He had just never asked it to be kind.

He climbed onto the rail of the Auditors’ Gallery. He took a last look at the sky, as if checking whether it had anything to add.

“Well, well, well,” he said.

He stepped off.

The daggers came out of him before he had fallen ten feet: dark, long, weightless, dozens, then a hundred, flying out ahead of him in a wedge, and he let them pull him down the length of the air. He crossed the plaza in a long black arc, with the cask banging at his hip and his coat streaming behind him. His mouth was open. He might have been laughing.

“ENDARKEN!”

Every blade found it. They went in to the hilt, across the back, the neck, the ruined shoulder, and for the space of a breath the Dracol stood stuck through with darkness like a pincushion, and its head swung around.

Then the obsidian closed over them. It ran like cooling glass around every blade, and the blades went out one by one, and the Dracol was whole again. It had barely been scratched.

James landed on the stones in a crouch. His eyes found Figo, only for a moment, the way you look for a hand to hold. Then they found Vael, thirty feet away, on his back, with his mouth open and no sound coming out of it.

“So long, Vael!” James shouted. His voice cracked in the middle and he didn’t mind. He opened his arms. “Now it’s your turn! Give it your al—”

The light came down.

For the length of a breath he was a dark shape with his arms flung wide, laughing, haloed in white. Then he was not. When it had passed there was nothing where James had been. The cask had come loose at the last moment, its cord burnt through, and it dropped out of the glare and landed on the stones three paces from Vael and rolled, once, and stopped. Something inside it sloshed. It wasn’t even scorched.

For a long time there was no sound at all.

Vael lay where Trista had thrown him. He looked at the sky, and then he turned his head and looked at the rest. Figo, in two places. Sheela on her side with her haft split and a knot of green grass under her blade. A dark shape on the stones, and beside it a scrap of white with a rust-colored hem, lifting and settling in the hot air as if it were breathing. A cask on its side. A shoulder guard with every buckle still done up, lying by itself, looking as though it had only been set down.

He didn’t cry. He wanted to, and nothing came. It was too large. It stood in the middle of him like a house with all its lights off.

And then the room came back.

It came the way Aava had said her mother’s voice had come back to her: with no warning at all, from nowhere, in the middle of the most common hour of the day. Pale boards. A window. The sun on the floor, laid over the plaza like a second skin of light, so that the stone showed through the boards and the boards through the stone. It had more in it than before. A second chair. A cup with a chip in its rim. Something baking, somewhere out of sight. Things he could not have made up.

Saela stood at the window with her back to him, humming. It was a lullaby, a little flat in the third bar.

“I told you there were two keys,” she said, without turning. “I told you that you had the first.”

She turned. She was not smiling this time. It was the same careful face, with all the care taken off.

“I’m sorry, child. I had hoped you would never need the second.”

Out on the plaza, the Dracol lowered its head. Its claws found the stone and dragged, and the stone shrieked.

Vael got up. He hadn’t decided to. His legs simply remembered what they were for.

“Grief isn’t the price,” said his mother, gently, over the shriek. “It’s proof. It’s proof of what you were protecting. Don’t let anyone shame you out of the size of it. Not even yourself.”

The Dracol took one step, then another.

“And don’t hold it all up alone. I did. It was a mistake.”

“I’m not alone,” said Vael. His voice sounded like someone else’s, and it was very steady. “They’re right here.”

He looked at the cask. He looked at the scrap of white. He looked at the shoulder guard with its buckles done up, and the split haft, and the green knot in the dust.

Saela laid her hand against the glass. “Then, when it comes, say its name. Say it as if you mean it.”

The Dracol ran.

It came the way it had come the first time, dead straight, but faster, because it knew him now. The plaza shook. The lattice along its chest burned white, every channel of it but one.

Vael put his hands together in front of his chest.

And the room went out.

He was standing on open ground, in a wind that smelled of burning, under a sky that had gone wrong. Light was tearing down out of it in a cascade, each star trailing fire, and between him and the falling sky there was a figure. White. Luminous. Arms spread wide, holding something back. Not succeeding. Still trying.

He had seen her like that a hundred times, in an inn bed, in the grey before dawn. He had never once seen her turn.

She looked back over her shoulder, and it was the face from the room.

“You must,” she said. The sky roared over the words and she raised her voice above it. “Don’t let it destroy—”

The Dracol’s claws hit the stone, and hit it again.

“You are our only hope, my child.”

Vael closed his hands into a fist.

“Please remember me.”

The light was in her hair and along her arms. She was looking at him, and there was nothing in her face but love.

“I love you, son.”

He had it. He had the whole of it, at last, the end of the sentence that had stopped in the dark so many mornings. He held it a moment in the hollow of his fist, the way you hold something that is going to be taken, and then he closed the fist around all of it.

“Jack,” he said. “Come forth.”

The Dracol was running too fast to see what the shadows had seen. In the lee of a dead engine at the edge of the plaza, something that was not quite a person had been standing very still for a very long time. It drew. It did not seem to aim.

A single black arrow, long and thin as a thought, took the Dracol in the eye.

The Dracol whimpered.

It was a tiny sound, a puppy’s sound, the first honest noise it had made all day. It lasted one second, and the charge faltered for one second.

Vael opened his hands.

Jack did not climb itself this time. It was simply there, white as a struck match, so bright that the shadows in the plaza ran from it. All four eyes opened, bottom to top, in the time it takes to blink. The smile came last, and it was wider.

It caught the charge with one hand.

The plaza boomed. The paving buckled under its heels, and it did not seem to notice. It regarded the Dracol from behind the smile, and the Dracol looked back out of its one good eye, and for the second time that day it tried to step back.

Then Jack turned its head and looked at Vael, four eyes and one smile, and waited. The first time it had come on its own, and done what it did. This time there was a hand on the other end of it.

Vael made the third sign.

Jack’s left arm ran like wet clay and came out as a tongue, a long rope of grey-pink flesh that whipped around the Dracol’s throat and pulled. The Dracol came off its feet. It hit the stones on one side and was hauled up by the neck and slammed down on the other.

The first sign.

The right arm went black to the shoulder. The fingers lengthened and curved, and the claws were made of the kind of dark that has no bottom. They went in where the plates were thinnest, along the one dark channel in its chest where a shadow arrow had gone before, and opened it from throat to ribs, and the plates peeled back like slate. The lattice blazed white. The Dracol’s regeneration surged, and it was too slow. Jack tore faster than it could heal. It did not hurry. It did not stop smiling.

The second sign.

Between the white shoulder blades the skin parted, and wings came out, wide and pale and made of light, more the idea of feathers than feathers, and when they snapped open the shock of it pushed Vael back a step. Jack crouched. It leapt.

The plaza fell away. Jack went up with the Dracol hanging from its tongue like a fish, its wings beating the air into sound. The Dracol’s good wing flailed. Its jaw cracked open and the light gathered in its throat, the beam, the white river, and the clawed hand came up out of its chest and closed around its muzzle and shut it.

The light had nowhere to go. It turned back down the Dracol’s neck and showed through its seams like a lamp through a cracked shade. The creature made a noise that Vael did not want to remember.

They went up through the thin ceiling of the cloud and out of the top of it, into clean blue air.

Vael stood in the plaza with his head thrown back and watched them shrink: a white spark, a dark smudge. He thought of Figo’s hand, heavy on his shoulder, saying brother. He thought of Trista, wet and furious and laughing, saying you idiot. He thought of James in a doorway with his arms spread wide, delighted with himself.

“I will end it here and now,” he said.

Far above the cloud, Jack drew back its arm and threw.

The Dracol fell for a very long time. It fell through the cloud and out of the bottom of it, a black speck that grew and grew, and Vael watched the whole way down and did not look away. It took him longer than it should have to see that the speck was not coming toward him.

The Dracol had spread its one good wing.

It was the oldest thing it knew. The long fall bent, in a slow curve across the sky, away from the avenue and the small figure on the stone, toward the throne, toward the circle, toward the one warm place in the world. A hurt thing goes toward warmth.

Pray do not fight it upon the iron. Vael heard it in an old elf’s dry, courteous voice, much too late.

“No,” he said. “No, no—”

It hit the heart of the iron circle head first.

The sound did not arrive for a moment. When it did, it came up through the soles of his feet. The plates caved like the lid of a tin. A crater opened, and the shock threw Vael flat. Dust went up to the sky and hung there and came down.

There was no whimper. There was no noise at all.

Jack came down out of the cloud after it, slowly, with its wings spread, and landed at the lip of the crater. It looked at the thing it had made. Then it folded its wings away into nothing and turned toward Vael, a long way off across the stone, and its four eyes closed, top to bottom, and it was gone.

Vael’s legs held. Jack’s first coming had cost him his legs. This time, with the second key in his fist, they held.

He crossed the plaza on foot. It was a long way, and nobody stopped him.

The Dracol lay at the bottom of the crater on the broken plates, with its neck at a wrong angle and its great head turned toward him. A black arrow stood from the ruin of one eye. The other was open, and the light in it went out slowly, like a window at evening.

He stood in front of its face. He didn’t know what he had expected to feel. It was still only a face.

Her face had stayed. He checked, the way you check a pocket for a key. The room was gone, and the sky of falling stars, but the face was still there, and the tune, a little flat in the third bar. He stood with it in front of the dead thing and did not know what to do with his hands.

Footsteps behind him. Unhurried.

“Well done, Vael.”

He did not turn. He knew the voice. It was soft and level and seemed to arrive from a long way off, as if each word had traveled to reach him.

“We never doubted you,” said Varesa. She came to stand a little way off, looking down into the crater at the corpse, a bow across her back. “The Vigil sends its thanks.”

Her eyes moved over the plaza, far across the black stone to the mouth of the avenue, where small things lay that had been people. They rested there for half a second. Then they moved on.

“The others have done their jobs well too, I see.”

Vael had none of it. He turned his back on her and walked.

“I didn’t do it for you.”

He walked past her, back across the long black stone, toward what was left of his friends, and she did not stop him.

“I know,” said Varesa.

Behind him, her edges came loose. Her coat, her hair, the line of her bow, they went long and thin like ink drawn off a page, and the shape slid across the broken plates toward the nearest shade.

“I’m sure we will meet again, Dracol Slayer,” said the shadow, and was gone.

Far below the crater, under the broken circle and the thing that had died on it, the hum stopped.

It had been in the ground since the Fourth Ring, a pressure behind the teeth, so constant that no one had noticed it as anything but the sound of the world. It stopped, and in the stillness it left, something opened.

There was no one on the Provost’s Stair. Atver was nowhere to be found.

In the dark, an eye flashed.

It is time, something said, in no voice at all. For the new era.
