Chapter 5: What We Carry

Part 1 — Zefra

Year 0. The Starfall.

The sky had been wrong for three hours before the screaming started.

Ragnik would remember it that way for the rest of his life — not the moment the first star came down, not the light that turned night into a color the world had no name for, but the screaming, because the screaming was the moment it stopped being something happening far away and became something happening here.

The tavern shook. Somewhere outside, a support beam that had held for two generations gave way with a sound like the earth clearing its throat, and a cloud of grey dust rolled past the windows where blue sky had been that morning.

Zefra was already at the door.

“Zefra, please—” Ragnik had her hand in his, and he was holding it the way you hold something you already suspect you’re about to lose. “We go to the bunker. It’s safer there. We wait it out, we—”

“Look outside.” She didn’t raise her voice. She never needed to. “Look at it, my love. Can’t you see.” Her eyes found his, and there was no fear in them — only the terrible clarity of someone who had already made her decision and was simply waiting for him to catch up to it. “This is the end of the world. If we let it be.”

She pulled her hand free.

“Anyone who can fight — with me.” Her voice carried now, filling the tavern’s main hall, cutting through the noise of falling plaster and frightened voices. “Ragnik. You stay. I’ll bring as many as I can find back to this door. Get them into the bunker. Keep it safe.”

He didn’t stop her the second time. He wanted to. His hand reached and found only air where hers had been.

Three men were already moving toward her — warriors he recognized, regulars, men who drank at his bar and complained about small things and had apparently decided, in the space of one terrible morning, that they were willing to die for strangers. A healer joined them, young, her satchel already open, checking supplies with hands that shook and didn’t stop moving anyway.

Outside, something enormous came down half a mile distant, and the sound of it arrived a full second after the light did, and the light was the color of the inside of a furnace.

Zefra walked out into it without looking back.

The bunker filled through that day the way a wound fills with blood — steadily, terribly, more than the space was built to hold. Children first, herded by whoever could spare the moment. Then the old, the injured, the ones who couldn’t run anymore and had stopped trying. Ragnik counted them as they came, and stopped counting when the number became a shape too large to hold, and simply kept the door open and kept them moving through it.

He caught glimpses of outside every time the door swung — brief, terrible frames of a world rearranging itself into something worse. A street he’d walked a thousand times, unrecognizable. A building that had stood since before his father’s father, gone. And once, just once, far down the road through a gap in the smoke, a figure in familiar robes standing between a fallen wall and a group of huddled survivors, one arm raised, something bright and defiant burning at her palm.

He didn’t get to watch longer than that. The door swung shut. More people needed to come through.

A day passed in the compressed, formless way that terrible days pass — not measured in hours but in the number of people who came through the door and the number who stopped coming.

The three men returned as the light outside was finally starting to fade from wrong to merely dim.

They were carrying someone.

Ragnik’s chest did something complicated and hopeful before he registered the details — the body slung between them was not Zefra’s. It was a young man, unfamiliar, and strangely, impossibly, he looked fine. No burns. No wounds. His face had the soft, untroubled quality of someone deeply asleep rather than someone who had just survived whatever these three men had clearly not survived unscathed.

They set him down carefully near the fire. Then they stood there, all three of them wounded, all three of them not looking at Ragnik.

The healer’s tears had already started.

“Zefra?” Ragnik heard his own voice from somewhere outside himself. “Where is she?”

Nobody answered right away. That was the answer.

“She sacrificed herself for us,” the healer finally managed, her voice breaking apart on the last word. “She—” She couldn’t finish it.

“I’m sorry,” one of the men said. The other two couldn’t lift their eyes from the floor.

Ragnik stood very still. He was holding a box of bread — he would remember that detail forever, the absurd, mundane weight of it in his arms, bread meant for people who were still alive to eat it. He looked down at it as though seeing it for the first time.

He set it down.

He took a breath that cost him more than any breath had ever cost him before.

“Rebecca.” His voice came out steadier than he expected, though it carried a weight underneath the steadiness that would never fully lift. “Help these lads. I need a moment.”

Rebecca — younger then, but already the person she would grow into — looked at him with an expression caught between concern and the understanding that some moments cannot be shared, only permitted. She nodded. “Come on,” she said to the warriors and the healer. “Let’s get you seen to.”

Ragnik walked to the door.

He sat on the porch steps in a world that no longer resembled the one he’d grown up in. The sky still held that wrong color at its edges, fading slowly toward something that might, eventually, become night again. The smell reached him in full now, unfiltered by walls — smoke, and stone dust, and underneath both of those, the specific, unmistakable smell of a world that had lost too much too fast to properly grieve any of it yet.

He sat there and looked at the horizon where he had last seen her.

Then he let out a sound that the world around him absorbed without comment, because the world around him had heard a great many sounds like it that day and had stopped being able to distinguish one grief from another.

It was not a short sound. It carried in it a marriage, and a promise made in a bunker doorway that he was only now understanding the true cost of, and a woman who had walked toward the end of the world because she couldn’t do anything else and still call herself who she was.

When it finally ended, Ragnik sat for a long time in the settling quiet.

Then he stood.

His face, when he turned back toward the door, had changed. The grief was still there — it would always be there, folded somewhere under everything else he would ever do — but something had been built on top of it. A decision. Simple, and total, and entirely without drama.

I will help them. All of them. However many there are, however long it takes.

This will not be the end. Not while I have a door to hold open.

He walked back inside.

Part 2 — The Graves

The current timeline. Present day.

The morning was cold and clean in the way mornings are after something terrible has finished happening. Frost lay thick on the ground behind the tavern, in the small cleared plot where five new mounds of dark earth sat in an uneven row, each one marked with a plain wooden stake.

Ragnik stood at the head of the row with a shovel in his hands, the blade still dark with the last of the turned soil. Three warriors stood behind him with their own shovels, breath fogging in the cold air. Vael. Figo. A third hunter who had ridden the long path and made it home.

“I couldn’t keep my promise,” Ragnik said, to the graves, to no one, to the memory of a bunker doorway and a hand pulling free of his. His voice held, barely, on the last word. A single tear traced down through the lines of his weathered face and he didn’t move to wipe it away.

Vael put a hand on his shoulder.

“I’m sorry,” Vael said. “My plan wasn’t good enough. There should have been a better way. I should have—”

He stopped. The weight of it sat behind his sternum like something physically lodged there — five people, five names he hadn’t learned in time to properly mourn, five families that existed now only in the past tense because of a route he had chosen on a hand-drawn map in a panicked morning. He looked at the graves and felt the particular, hollow devastation of someone measuring the exact size of a mistake against the exact size of the cost, and finding that the two numbers didn’t comfort each other at all.

Ragnik turned to look at him. Whatever was in his face wasn’t blame. It was something closer to the opposite of blame.

“Without your plan,” Ragnik said, “and without every fighter who stood on that ridge — we would be burying more than five.” He looked past Vael to Figo, whose scarred face carried its own version of the same grief. “You’ve all done well. Grief doesn’t mean you failed. It means the cost was real.”

He looked back at the graves. His eyes settled on the third one from the left.

“This one had a family,” he said quietly. “Wife died a month back — injuries from a Dagaruma raid, nothing that could be fixed. His son survived. Made it into the bunker. Raul, the boy’s called. He’s seven.” He was quiet a moment. “I told the father, before he rode out yesterday, that if anything happened to him, I’d take the boy in as my own.” He straightened. “I intend to keep that promise. At least that one.”

He turned and walked toward the tavern door.

Inside, the hall held the particular mixture of a place processing two truths at once — relief at having survived, and grief at the price of it. People moved quietly. Someone was crying softly near the hearth. Someone else was laughing, once, briefly, at something a friend had said, and then looking guilty about the laugh.

Ragnik pushed the door open and raised his voice — not loud, but carrying.

“Everyone.” The hall turned toward him. “I know we’ve just survived death. I know we’re grieving. But we have to prepare — we’re low on supplies, and we need to move before that becomes a second crisis on top of the first.”

Atver, who had been sitting near the hearth with his book closed on one finger to mark his place, rose and produced a folded map from inside his robes. He unfolded it across the nearest table — old, detailed, clearly drawn before the world had rearranged itself into its current shape.

“Your best option is here.” He tapped a point on the map.

“The volcano?” Figo said, eyebrows climbing.

“Yes.” Atver didn’t look up. “There will almost certainly be a village at its base. If there is, it survived the Starfall — that region has held stable since Year 0. Volcanic regions are resource-rich besides. You’ll be welcomed.”

James, seated nearby with his boots up on a second chair and an expression of profound unconcern, glanced at Ragnik and gave a single confirming nod. “Your best bet,” he agreed.

In the corner, Trista moved between three injured hunters, her hands glowing faint and steady, her focus entirely on the work in front of her.

The packing began in earnest — the particular controlled chaos of an entire community preparing to move at once. Boxes stacked by the door. Blankets rolled and tied. Rebecca moving through it all with a list in her head and an answer for every question thrown at her.

Vael found Atver at the edge of it, still bent over his map.

“You said you know where it went,” Vael said. “The Dracol.”

“A lead,” Atver corrected, without looking up. “Here.” His finger moved to a wide stretch of territory east of the tavern, marked in old ink with a name that had clearly once meant something grander. “The Molten Imperium. Whatever remains of it will be Machine Wastes now, after the Starfall did what it did.” He finally looked up, and something in his expression was carefully, deliberately neutral. “That civilization was engaged in work it should not have been engaged in. Its absence is not, on the whole, a loss to mourn.”

“You’re not telling me everything,” Vael said.

“No,” Atver agreed pleasantly. “I am telling you enough to get us moving in the correct direction.” He rolled the map with brisk, precise movements. “I’ll join your party. There’s more to uncover about this variant — why it’s raging, why now, why this one — and I intend to uncover it. The Wastes are roughly a month’s ride from here.”

“We can’t spare the horses,” Ragnik said, passing with an armful of boxes, not slowing. “Every animal we have goes to the caravan. The people need them more than the road does.”

Atver considered this without visible distress. “Two months, then. On foot.”

Vael looked around at his team. James, still reclined, radiating the specific unbothered calm of a man who had already seen this conversation happen and approved of the outcome. Figo, fully recovered now, Sheela steady on his back, ready in the particular way of a man who processes grief by preparing to act on it. Trista, still working, her hands never quite stopping even as she listened.

“We go to the Molten Imperium,” Vael said. His hand closed into a fist. “We end this. Whatever it costs.”

Atver nodded once, satisfied.

“Then we help however we can today,” Vael continued. “And leave at first light tomorrow.”

The rest of the day dissolved into work.

Figo carried what Figo could carry, which was considerably more than most, hauling crates and furniture toward the waiting carts with Sheela strapped across his back the entire time, refusing every offer of help with the specific stubbornness of a man who needed his hands full of something. Trista moved between the injured and the packing in equal measure, splitting her attention and her strength across both without complaint, though the exhaustion showed in the set of her shoulders by midafternoon. James, surprisingly, turned out to be genuinely useful at logistics — directing traffic, sorting priority from clutter, occasionally producing a piece of information about probable weather or road conditions that he had no obvious way of knowing and declining to explain how he knew it.

Vael threw himself into the work wherever the line was thinnest: lashing crates onto the carts, hauling water, taking the weight of loads that were too heavy for the people carrying them. It was easier than thinking. Somewhere in the middle of it, he noticed Raul, the boy Ragnik had named at the graves, sitting apart from the others near the wagon wheels, small and quiet, watching everyone else move with a stillness that didn’t belong on a child his age.

Vael sat down next to him. He didn’t say anything at first. Neither did Raul.

“Your dad,” Vael finally said, carefully, “did something that mattered. All of them did.” He looked at the wagons, the packed boxes, the people preparing to survive another day because of choices made on a battlefield. “It doesn’t make it hurt less. I know that.”

Raul didn’t answer. But he leaned, very slightly, against Vael’s side, and Vael let him.

Evening came, and with it, the last of the light Ragnik would see from this porch as its owner.

He walked the tavern’s ground floor alone after most had gone to sleep, running his hand along the bar he’d built with his own two arms — one of them, now, the only one he had — and the shelves, and the door frame that had held a thousand comings and goings since the day the world ended and he’d decided to keep it open anyway.

He stopped at the window that faced the horizon Zefra had walked toward, that terrible morning not so long ago.

“We made it,” he said quietly, to no one, to the glass, to the memory behind it. “Longer than I thought we would. I kept the door open, Zefra. Like I promised.” He was quiet for a moment. “I’m taking the boy. Raul. I told his father I would, and I’m keeping that one too.” He touched the window frame once, lightly. “I don’t know where this next part takes any of us. But I think you’d have liked them. The ones I’m sending out to finish what you couldn’t.”

He stood there a while longer.

Then he went to bed, because morning was coming whether he was ready for it or not.

Part 3 — First Light

The yard outside the tavern was full of the particular energy of a departure that has been dreaded and prepared for in equal measure.

Two directions. Ragnik’s caravan, loaded and ready, would head south toward safer, more populated ground — the people, the children, Raul small and solemn at Rebecca’s side, already looking like he belonged there. And east, on foot, with packs and weapons and whatever they could carry, the five who were going the other way.

Ragnik found Vael last, when most of the goodbyes had already been said and the yard had thinned to its final moments.

Vael took his hand. He held it, and found, as he suspected he would, that he had absolutely no idea what to say. Every sentence that arrived in his head seemed too small for what it was trying to hold.

“Thank you, Ragnik,” he settled on, finally. “For everything.”

Ragnik’s grip was firm despite the missing arm, despite the years, despite everything the morning was asking of him.

“It’s not me you should thank.” He looked at Vael’s face for a long moment — really looked, the way he had that first morning in room ten. “My wife gave her life so yours could continue. Whatever that was worth to her, it’s yours to spend now. So spend it.” His mouth twitched, almost a smile. “Go and beat that oversized orange rock.”

Somewhere behind them, James made a sound that was unmistakably a laugh, quickly and unsuccessfully disguised as a cough.

Ragnik released Vael’s hand. He looked at the rest of them — Figo, Trista, James, Atver — and gave a single nod that held in it everything a man like Ragnik allowed himself to say out loud.

Then the caravan moved south, and the five of them turned east, and the tavern that had been the first solid ground under Vael’s feet since he woke up with no memory at all grew smaller behind them, and then smaller still, until it was only a shape on the horizon, and then it wasn’t anything at all.

The road ahead ran east, toward the place where sky broke into floating stone, and beyond that, toward wastes that had once been an empire, and somewhere past all of it, an enraged and wounded thing that was, even now, healing in the dark and waiting to be found.

The journey had begun.
