Chapter 8: What the Stars Left

Part 1 — The Sixth Star

Twenty-three years before the Starfall.

The Framedious lay on its back in the trampled clover, all five limbs curled in toward its belly like a dead spider’s. In the slanting light of late afternoon it was even uglier than it had been alive.

“That mouth works two jobs,” Jade said, prodding it with her boot, “and I refuse to find out which one it’s better at.”

“Don’t,” said Howard, not looking up. He was scraping dark blood out of the serpent carved into his great door of a shield, one groove at a time. “And boot off it. Nothing’s safe until the plates go cold.”

Jade took her boot off, grinning. Her poison did that to large things: a slow, helpless shudder from the inside, like a bell that had been struck and could not stop ringing. It was how the party won most of its fights. Howard held the line, Jade made the monster shake, and the rest was arithmetic.

On a stump at the edge of the field, Samuel sat with his serpent-headed staff across his knees, polishing its scales with a rag. He had said nothing since the fight ended. He seldom did.

“It was afraid,” he said, so quietly that Jade almost missed it. “At the end.”

Everyone looked at him.

“It was a Framedious, Samuel,” said Jade.

“I know.” He went on polishing. “I only said it was afraid.”

Howard shared a long look with Jade over the top of his shield. Samuel had been odd for the whole two years they had known him, and so far the oddness had always turned out to be useful. Nobody had worked out how to ask about it.

Saela stood a little apart from them, with her back straight and her hands loosely folded. All three of her summons were still out. The shadow wolf stood at her heel, a shape of darkness with only its eyes left. The spirit bird turned slow circles overhead. The toad squatted in the clover like a boulder that had decided to breathe. She lifted one hand and made the sign, two knuckles up and the rest folded in. The wolf came apart at her feet like smoke let out of a jar. The spirit bird blinked out of existence between one beat of its wings and the next. The toad, last and largest, simply melted into the ground like wax into sand.

“How are you still clean?” Jade demanded.

Saela looked down at her robes. They were white, as they always were. There was no blood on them, no mud, and not a single splash of whatever the Framedious had been doing with its stomach. It was a small, specific miracle that had been annoying the party for two years.

“I planned where to stand,” she said.

“I hate you a little.”

“I know.” She smoothed a sleeve. “I’m going down to the river.”

“I’ll come with you,” said Jade at once, already reaching for her bow. “Someone should keep watch—”

“Jade.”

“—and carry your towel—”

“I’d like to be alone,” Saela said gently. “I prefer my private time.”

“Nobody prefers private time. It’s a lie people tell to get out of carrying towels.” But Jade let her bow drop and waved her off. “Fine. Go and be clean at the water. Scream if anything eats you.”

“If anything eats me,” said Saela, “I will.”

The path to the river ran downhill through the whole of the afternoon.

Saela took it slowly, with her hands loose at her sides, the way she walked when a plan was finished and she was finally allowed to stop holding it. The land was in full summer. The meadow was thick with clover and yellow vetch, and the bees were so heavy with it that the air hummed in a low, steady chord. Grasshoppers sprang ahead of her boots in bright, stupid arcs. A heron lifted out of a drainage ditch with all the dignity of a man leaving a party. On the far slope a boy was herding a flock of goats, and he waved to her with his whole arm, and she waved back. A hawk wheeled over the hill. Down on the old road, a salt caravan crawled along with its bells ringing in the heat.

Far to the north-east, so distant it was only a pale splinter at the edge of the sky, the Spire of the Concord caught the sun. It had stood there for longer than anyone could remember. She had never thought of it as a thing that could stop.

She thought about the Framedious instead.

It was the fourth beast this season. A marsh-stalker that had walked out of its marsh. A pack of ridge wolves that had marched straight into Howard’s shield and kept pushing. A stag-bear, twice as thin as it should have been, charging a village wall. And now the Framedious, a creature that by every account she had ever read hunted alone, at night, and never left the deep trees, in an open pasture at midday. It had not been hunting. She had watched it carefully. It had been running, and when it could not run any farther, it had fought like a thing that was cornered and not a thing defending a kill.

It was afraid, Samuel had said.

A hungry animal hunts. These had not been hunting. They had been driven.

That morning, while the others were still eating, she had drawn the four kill sites on a page of her notebook. The dots made an arc. A shallow one, curving away to the north-east, as though something had pressed on a table and everything on it had started to slide.

She had shown no one. Arcs were not the sort of thing a tactician brought to a party that had just killed something and wanted to eat. She told herself the arc was an accident of where the villages were.

By the time she reached the river, the gold had gone thin on the water and the first star was out.

The river was cold enough to take her breath.

She let it. She stood in it to the shoulders, her white robes folded on a flat rock behind her, her hair floating loose around her, and let the day drain out of her arms. Holding all three at once for the length of a fight was a great deal. She felt each one as a faint ache along the inside of her wrists, three tethers, three small weights. The water took the ache a little at a time.

She started to hum. It was a lullaby from nowhere, half remembered, and she was a little flat in the third bar. By the time she floated on her back and looked up, the last of the dusk had drained out of the sky and the stars had come out in their thousands.

She found the Hunter’s Crook the way every hunter in the world did, the curve of six stars that pointed to north. She had counted them every night of her life.

Tonight she counted five.

She counted again. Five. The sixth star was gone, and that was not the strange part. The strange part was that she could feel where it had been, the way the tongue finds the hole where a tooth was. The sky around the gap sat slightly wrong, like a held breath.

I’m tired, she told herself. Three summons. I’m seeing things.

Something crackled on the bank, dry and small. A twig.

“Jade,” said Saela, without lifting her head. “I told you not to follow me.”

She turned in the water, and her heart stopped.

It was not Jade. A man was standing at the edge of the reeds, perhaps twenty paces away, with the moon behind him. He was tall and lean, in a long gray traveling coat gone pale at the seams. On one shoulder a patch of cloth was lighter than the rest, the shape of an emblem that had been picked off thread by thread. His dark hair was silvering at the temples, and his face was weathered rather than aged, lined at the eyes from a lifetime of squinting at distances. A fine brush was tucked behind his ear like a scribe’s pen, a leather tube of charts hung across his back, and his fingers were stained black with ink to the second knuckle.

Saela screamed. It was a good scream, high and sharp, and a pair of night birds went up out of the reeds at it.

The man looked at her for exactly as long as it took to understand what he was looking at. Then, without any hurry at all, he turned his back, like someone quietly closing a door.

It was the lack of a reaction that did it. A man who gapes can be dealt with, and so can a man who leers. A man who simply turns around and waits was, to Saela, an unreasonably interesting problem.

She stood in the cold water for a long, slow breath, and then she began to get dressed.

“You can turn around.”

He did. She stood at the edge of the shallows with her hair dripping into the white collar of her robes, her arms folded, and her chin up. He bowed his head very slightly, as one acknowledges a stranger on a road.

“I did announce myself,” he said. “Twice. You were singing.”

“I wasn’t singing.”

“A lullaby, I think. A little flat in the third bar.” There was no teasing in it. He said it as if reading a figure off a page. “I am sorry to have startled you. I was not watching you. I was watching the river.”

“The river.”

“On a windless night it is the only honest mirror in the Reach. A lens bends what it sees, and water only holds it.” He lifted a flat slate from the crook of his arm, covered in small bright points joined by fine pale lines. “I am mapping the Reach, one clear night at a time.”

Saela stepped closer before she knew she was going to. She was a planner and a mapmaker by habit, and she knew a good chart when she saw one. Drawn on the slate, small and exact, was the curve of the Hunter’s Crook.

There were six stars on it.

“That’s wrong,” she said.

“Is it?”

“There are only five tonight.”

The change in him was slight. He did not move, but something in his stillness arrived, as if a man who had been walking carelessly through a dark house had just heard a floorboard that was not his. It was the first time he had truly looked at her. She had been looked at a great deal by men in taverns, and she had never been looked at like that, as though she had said something important. She felt it all the way down, and she mistook what it meant.

“You can see that,” he said.

“I can’t see it. I can—” She faltered. “I know where it was. Is that—should I tell someone?”

“Whom would you tell?” he said, and for the first time his voice was quiet. “How long?”

“Tonight. I think tonight. I counted them yesterday.” She frowned at him. “Why does it matter to you?”

“It matters to anyone who counts stars.” He lowered the slate, and for a moment he seemed not to know what to do with his hands. Then he did. “Those are a mage’s robes, on the rock. And not a speck on them, for someone who sleeps in the field.”

“I plan where to stand,” Saela said.

“It shows.” He said it simply, the way you state the weight of a stone. “Someone who keeps her robes that clean notices small things. That is rarer than power.”

It was the first time anyone had admired her tidiness instead of resenting it, and she was embarrassed at how much she liked it.

She noticed afterward that he had asked how long the star had been gone before he had asked her name. She did not notice that for a long time.

“I’m Saela,” she said.

“A good name.” He inclined his head. “I have gone without my own for a long time. Call me someone who counts stars.”

“That is not a name.”

“Then I have no name worth carrying tonight.” The corner of his mouth moved, which on his face was nearly a smile. “The river will be here tomorrow, Saela. So will the sky. If you wish to know what you are seeing in it—”

“My party moves on at dawn.”

“Then I will be quick,” he said.

“Good shower?” said Jade, when she came back into the lantern light.

“Cold.”

“You’re smiling.”

“I’m not.”

Jade’s eyes narrowed. Her gaze went to the wet hair, the damp collar, and the odd, steady look on Saela’s face, and she opened her mouth. Then Howard grunted something about first watch and Samuel said, quietly, “Let her be,” and the moment passed.

Saela lay down at the edge of the circle with her notebook open on her chest. Her charts were all tactical: sight lines, retreat routes, the wind. She turned to a clean page and drew a curve of six small dots. After a moment she crossed one out.

Tomorrow, she told herself, I will plan to stay in camp.

She planned a great many things, and she followed every plan to the end.

That one lasted until the moon came up.

Part 2 — What the Ash Kept

The road did not bend.

In the whole of that first day it did not bend once, and Vael began to understand why the plain had looked, from the clouds, like a page ruled for accounts. Everything here had been ruled. The road, the ditches beside it, the pylons that had once carried the pipes, all ran straight, in a file that narrowed to a point at the edge of the world. Milestones stood at exact intervals, each a block of black stone carved with a mountain and a flame.

And every mile or so, an engine stood where it had stopped.

They never got used to them. A hauler with its arms half-raised, as though about to wave. A long-legged thing built like a heron out of girders, one foot lifted over the ash, so exactly mid-step that Vael kept waiting for it to finish. Ash lay on their shoulders. Not one of them had fallen over. They had simply stopped, all at once, and gone on standing there in the shape of the last thing they had been told to do.

Beside the road, terraces stepped down the slope: long iron troughs under cracked glass, each filled to the brim with grey ash. Vael crouched at the edge of one. There were pipes under the glass, thin as a finger, with little brass valves every yard, and a row of dead lamps strung on wires above.

“Grain,” said Atver, behind him. “Or the place where grain was persuaded to grow.”

“Persuaded?”

“Nothing here ever grew in the ordinary way, child. That is not soil.” Atver turned a pinch of ash between his thumb and finger and let it fall. “Under the ash is slag, poured and spread. The heat came up those pipes from the cones, and the seed was sown, tended and cut by machines. They grew a harvest the way one casts a bell.” He brushed his fingers clean. “It fed millions. And in all that time, not one plant on this plain was ever asked to put down a root of its own.”

Vael looked at the troughs, and the dead lamps, and understood something he had not understood from the cloud. The land had not died. It had never been alive. It had been held up, like the islands in the sky, by something that was no longer there.

“So when the machines stopped,” Trista began.

“Every farm in the Imperium stopped on the same morning,” said Atver. “And then the cold came for whatever was left.”

They camped at dusk in the lee of a milestone, because Atver would not let them near the engines. The cold came down fast, and they pulled their blankets around their shoulders and made a small, dark, fireless circle with nothing in the middle of it.

Trista laid out the last of the caravan’s stores on five squares of cloth with the solemn exactness of a priestess at an altar: a round of flatbread, a handful of dried apricots, a thumb of hard cheese, a strip of salted meat. By Vael’s reckoning it was four days of food, if everyone was reasonable, which left out Figo.

Figo looked at his portion. Then he looked at Trista. Then he looked at his portion again.

“Is that—”

“That’s all of it,” she said. “Eat slowly.”

He ate it in two bites. For a moment he sat with a stunned, betrayed look on his face, chewing the memory of it. Then he bent his head over the axe across his knees. “Sheela,” he whispered, “your husband is sleeping hungry tonight.”

James was lying on his back with his boots toward the fire that wasn’t there, his cask balanced on his chest. He had not touched his own portion. After a minute he sat up, broke his flatbread in half without a word, and held out the bigger piece.

Figo stared at it. “That’s yours.”

“I’m being generous. Take it before I notice.”

“You need it. For the thing. With your eyes.”

“My eyes don’t run on bread, big man.” James put the piece in his hand and folded the great fingers closed around it. “They run on spite. I’ve got plenty.”

Across the blankets, Trista watched with an expression she was trying very hard to make disapproving. “You’ll faint on the road.”

“Then Figo will carry me.”

“I will,” said Figo, with his mouth full. “I’ll carry you.”

“There,” said James to Trista. “Logistics.”

“If you faint, I’m leaving you for the Dracol.” But when she thought nobody was looking, she pushed her last apricot across the cloth to him, and James, for once in his life, said nothing at all and ate it.

Later, when the others had begun to doze, Vael, who had the first watch, noticed that Figo was sitting up with Sheela across his knees, looking east. Far out in the dark, at the heart of the ring of mountains, the thread of smoke had gone red at its root. It looked like a very small candle in a very large room.

“Do you think she got home?” Figo said, very quietly, to nobody in particular.

Trista had not been asleep either. “Little?”

“She’s small. The road’s long.”

“She walked out from under a Varkador’s reign and led five strangers through her own front door,” said Trista. “She’s the cleverest person I’ve met since I was a girl. By now she’s in her own bed, telling the whole warren how she won.” She reached across the dark and found his hand where it rested beside the little braided knot under Sheela’s blade, and she did not say anything else.

“I know,” said Figo, after a while. “I’d just like it if she were here.”

“I know,” said Trista.

Vael turned the Ring of Eons on his finger and counted the days of food again. It was an easy sum. He did not like the answer any better the second time.

The second day, the mountains came up to meet them.

By midmorning the mountains were no longer a necklace on the horizon. They were two vast cones, one on either hand, like the posts of a gate built for giants, each wearing a collar of red iron and trailing its tangle of pipes into the plain. Between them ran a ridge of fused slag, and through it the road went in a single deep cut, its walls so clean they might have been sliced with a wire, twice Figo’s height. It was the only way into the bowl for twenty miles.

James slowed as they came to it.

Vael knew the signs by now. James went quiet in a particular way, and his eyes went somewhere else, and his hand drifted up to the cask at his side and stopped halfway there. It was never a good sign.

“What is it?”

“I don’t know yet.” James rubbed his eyes. “Everything looks like it ends in the same place.”

“Which place?”

“This one.”

Atver had stopped, one hand raised. The lens of his monocle had shifted from clear to a pale, careful amber. “There is a hauler across the far end of the cutting.”

“There’s a hauler across every end of every cutting,” said Figo. “They’re all over.”

“This one is not at a milestone.” Atver was looking at the ash in front of the machine. “And it has drag-marks behind it. A wagon’s width of them, and not a day old.”

“Back,” said Vael. “Everyone back, slowly—”

Behind them, with a long, dry, grinding shudder, a second hauler rolled out of the shadow of the slag and across the mouth of the cutting, pulled on rollers of salvaged pipe by a dozen people straining on ropes. The ash it had been hiding behind rose up in a curtain, and when the curtain fell, the walls above them were no longer empty.

They had been wearing ash. That was the first thing Vael understood. Grey cloaks, grey scarves, grey paint on their faces, lying flat along the lip of the slag as still as the engines. Now they rose all along both walls at once, forty or so of them, ragged and gaunt, and every one of them held a lance.

They were nothing like any spear he had ever seen. They were pipes of dark iron as thick as a man’s arm, scorched and patched, with a fist of white fire socketed into the side of each one, burning so brightly that it hurt to look at. On the cutting’s floor, in front of the far hauler, a man stood up on the machine’s shoulder with the sun behind him. He was big and heavy through the chest and stooped through the back, and he wore the remains of a foreman’s leather coat. He aimed his lance at the road between Figo’s boots, and fired.

The sound was a whip-crack the size of a hillside. A bolt of white-orange fire struck the black paving, and the stone did not break. It ran, slumping and glowing like wax, and left a pit the size of a cartwheel.

Figo’s boots had been a hand’s breadth from it. He did not move. Neither did anyone else.

“Hands,” said the man on the hauler. His voice was a gravel road, and tired to the bone. “Where I can see them. Then the packs on the ground. Nobody has to bleed.”

Vael’s hands were already moving toward the sign. The wolf was in his fingers, a cold weight against his palm, and the Ring of Eons was warm.

“Don’t,” said Atver, very quietly, beside him.

“I could—”

“You could. And forty-one lances would answer. Twelve of them are aimed at the people you care about, and every one of those will fire in the time it takes you to finish the sign.” The old elf did not turn his head. He was looking at the white cores, and the amber in his lens had deepened to the color of old honey. “Imperium siege-cells, torn out of dead engines. Every one of them nearly spent. Two shots each. Perhaps three.” A breath. “It is enough, child.”

“Vael.” That was James, from the corner of his mouth. He was not looking at the lances. He was looking at Trista, and he had gone the color of old paper. He did not say what he saw. He leaned a fraction toward Figo instead, light as a man remarking on the weather.

“Figo. Whatever you do, do it slowly. You get so dramatic when you’re hungry.”

“I’m not moving,” Figo said through his teeth. “I’m a rock.”

“Good rock,” said James, and his voice was not quite steady. “Stay a rock.”

Vael looked at the lance that was aimed at Trista. It was held by a young woman with a face like a hatchet, who had been holding it for so long that her arms were shaking. She looked starved and scared.

He took his hands out of the sign and opened them, slowly, in front of him.

“We’re not your enemies,” he said, to the man on the hauler. “We’re not here to take anything from you. We came to deal with the beast at the Seat. That’s all.”

“Everyone’s here for something,” said the man. “Packs.”

“Wellhouse?” called a voice from the left-hand wall.

“They’re not Wellhouse.” That was the hatchet-faced woman. She lifted her chin from behind the lance and sniffed, like a dog at a closed door, and a strange expression came over her. “Look at them. They’ve got stitching in their boots. They’ve got color in their faces.” She sniffed again. “She smells like flowers.”

“There are no flowers.”

“I know there aren’t, Pell. That’s what I’m saying.”

A silence went along both walls.

“Over the water,” said a boy’s voice, hardly above a whisper, and the words ran along the ridge from mouth to mouth like a spark along a fuse. They came over the water. Foreigners. They’ve come over the water. There was hunger in it, and something worse than hunger. It sounded very much like hope.

“Where’s your boat?” said the man on the hauler.

“We don’t have one,” said Vael.

“Don’t lie to me, boy. I’m too hungry for it.”

“We came down from the sky islands.” He heard how it sounded as it left his mouth. “From the floating stone, to the north. There was a column of light. We didn’t cross any sea.”

Somebody laughed. It was not a kind sound. Then it stopped, because the man on the hauler had not laughed, and he was looking at Vael’s face for a long, long time. Vael did not know what he saw there. Whatever it was, it did something to the man’s shoulders.

“There’s no boat,” he said, to the ridge, and nobody answered him.

One of the lances along the wall lowered a fraction of an inch. Somebody began to cry, without any noise, with a hand over his mouth. The hope went out of the cutting like water out of a cracked jar.

“Then what use are you,” said the man, in a flat, level voice. “Packs. Now.”

Figo made a sound deep in his chest, low and dangerous, and James’s hand shot out and closed on his wrist.

“Not first,” James breathed.

Atver set his pack on the ground with great deliberation, like a man putting down a tray. “Hands, children,” he said pleasantly. “Not today.”

They set their packs on the road.

They took everything that could be eaten, drunk, burned or sold.

It was done quickly, and without cruelty, which was the worst part. A dozen of them came down the wall on ropes and went through the packs with the swift, practiced hands of people who had done it many times before. The waterskins went into one sack. The flatbread and the cheese and the dried fruit went into another, and when the lid of the cloth was opened, a hush fell over the cutting, because there was more food in that one sack than most of them had seen in a week. The man on the hauler took a heel of the bread and held it to his face and closed his eyes. He did not eat it. He just breathed it.

They took the blankets, and the coin, and the little flint-and-steel, and the oil. A boy of about fifteen with a face too old for him reached for the braided knot of grass under Sheela’s blade, and Figo said “No” in a voice so low and level that the boy snatched his hand back as if from a hot stove.

“Leave the axe, Pell,” said the man.

“But it’s—”

“Leave the axe. We take what can be eaten, drunk, burned or sold. We don’t take blades.” He did not look at Figo, but his voice was for Figo. “Blades don’t fill a belly. And a man who’s lost his blade has nothing left to lose, and I’ve got ninety-one people to look after.”

Another one lifted the cask from James’s belt, pulled the stopper, and sniffed. Her whole face folded in on itself.

“It smells like a tavern floor,” she said.

“It’s medicinal,” said James, faintly.

She shoved the stopper back in and tossed it to him, and he caught it against his chest like a baby.

“Keep your poison.”

Then they put the five of them into a cage.

It had been a tithe-wagon once, an iron box on four stopped wheels at the side of the road, with a door that still locked, and it smelled of rust and old fear. They sat inside it in a row with their knees drawn up, and the sun came through the bars in long, hot stripes. Outside, at the foot of the hauler, the men and women of the cutting sat in a ring around the sack and began to argue.

It was not like any argument Vael had ever heard. It was quiet. It was exact. They counted the food in portions and the portions in days, and every time a voice rose, it was hushed again at once, as though noise cost something.

“We take them to the Foundry. The council can ask them about the water.”

“What about the water? They know nothing about the water.”

“They might know someone, over there. They might know where the sea’s gone clean.” That was the hatchet-faced woman, the one who had smelled the flowers.

“They came out of the sky, Marn.”

“Then we feed them till they say something useful.”

“With what?” That was the man from the hauler, the gravel voice. He had not raised it. “We’ve nine days of grain. Ninety-one mouths. You’d put five more on it?”

“We could trade them to Wellhouse,” said a third voice. “A day of water for five of them.”

“We’re at war with Wellhouse.”

“We’re at war because of the water, Dorrek, we could just—”

“No.”

The word fell like a stone. Nobody said anything after it.

“They didn’t hurt anyone,” said the boy called Pell, at last, very low.

“That’s why they’re not dead,” said Dorrek.

Trista had gone to the bars. She stood with her hands wrapped around them, and her voice, when she spoke, was the voice she used at the bedside of people who were afraid. “Please. You don’t have to feed us. Let us walk on. Every morning you keep us here is a morning you don’t have.”

“No more talking,” said Dorrek.

“Sir,” said Vael. He rose with his hands open. “There’s a beast at the Seat. We came here to kill it. If we do—”

Dorrek turned his head.

For the first time, Vael saw his face properly. It was a broad, tired, deeply lined face, burned dark by the sun and hollowed under the cheekbones, with the look in the eyes of a man who has not slept in a long time because there was something to count. He might have been forty. He might have been twenty years older.

“The beast,” said Dorrek. “Kill it, and then what?”

Vael opened his mouth.

“Will the grain grow? Will it rain? Will the wells fill?” He said it without heat. “The beast isn’t what’s killing us, boy. The silence is. It roars twice a night, and I sleep better for it. It’s the only thing out here still making a sound.” He turned to the others. “Open it. They walk. Don’t come back by the Gatecut. If I see your faces here again, I won’t have spent this much thought on you.”

“They’ll starve,” said Marn.

“Yes,” said Dorrek. “So will we.”

They walked out of the cage with their hands empty and their blades still on their backs, and the ring of grey faces turned to watch them go. Not a single lance followed them. Nobody threw anything. The rollers were hauled aside, and the far hauler was dragged to one side with a long, grinding shriek, and the road was open again, running on east between the black walls into the heart of the Molten Imperium.

Vael looked back once. Dorrek was sitting on the shoulder of the hauler with a heel of bread in his hands. He had not eaten it. He was looking at nothing at all.

The gate let them in, and then it showed them what it had been guarding.

Beyond the Gatecut the land fell away in a long slope, and the whole bowl lay open below them in the afternoon light, a plain so wide its far side was only a rumor of mountains. Avenues ran across it, dead straight, like the spokes of a wheel, and along every avenue, rank upon rank, stood the engines: haulers, heron-walkers, brush-handed sweepers, thousands of them, still as a crowd waiting for a speech that would never be given. Where the avenues met, a dark crown of towers hung on the horizon, and from the middle of it the thread of smoke climbed into the white sky.

“Well,” said James, to nobody. “That’s the biggest funeral I’ve ever been to.”

Nobody laughed. It was the last joke of the day.

Thirst came first, the way it does. By evening, Vael’s tongue had begun to feel like something borrowed from a larger animal. They made a dry camp in the shadow of a walker’s leg, with no blankets and no fire, and huddled in a heap for warmth in the dark. Nobody slept well. Figo’s stomach spoke, loudly and at length, all night long, like a small and offended thunderstorm. Around midnight James took out the cask and held it for a while in both hands without opening it.

“You’ll not,” said Trista, in the dark.

“I’m not.”

“It’ll dry you out.”

“I know.” He turned it over once. “I’m just holding it.”

She did not argue with that.

The second morning was worse. The sun came up white and the ash gave back the heat in waves, and by the time it stood over their heads, the five of them were walking in a loose, silent line with their eyes on their own feet. Trista’s lips had cracked. Figo was carrying Sheela at his side instead of across his back, and every few steps his hand tightened and loosened on her haft, as though checking that she was still there.

Vael called the wolf at noon, and it went ahead of them, tireless, for a mile and then another, and came back with nothing. He sent the Spirit Bird next, up and up until it was only a glint against the white, and it came back with the same flat nothing.

There was nothing alive in the bowl. Not a bird. Not a beetle. Not a weed in a crack. The only moving thing in it was the five of them, going slowly down a straight road toward a sound.

“Two days without water is survivable,” said Atver, to no one in particular. His own lips were pale and dry, and his steps had gone short and precise, but he had not complained, and he did not seem to notice that he had not. “Three is a funeral.”

“Thank you for that,” said James, hoarsely.

“I find it helps to know the number.”

Trista tried what she had left. She stopped in the road and laid her palm on Figo’s chest and let the light run through him, soft and white, and he sighed as if a weight had come off. But she took her hand away with a small, shaken look. “It isn’t water,” she said. “It doesn’t make anything. It only moves what’s already there around, and there’s nothing left to move.” She tried to smile. “I’m sorry. I thought I could fix this one.”

“You fixed it a bit,” said Figo, roughly. “I can hear again.”

By late afternoon, Vael had begun to think about Dorrek. He had not meant to. He thought about the sack of bread, and the man who had held it to his face and not eaten it, and the hatchet-faced woman who had shaken so badly she could not keep her lance steady, and he could not make himself hate any of them. He had tried. He wanted to, with part of him, because it would have been easier. They’d have done it to anyone, he thought. I’d have done it myself, if it were Rebecca and the children. It was a terrible thing to know about yourself, and an even worse thing to know about the world: that a good man and a ruined one could stand on the same road with the same face.

It was the wolf that saw her first.

Part 3 — What the Ash Gave

The wolf had been walking at his heel, head low, since the afternoon. Now it stopped so abruptly that its shoulder bumped his leg. The hackles rose along its back in a long dark ridge, and it turned its head, not toward the road, but to the left, to a shape that Vael had taken for another stopped engine at the edge of the ash.

There was a person standing on its shoulder.

“Get behind me,” Vael rasped, and the hand sign rose in his fingers, and for a moment the whole bowl seemed to listen.

“If I wished you any harm,” said a clear, dry, carefully modulated voice, “I would hardly have shown myself.” The person on the engine lowered a lamp, a hooded thing with a pale blue core no larger than a thumb, and climbed down the walker’s knee-joint in the neat, economical manner of someone who had done it a thousand times. “I’m not Foundry and I’m not Wellhouse. I have water. Please put the dog away.”

“It’s a wolf,” said Vael. His voice cracked.

“Is it? How remarkable.” The speaker reached the ground, and Vael saw that she was a young woman about his own age, hardly more than a girl, thin as a rail and grey to the elbows with ash. Her hair had been cut short with a blade, badly and recently. She wore a patchwork of good cloth sewn to bad purposes: a velvet cuff under a hide vest, a scholar’s collar of fine linen turned inside out to hide the stitching, boots resoled with strips of hose. But her hands were scrubbed pink, and the nails, though broken, were clean, which in that country was either vanity or discipline. “Sit down. All of you. No, sit, standing makes it worse.”

She had already unslung a waterskin from her shoulder and was holding it out. It was Trista, with her cracked lips and her unsteady hands, who took it first, and she did not drink. She passed it to Figo, who held it as if it were made of glass.

“Three sips,” said the girl. “Then wait a count of sixty. Then three more. I’ve watched people gulp their first water in this place, and it does not stay down. It’s a waste, and it’s undignified.”

Figo took three sips. His eyes filled. He counted sixty, moving his lips, and then he took three more, and passed it on, and when it came back round to the girl, it was only a little lighter.

She reached into her pack and took out a brick.

It was the size of a man’s hand and the color of old putty, stamped on one face with the mountain and the flame. She snapped it into five pieces with a precise little crack and handed them round. “Please don’t thank me until you’ve tasted it,” she said. “It is not food in any sense a cook would recognize.”

It tasted of chalk, and of something faintly sweet underneath, and of the dust of a sealed room. It was the finest thing Vael had ever put in his mouth. He ate it very slowly, with his eyes closed, and when he opened them she was watching him with an odd, unreadable expression, as if she were recalling something she had not meant to.

“So,” said the girl, when the last crumb was gone. She had not touched a brick herself. “Who robbed you?”

Nobody had mentioned a robbery. Vael looked at the others. “How do you—”

“Five people out of the Gatecut with no packs worth the name, lips cracked, and all of you wearing the same face.” She tilted her head, as if reading down a column. “It is the face of someone who has been very politely relieved of everything. Who was it?”

“Dorrek,” said Trista. “The Foundry. He asked us for a boat.”

“He does that.” Her mouth did something small and entirely unamused. “Lucky, then. They left you your blades. Last month they left a man his boots, and it was spoken of for days as an act of mercy.”

“Lucky,” James agreed, with enormous feeling. “We’re very lucky. They even left me the cask. I’d like that noted somewhere.”

“Noted,” said the girl, and very nearly smiled, and visibly stopped herself.

“Vael.” He put his hand on his chest. It seemed absurdly formal. “That’s James, and Trista, and Figo—”

“And Sheela,” said Figo, lifting the axe an inch. “This is my wife.”

There was a pause. Vael braced himself. But the girl only looked at the axe, and then, with the grave courtesy of someone who had been trained since the cradle in what to do when presented to the wife of a guest, she inclined her head to it.

“Madam,” she said. “I am honored.”

Figo’s whole face lit up. He looked around at the others with the radiance of a man whose child has just been called the cleverest in the class. “She likes her,” he whispered, to James, quite audibly.

“Everyone likes her, Figo,” said James weakly. “It’s the best thing about you.”

“And I’m Atver,” said the old elf, and he rose to his full, unimpressive height and bowed, stiffly and a little to the side, in the manner of a man who bows seldom. “I’d like to be charming. I regret that I am too dry.”

“And I,” said the girl, “am Aava.”

She said it simply, with no family name after it. Vael noticed. He noticed, too, that she had not said Aava of anywhere, and that she had paused for a fraction of a second before she said it, like a person stepping over a loose board.

“I live an hour’s walk from here,” she said. “It is a post, not a home. But it has a cistern and a floor, and a party of five that wanders the Gate Road after dark will be found by someone, and I would rather it were me.” She said it the way a clerk notes an item, not the way a person offers a kindness. “You may come. You will walk behind me, not beside me, and you will not touch the shelf.”

The tower stood on the inner slope of the left-hand cone, a thin black finger of stone that leaned a little to the south, like a man listening. It had been the Gate-warden’s, she told them, the last post on the old Gate Road. Its lowest floors were rubble, and the stairs began a good twenty feet up, reached by a rope ladder she hauled up after them. There were a hundred and twelve steps. Figo counted every one aloud.

At the top was a single round room under a cracked glass lantern.

It was very clean. That was what Vael noticed first: that in a country of ash, this room had been swept. There was a cot, made with squared corners; a table with one leg propped on a rag; a shelf bearing a ledger, a row of tins, neatly stacked bricks, and four jars sealed in wax. A great iron cistern stood in the corner under a funnel, and a little stove beside it burned something that smelled like old books, and was. A stack of bound ledgers stood by the hearth, with pages torn from the top one. Atver looked at the stack for a long moment and, visibly, chose to say nothing. Nothing was spare.

On the windowsill, in a cracked teacup, grew a smudge of grey-green.

Vael looked at it for a long time before he realized what it was. It was moss. It was no bigger than his palm, and it sat in a nest of ash like a green coin dropped on a grey floor, and it was alive. It was the only living thing he had seen in three days.

“It came in the third week,” said Aava. She had followed his eyes. “I don’t know where from. I don’t know what it eats.” She said it lightly, but she did not quite meet his gaze. “I give it a spoonful of water on the first of the week. I’m aware that this is absurd.”

“It isn’t,” said Trista softly. She had bent over the sill and was looking at it with a kind of tender solemnity, as at a sleeping child.

Atver had gone very still beside her. He lifted the monocle, and the lens shifted, and shifted again, and settled on a dull pearly white. “It’s eating the ash,” he said, to himself. “Not what’s in it. The ash itself.” And then, softer, almost wonderingly: “Well. I’ll be.”

“Is that good?” said Vael.

“I think,” said Atver, slowly, “that it means the ash is not the end of the story.” He lowered the monocle and straightened and, for a moment, looked almost hopeful. Then the look was gone, folded away somewhere, and he said, briskly, “Pray don’t tell the Wellhouse. They would eat it.”

Aava gave them broth.

It was a thin grey thing made from a crumbled brick and a spoonful of salt from one of the wax-sealed jars, hot in a tin cup, and it was better than any feast. She served it with the same exactness with which she’d served the water, five cups, filled level, and then she sat down on the one stool in the room and took out the ledger and opened it on her knee. The ledger was full of tidy columns.

“Forty-one bricks,” she said, running her finger down the page. “Eleven tins, of which three are of an unknown nature and I do not propose to open them. Four jars. The cistern holds nine days, if it does not rain, and it has rained twice since the Stopping.” She looked up. “I’m sorry. My father kept inventories. I find I cannot sit down in a room without doing one.”

“Eleven tins,” said James thoughtfully. “In a city that’s eating its own boots. Where does a person find tins?”

“Salvage,” said Aava, a shade too quickly, and turned a page.

Atver, who stood nearest the shelf, lifted one as if idly, turned it in his fingers, and set it back precisely square. Vael saw the lid. It was stamped, but not with the mountain and the flame. The old elf said nothing, and went on saying it.

“What’s your father’s—” James began.

“Later,” said Aava, quite gently, and turned a page.

So they ate, and she told them about the bowl.

There were two villages left, she said, and only two, and not because there had been no others. The Stopping had come on a morning like any other. (That was what the people here called it. The Stopping. “You call it the Starfall, I think,” she said. “We saw the stars fall too, a long way off, over the sea. But what mattered to us was the engines. It was the day they stopped, and nothing else about it counted.”) Twenty thousand had lived in the bowl. In the first week the dead were uncountable. The strong had scattered along the roads toward the sea, and some had come back, and some had not. What was left had drawn together around the two things that would keep them alive another night.

“The Foundry has the warmth,” she said. “Its vents are still faintly hot, and that is where Dorrek’s people sleep, in heaps, in the engine-halls. They have the lances too, stripped from every dead hauler within ten miles. The Wellhouse has the water. It’s a pumping-station on the deepest bore in the bowl, and the pump still turns by hand.” She turned a page. “Each of them needs what the other has. And each of them hates the other for it. It began with trade. By the second week it was theft. Now it is a war, and the war is conducted, you will have noticed, with the greatest possible economy, because nobody can afford to lose a single mouth.”

“That’s why they didn’t kill us,” said Trista.

“They didn’t kill you because they have not decided that they can afford to. Dorrek is not a cruel man.” Aava closed the ledger. “He was a foreman at the Third Foundry. He had thirty men under him and he knew every one of their children by name. He has ninety-one in that hall now, forty of them under ten. He has robbed seven parties since the Stopping, and he has asked each of them for a boat. I believe he would ask a corpse.”

“Why can’t they just leave?” Figo said. It was the first thing he’d said since the water, and it came out soft. “Why don’t they walk to the sea? Why not all go?”

Aava smiled at him, a little sadly, as one smiles at a child who has asked something wise by mistake.

“They tried. In the first weeks, hundreds tried. The sea is two days east of the Seat, but every hull in the harbor is an iron box that has never floated without the tide-engines to carry it. Machines laid the keels and drove the rivets. A man might know how to steer one, but there isn’t a man left in the bowl who knows how to make one.” She looked at her hands. “And a hull needs timber, or a forge. There is no timber. The last tree in the district grew in my father’s atrium, and they burned it in the second week to boil water. The forges are cold, and the people who knew how to light one by hand were engineers. Engineers were nobles. Nobles were the first to die.”

She said it quite evenly. Nobody said anything.

“So we cannot leave,” she said. “There is nothing here to build from, and nobody left to build with. The Imperium did everything. When it stopped doing it, we found we had forgotten how to do anything for ourselves.” She said it with a kind of tired pride, like a woman reciting a family motto at a funeral. “It was the finest order the world ever made, you understand. No hunger inside the rings. No war inside the rings. Everything provided, for everyone, by the mountains and the engines and the Seat. And it was murdered in a single morning by a sky that had never heard of us.”

“May I?” said Atver.

He had not turned from the window. He stood with his hands folded behind him and the monocle dark in the lamplight, looking at the moss.

“I have walked in and out of this country for the better part of a thousand years, Miss Aava, and I should like to correct three things, if you will permit an old man his one vice.”

“Please.” She closed the ledger on one finger. Her voice had gone very cool.

“No hunger inside the rings. Your terraces, down the slope, are glass over iron over ash. A harvest in this country was a sum, not a crop. Pipe, and heat, and a great deal of arithmetic. The land was dead long before the sky fell, child. It was held up, like a drowned man by a clever rope, by machines that did not tire. The sky did not kill your land. It only let go of the rope.”

She opened her mouth, and shut it again.

“No war inside the rings. Quite true. It is rather like saying there is no violence in a house with a slaughterhouse for a cellar. The lands beyond your borders were not provided for. More than one homeland was reduced to ash, and not by soldiers, or by haulers. It was done with engines built to take mountains apart, so that armies might walk through the gap. Your Seat called it expansion. The people it was done to had another word.”

“That was a war,” said Aava. “They struck first. We were taught—”

“Those people asked for terms three times, and were answered with a fourth engine.” He said it without heat, as one reads out a date. “It is in your own archives. I have read the copies. I can recite the dates, if you wish. I would greatly prefer not to.”

Nobody moved. Even James had found something interesting about his boots.

“And third.” For the first time he turned from the window. “Provided for. Who carried the water, Miss Aava? Who swept the avenues you were so proud of? Who scraped the vents?”

“The maintenance,” said Aava. “The craftsfolk. They were—” And she stopped, because she did not know the rest of the sentence.

“Quite,” said Atver, and his voice, for the first time all evening, was almost gentle. “I do not say it to wound you. I say it because you are asking why it ended so badly, and the answer is not the sky. The sky only cut the rope.”

For a long moment she looked at the ledger on her knee, at the tidy columns, at the neat pencil that hung from her neck on its string.

“I was taught otherwise,” she said at last, very quietly. “By people I trusted.”

“Most of us were. That is how it works.” He inclined his head, and the monocle caught the lamp. “You are asking a better question than you know. Hold on to it.”

“I think that is the cruelest part,” said Aava. “Not that it ended. That I lived inside it so long, and thought it was mine.”

The lamp burned low and blue in its hood. Far to the north, a long dark shine lay across the roofs like a spill of ink that had set, and at the heart of the bowl the thread of smoke glowed a small, steady red.

“We came to kill that,” said Vael.

He had not planned to say it. It came out of him because the room had gone still, and because the girl had given them water and a floor and the truth, and he had nothing to give her but his own.

“The Dracol,” said Aava.

“You know it?”

“I watched it arrive.” She rose and went to the window, and stood with her arms folded, looking out. “Nine days ago, over the north-western rim, on a wind like an oven door being opened. I have never seen anything so large. It was bleeding fire from the shoulder, from a seam in its plates, and one of its channels had gone dark, and it flew like something that was ashamed to be seen flying.” Her voice had dropped. “It did not come straight to the Seat. It fed first. Do you see that long dark shine to the north, where the roofs have run like wax? That was its table. It crept in a district at a time, eating the foundries, drinking the slag, sleeping in what was left, and I watched the glow of it from this window for four nights. And then, three nights before you came down, the whole bowl began to shake. It rose. It circled the Seat three times. Then it went down on the throne plaza and coiled itself around the throne as if it were a stove, and it hasn’t stirred since. Except to roar.”

“We know,” said Trista.

“Then you know why I shiver.” Aava’s arms tightened. “The Foundry sent twelve people to the Seat in the second week. They wanted the vents beneath the plaza, because they stay warm. That was before the beast. Two came back. Neither could tell me what they’d seen. One of them has not spoken since.”

“Do you think we can kill it?” said Vael.

She did not turn.

“No,” she said.

Nobody breathed.

“I’m sorry. You asked. I have lived six weeks by being honest about numbers.” She turned from the window then, and looked at them, one at a time, at the cracked-lipped, ash-grey five of them, and something in her face softened. “But I think you are on the right road. Because nobody else is going at all.”

The hum began a little after midnight.

By then the others had settled, wrapped in the blankets she had pulled from a chest, laid in a row along the curve of the wall like seeds in a furrow. Trista had insisted on checking Aava’s chest after she’d heard her cough, a small dry hack, three times in an hour, and Aava had submitted with the grim politeness of someone who had never been looked after in her life. Trista’s palm had glowed against her ribs, white and gentle, and when the light faded, Aava had stood in the middle of the room with her hand half raised and her mouth a little open.

“It’s quiet,” she said, in a very small voice. “My chest is quiet. I haven’t breathed all the way in since the Stopping.”

“It was the ash,” said Trista. “It’ll come back if you stay in it, but not so fast.”

Aava had not said anything to that. She had gone and sat on the stool with her back to them, for rather a long time.

Now Vael lay awake and listened to something that was not a sound.

It was a vibration. It came up through the floor, through the bones of his back, a note so low that the ear could not hold it, only the chest. It rose and held, and fell, like the note a very large man might hum with his mouth closed.

“It’s been doing that since the night of the Stopping,” said Aava’s voice from the dark.

She was sitting up in her cot. She had not slept either. “At first I thought it was my own pulse. Then I put a cup of water on the sill, and it shook. Look.”

On the sill, beside the moss, a tin cup trembled. Rings ran across the water inside it, small and perfect, in time with the note.

Beside the window, Atver stood in the dark with his back to the room. He had not lain down. He had not touched his broth. He had been standing there for an hour, with his palm laid flat against the wall, and the lens of his monocle burned a strange deep gold.

“It began the night the thread snapped,” he said quietly. “Not before.”

“The night after the Stopping,” said Aava, surprised. “Yes.”

“And it rises, a hair a week. And since the beast came, it has been steadier.” He did not say it as a question. “The beast did not choose the plaza by chance. It went to the warm place, and the warm place is warm because of what lies beneath it.”

“The Seat has a lower city.” Aava’s voice dropped. “My father went down to it every morning of my life, by a stair I was never to use, and came back smelling of hot oil, and never once said a word about what he did there. I used to watch from the gallery. The Provost’s Stair, they called it. A red arch on the east wall, with an iron door.” She hesitated. “In the week after the Stopping, I went to the door. I wanted to know what he had been doing down there. It was sealed. I put my ear to the iron, and something on the other side was breathing. Not like a person. Like a bellows as big as a house.”

Atver had turned. In the light of the blue lamp his face was unreadable, but behind the lens his eyes had begun to glow, faintly, with something Vael had never seen in them: a deep, quiet, very old hunger. The light of the monocle spilled over his cheekbone like gold.

“You were taught,” he said, “that the Seat spoke.”

“The Voice,” said Aava. “We had whisper-stones on every street, black pillars, one at every corner. When the Seat spoke, every stone spoke, and every engine in earshot listened. It was how the whole Imperium thought one thought. It went silent the morning of the Stopping.” She tilted her head. “But they hum now. All of them. Very faintly. The same note as this.”

“The Seat never said a word, child. It is a chair. What spoke was the thread beneath the world, and the stones were only its ears.” He took his palm from the wall and looked at it. “I have wondered for six weeks what else the Voice did. I think I know. I think it did more than give orders. I think it also said not yet.”

“Not yet to what?” said Trista, from the dark.

“The old workers had a saying,” said Aava slowly. “I heard it as a child, from the kitchen. I thought it was a joke. Never wake the one that moves mountains.”

Something went through Atver. It was not a flinch. It was quieter and stranger than that, the look of a man hearing the first bar of a song he had feared he’d forgotten. The gold in his lens flared to white.

“It is not a joke,” he said. “I will tell you what it is, once, and then I should like never to say it again.”

Nobody spoke.

“Beneath the Seat, behind doors that closed themselves, there is an engine that was never made to carry, or dig, or serve. The Imperium built it for the day a mountain stood between its armies and something it wanted. It does not hunt. It does not chase. It sends small things ahead of it, little flying eyes that walk the ground and drop marks, and it looks at what it has marked from beyond the horizon, and it begins to fire. It fires in lines. It fires in domes. It does not stop. When it has finished with a mountain, there is a hole where the mountain was, and when it has finished with a city, the ground itself, at the end, falls in.”

Vael found that he was sitting up. He did not remember doing it.

“Its makers gave it one instruction,” said Atver. “Continue until nothing stands. And then, being careful people, they built a voice that could tell it to stop.”

“And the Voice is silent,” said Vael.

“And the Voice is silent, and the engine is warm, and a Dracol has lain down on top of it. Twice a night it roars. Every roar is a hand on the sleeper’s shoulder.” He looked at the wall. “I should very much like for the vaults to stay sealed. I said so. I meant it.” A pause. “I did not say I had no wish to see what was inside them.”

“You knew,” said Vael.

“I suspected.”

“You said you’d tell me enough to get us moving in the right direction.”

“And have I?”

Vael thought of the tavern, and of the neutral, careful expression on the old elf’s face when he’d said that civilization was engaged in work it should not have been. “You have,” he admitted.

“Then I have kept my word.” Atver turned away again, toward the window. “I will tell you the rest in the morning, child. Sleep. You are going to need it.”

Vael did not know how long he slept. When he woke, the room was dark and the lamp turned so low that its blue core was a seed of light in the hood. The hum was in the floor. Along the curved wall the others lay in their blankets: Figo on his back with Sheela on his chest, Trista curled on her side, James with one arm flung across the cask. Atver’s place by the wall was empty, and a faint creak below told him the old elf was walking the ridge, as he did when he thought nobody was watching.

Aava was at the window.

She sat on the sill with her knees drawn up, looking out at the bowl, and did not turn when he came to stand beside her. At the far center of the plain a red glow lay along the rooftops of the capital, steady as a banked fire, and against it stood the dark crown of towers and three round domes, green with age, clustered like eggs in a nest.

“You should sleep,” said Vael.

“So should you.”

“I tried.”

“So did I.” She did not look at him. “That ring. Hold it up.”

He did, puzzled. In the dark the stone in it gave off a dull deep red, like a coal at the bottom of a banked fire, and now that he looked, it was brighter than it had been at noon, a little, in time with the hum.

“It does that near the beast,” he said. “Or near whatever is under it. It started a few days ago.”

“A ring that glows like a coal,” said Aava, to the window. “A wolf made of shadow. A boy who says the word Dracol like a man naming someone who owes him money.” She turned, then, and for the first time there was nothing careful in her face. It was only tired, and very watchful. “Where did you get it?”

“I don’t know.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It’s the only one I have.”

He had meant to say something short. But she waited, the way no one had waited for him in six weeks, without filling the silence, and it came out of him in a rush. He told her about waking in a tavern bed with nothing, no name, no face in the mirror he recognized, and a stranger kind enough to let him stay. He told her about the shadow that came out of his hand before his mind knew how, and the hand sign, and the other two summons, and the word Trista had said to him one morning, in the rising light: Dragodor. He told her about his mother. Saela. A woman in white, a hunter and a mage, whom he could only reach in dreams, and who had said, once, You must defeat the Dracol. You are our only hope, and who had said, I love you, son, and he had no face to put to the voice.

“I’ve been collecting her,” he said. “From other people. A woman who knew her told me she wore spotless white robes and kept to a plan even when she should have adapted. I’ve never met her. Not that I remember. I carry her around in pieces.” He rubbed his face. “And every day I wake up and I think, today it’ll come back. Today I’ll just know. And it doesn’t. And I feel like I’m running out of time, and I’m so—” He stopped. “I’m tired of being furious at a hole.”

Aava was silent a while. When she spoke, her voice had changed. It was not softer, exactly. It had simply stopped being a wall.

“My mother’s voice,” she said. “Do you know, I could not remember it. For weeks after. I’d close my eyes and try, and there was only the silence where it should have been. I made myself ill with trying.” She turned the ledger-pencil that hung from her neck on its string. “And one afternoon I was scrubbing a pot. In the second month. A common iron pot, with a rag. And without any warning, she was there, saying my name from the next room the way she used to, a little impatient, a little fond. As clear as a bell. I dropped the pot.”

“What did you do?”

“I finished the pot.” She smiled. “It had to be finished. But I remembered, afterward, that I’d stopped looking for her an hour before.”

“So I should stop trying.”

“I think the past is rather like a road,” said Aava. “You can stare at the horizon all day, and it will not come any closer. It only comes when you are walking.” She tilted her head, not looking at him. “You are already walking, Vael. I wouldn’t worry about the rest. It knows where to find you.”

He found he had nothing to say to that. It sat in his chest and was warm there, like something swallowed very hot.

“Then I owe you something true,” said Aava. “I find I cannot spend the whole night being clever.”

He waited.

“I was told to look for you.”

The hum rose in the stones. Vael did not move.

“Not by name. A woman came to this tower in the first week after the Stopping, when I had been four days in a cistern and had begun to talk to the water. She came in through a door I had barred, and put a tin on the table, and sat down as if she had been invited. She had a bow, and a coat that drank the lamplight, and a way of crossing the ash without leaving a mark. She never gave me a name.”

“Varesa,” said Vael.

Aava’s head turned. “You know her.”

“She fought beside us once.” He considered it. “I don’t trust her. I don’t think she means me harm. I don’t think she would tell me if she did.”

“That is exactly my position,” said Aava, dryly, and for a moment something that was almost fellow-feeling crossed her face. “She has fed me since. A tin, a brick, a jar of salt, every ninth day, left at the foot of the ladder. In exchange I watch the Gate Road and keep a count of who comes through it. That is all. A column of names and dates. I have never been told whom it goes to, and I have never been foolish enough to ask.” She drew her knees in. “I know the word for people who do that sort of work. Everyone in the Imperium knew the word and nobody knew a face. The Vigil. I am not one of them, Vael. I am a woman with a pencil who has to eat.”

She had said it fast, the way one pulls a splinter. Now she sat rigid on the sill with her chin up and her hands knotted around her knees, braced for whatever came next: the flinch, the cold look, the hand drifting to a weapon. She had plainly decided which of them she could bear.

Vael gave her none of them. He turned it over slowly. A tin on a table. A girl four days in a cistern, so hungry and so alone that she had begun to talk to the water, and a stranger walking in out of the dark with a bow, a quiet voice, and the only food in the bowl. He thought of the tavern, and of waking with no name, and of a stranger’s kindness being the one thing between him and the street. He knew what it was to take what was offered because there was nothing else to take, and to hate yourself a little for how grateful you were.

She didn’t choose a side, he thought. She chose a meal.

And the room looked different to him then. The squared corners on the cot. The ledger, kept so carefully. The water measured out level in five cups to five strangers, when she could have drunk it alone. It had never been a hiding place. It was a debt, kept in perfect order by someone who was terrified of defaulting on it.

Something in his chest eased and ached at the same moment.

“I know,” said Vael. He said it gently, and he meant all of it.

“Four days ago she came at dusk and said that five would walk the Gate Road, out of the sky. That one of them would carry a ring that burned like a coal. She said: Feed them. Tell me what they do.” Her mouth twisted. “Then the light came down, and I thought I had imagined it. And then you came out of the Gatecut looking like a dropped basket of laundry, and I thought: well. Not very impressive. But it fits.”

“You’re right about the laundry,” said James, from the dark. Nobody had known he was awake.

“James,” said Trista, from elsewhere in the dark.

“I’m asleep.”

Aava did not smile. “Tonight I am supposed to write that you are five hungry fools and that one of you wears a ring. I am telling you instead, because you told me something true, and I find I cannot write the lie. I will write something, Vael. I have to. There is no other tin.”

“Then write it,” said Vael.

She stared at him. “I beg your pardon?”

“Write what you need to. You have to eat.” He turned the ring on his finger. It was quiet now, a coal going dull. “I know some of them. Vigil. Not the ones who sent her. A few who want to change the sound of that name. And right now they want what we want, which is the beast dead. I would rather you ate and told them that than starved and told them nothing.”

“They want it dead,” said Aava slowly. “And so do you.”

“That is all I know of what they want.”

“For now,” she said, “is a phrase I have learned to listen for.”

“So have I.”

She looked at him for a long moment. Then, quite suddenly, she laughed, one short, startled breath, the sound of a person who has been braced against a door for weeks and found that it opened outward. “You are a very strange person to be saving anything.”

“I’m not saving it. I keep walking into it.”

She turned back to the window. The red glow lay along the rooftops, steady as a banked fire.

“Do you see the three domes? The middle one.”

“I see them.”

“I grew up under that one.” She said it flatly, the way one reads out an address. “The one with the broken finial. That was my father’s house. You can see the roof of my bedroom from here, if you know where to look, which I do, and which is why I’m up here at this hour every night, being foolish.”

He did not say anything. After a moment she went on.

“My father was an important man, Vael. I’m not going to tell you what he was, because I don’t know. I was twenty-one years old and I did not know what my own father did for a living.” She laughed, very shortly, and it was not a good sound. “I knew he went down the Provost’s Stair every morning. I knew he was respected. I knew that people stood up when he came into a room. I knew we had a cook, and a cellar, and a garden in the atrium with a tree in it that was older than the house. I knew that I was going to work beside him one day, in his work, and that it was something important and clean and very much needed, and I was proud of that. I never once asked what it was.”

The red light slid across her face. She did not seem to notice.

“The morning of the Stopping was his birthday. There was a cake. I was on the gallery over the plaza, because the family sat there for the Seat’s ceremonies, and I was holding the ribbon for him to cut. And the engines stopped.” She said it very evenly. “Not slowly. All at once, all of them, mid-step. Do you know what it is like to live your whole life on the back of something that carries everything, and have it let go? The haulers fell on the crowds that were under them. The pipes burst along the avenues. The vault doors came down, and some people were still standing in them.” She drew a breath. “My father was on the steps of the Seat. He wasn’t quick enough. I’m not going to tell you more than that. They weren’t quick deaths, and I saw every one.”

“Aava—”

“No, let me finish. I’ve never said it out loud. It’s very strange, to find that one can.” Her hands had tightened around her knees. “That was the first thing. Nobody tells you about the second.”

“The second?”

“The people who carried the water,” she said. “The ones who swept the avenues and scraped the vents and carried the ash out of the foundries in baskets on their backs. They had been waiting. Not for the sky to fall. For the first morning there was nobody left to stop them. They went up the hill with whatever they had in their hands, and they were not looking for a cause, they were looking for a house.” Her voice shook, and steadied. “I hid in a cistern for four days. I cut my hair with a kitchen knife. I learned to say pass in their accent, and to stand the way they stand, and not to say thank you too nicely. And I learned that I had been living for twenty-one years in a house built over a pit, and that the people at the bottom of the pit had been making the house the whole time, and that in all my life I had not once asked a single one of them their name. The old elf asked me tonight who carried the water, and I have lain here counting. In a house with ninety servants, I know the names of four.”

Vael stood very still.

“They were right to hate us,” said Aava. “I think about that every night. I wish they’d been a little less right about me.”

She was crying. She was doing it quite silently, without any sound at all, with her chin up and her eyes fixed on the red glow on the horizon, the way a child cries who has been taught that it is rude to be seen. It was the most dignified and terrible thing he had ever watched, and he found that he had no words for it at all.

“I thought every morning would be my last,” she said. “For weeks. I still wake sometimes and I’m surprised.”

“It wasn’t your fault,” Vael said.

“It wasn’t me who built it.” She wiped her face with the back of a grey-scrubbed hand. “But I ate from it.”

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The hum rose and held in the stones.

“After the beast is dead,” Vael said, “I’m going to find a way to get you out.”

She turned her head.

“All of you. The whole bowl. Back across the sea, to the mainland. There’s a world on the other side of it, and it’s alive, and it has fields and trees and people who’ll take you in. Nobody should have to live like this. Nobody deserves to.”

“That is a very large promise.”

“I know.”

“You can’t build a boat out of a promise.”

“No.” He found, to his surprise, that he was smiling, a small, tired, unsteady smile. “But I know people who might know how. A woman who built an elevator to the sky. A man who kept a whole town alive in a bunker through the worst day of its life. I don’t know how yet. I’m not going to tell you I do.” He met her eyes. “But I’ll find a way. And Dorrek goes first.”

“Dorrek.”

“He’s the one who asked for a boat.”

She stared at him. Then something unexpected happened in her face. It cracked, softly, at the corners, and out of the crack came a smile, small and startled and entirely real, the first she had let him see. It changed her whole face. It took years off her.

“That is the most honest promise anyone has ever made me,” said Aava. “You’ve no idea how to do it, and you’ve said so.” She wiped her eyes again, and this time she was laughing, just a little. “Very well. I’ll hold you to it. And in return, I’ll make mine. Whatever I have, you may have. It is lent to me by a woman I do not trust, but it is mine to lend. The cistern. The way down to the Seat.” She hesitated. “I mean, it is the party’s.”

“Thank you,” said Vael.

“Don’t mention it.” She seemed to find the sill extremely interesting for a moment. She tucked a strand of hair behind her ear, discovered that it was too short to tuck, and put her hand down quickly.

“I’m afraid I’m no help with the Dracol,” she said. “I only know the road.”

“The road is a great deal,” said Vael.

“Go to sleep,” said Aava. “You’ve a beast to kill, and I’ve a ledger to balance.” And then, a moment later, to the dark and the red light on the horizon, not turning her head: “Thank you for saying it.”

“I haven’t done it yet.”

“I know,” said Aava. “But nobody had said it before.”

The morning meal was a brick each and half a cup of broth, and it was the most pleasant breakfast any of them could remember.

Trista had woken early and washed her face in a spoonful of cistern water. Figo had slept so deeply that Sheela had slid off his chest in the night and he had woken clutching a corner of the blanket and had to be reassured. James had spent a quarter of an hour examining Aava’s shelf of tins with the frank interest of a connoisseur, until she took one out of his hand and put it back without a word.

It was James who noticed.

He noticed it the way he noticed most things, in the middle of a mouthful, and he went on chewing for some time with his eyes sliding from Aava to Vael and back, like a man watching a very small and interesting card game. Vael was telling Figo about the wolf. Aava was refilling his cup. She had refilled it twice already, to the brim, and the other cups only to the level mark.

“Our host,” James observed, to the room in general, “has a very generous ladle.”

“I beg your pardon?” said Aava.

“Oh, nothing. Just that she’s been looking at Vael since sunrise like he’s the last tin on the shelf.”

There was a small silence.

“I look at tins,” said Aava, with great dignity, “with considerably more suspicion.”

Trista choked on her broth. Figo, who had not understood any of it, looked from face to face with the pleasantly bewildered expression of a large dog at a wedding. Aava’s cheeks had gone the color of the stove’s coals, but her chin was up, and her voice, when it came, was crisp and cool as a ledger.

“If you would like to inspect the cistern, Mr. James, I would be glad to push you into it.”

“I’m just saying,” said James happily, “if there’s a spare room in this tower, I have never been so well placed to deliver a toast.”

“James,” said Trista.

“What are you talking about?” said Vael, genuinely puzzled, and the whole room burst out laughing, Figo a beat late and mostly because everyone else was. Even Aava did, behind her hand. Vael looked around at them with a baffled, good-natured frown, and went on eating his brick, the only one at the table neither laughing nor in on it.

Atver was waiting for them at the foot of the tower.

They came down the rope ladder one by one into a grey dawn, with the wind coming warm and slow from the east. He stood on the road with his pack at his feet, the same pack he had carried since the tavern. He had cleaned his monocle and brushed the ash from his burgundy robe. He looked, for the first time in all the weeks Vael had known him, like a man dressed for a journey that was not theirs.

“You are going,” said Vael.

“I am going.”

He had known it since the night. He had known it, perhaps, since the tavern. It did not make it any easier.

“I told you I came to learn why the Dracol is what it is,” said Atver. “That is true, as far as it goes. This is the rest of it.” He folded his hands. “The beast lies on a warm plate above a thing that was built to take mountains apart. The thing is waking, a hair a week, and the one voice that could tell it not yet has been silent since the Stopping. You will fight the beast. That is right, and I would not ask you to do otherwise. But you will fight it on top of the sleeper, and I do not think the sleeper will care which of you is winning.” He drew a short breath. “I cannot prevent the one. I can try to see that the other does not become the end of this bowl, and then of the road beyond it. Someone must be below, with a hand on the door. I have read what its makers wrote about it, and none of you have. If it can be told not yet a second time, I am the only one here who knows how to ask.”

“You’re going to the Provost’s Stair,” said Aava.

“If Miss Aava will be so kind as to show me where it begins.”

“And the Dracol?” said Trista. “You were going to fight beside us.”

“I have walked you to its door.” Atver smiled, very faintly. “I believe this is the door. Beyond it you do not need an old man with a monocle. You need each other.” He glanced at Vael. “And if the beast lies where I think it lies, directly above the sleeper, then every moment it holds your attention is a moment nothing below will stir. I shall be grateful for the noise. I should be a great deal less grateful for the blows. Fight it on the road, on the roofs, in the air, wherever you like. Pray do not fight it upon the iron.”

James whistled softly. “So we’re the distraction.”

“You are the reason I will get in,” said Atver. “It is a different thing.”

“Will you write to us?” said James. “Let us know you’re alive?”

“I do not write, James. I annotate.”

Figo, who had been standing with his lip trembling for a full minute, could bear it no longer. He crossed the ash in two strides and caught the old elf up off the ground in both arms. Atver’s feet dangled. The monocle swung on its chain. His face, over Figo’s shoulder, wore an expression of mild and philosophical resignation.

“Please put me down,” he said.

“I can’t,” said Figo, wetly. “I’ve only just got you.”

“Figo.”

“Sorry. Sorry.” He set him down, sniffing, and patted the robe flat with a hand the size of a spade. Atver straightened it and cleared his throat, and for a moment he looked, behind the lens, almost unbearably touched, and then he looked at Figo and said, with perfect gravity, “Mind that she does the thinking. She is the better strategist.”

“I always do,” said Figo.

“I know.” He turned. “Trista.”

She had already stepped forward, and she gave him the dip of her head, grave and gracious. “I’ll miss your lectures.”

“You will not. But you are kind to say so.” He regarded her a moment. “Keep them alive. You have a talent for it.”

“I’ll do my best.”

“You always do,” said Atver. “That is rather the point.” He looked at James. “You see a great deal more than you tell them. I understand why. I would only ask that, when it matters, you tell them the one thing that counts.” And to Vael, last, he turned fully, and for a long moment he only looked at him.

It was a look Vael could not read. The old elf’s face was composed, as it always was, courteous and dry and difficult, but something moved far back in it and was folded away again before he could name it.

“This is your battle,” said Atver, to all of them, in a quiet voice. “It was never mine. I have walked beside you long enough to say a thing I did not expect to say: I believe the world is in good hands. They are loud, and hungry, and prone to embracing, but they are good.” He drew a breath, and when he spoke again it was only to Vael.

“Do what is right for this world,” he said. “And trust yourself, child.”

Vael found that he could not speak. He nodded.

“That’s all,” said Atver, briskly, shouldering his pack. “That is the whole of my wisdom. I should hate to be thought to have more.”

He went with Aava, in the end, the two of them small against the haze, the old elf in his burgundy robe and the girl in her patchwork, walking east along a path that went by the cisterns and was not the Processional. He walked with his hands behind his back and his head a little bowed, listening, Vael thought, to the hum.

She came back alone, an hour later, climbing the road with her arms wrapped around herself. Her face had the closed, careful look of someone who has done a difficult thing and wishes to say so to no one.

“He’s gone,” she said. “I walked him to the head of the cistern road and told him how to find the red arch. He made me say it back to him three times, as though I might change my mind. Then he thanked me, quite gravely, and told me to go home.” She sat down on a milestone. “He seems to be a very odd man.”

“He’s all right,” said Vael. “He’s the best of us.”

“I’m inclined to agree.”

She got up. She found a length of pipe in the ash and knelt in the dirt and drew with it, a long line, straight, and a circle at the end of it.

“This is the Gate Road,” she said. “It joins the Processional at the Fourth Ring. After that, it is three miles of avenue, dead straight, with the engines on both sides. You’ll be seen from the plaza for the last mile. There’s no help for that. But the engines are tall, and if you keep close to the ranks, you’ll have cover almost to the end.” She tapped the circle. “This is the plaza. At the center is the throne. At the foot of the throne is a circle of iron plates, each as wide as a barn. They’re warm. I don’t know why. Stay off them if you can. It lies there.”

“What about this?” said Trista, pointing to a little mark at the east wall.

“The Auditors’ Gallery. It overlooks the whole plaza. The ceremonies were held below it. If one of you were to stand on that ledge, she could see everything on the iron circle without being seen.” Aava paused. “I’d wager that’s not nothing.”

“It isn’t,” said Vael. He was looking at the line in the dirt, at the long straight road to the Seat. “Aava, thank you.”

“Do not thank me. Do what you promised.”

“I will.”

“Then go.” She stood, and dropped the pipe, and wiped her hands on her patchwork skirt. “Be careful. All of you. Please.” And then she did something she hadn’t done before. She stepped close, and for a single moment she took his hand in both of hers, quickly, like a woman signing a paper, and let it go.

It was brief. It was almost nothing. Behind him, James opened his mouth, and Trista, without turning her head, trod hard on his foot.

They went down the Gate Road in a loose line, four of them now, with Figo’s great shoulders at the front and Sheela’s blade catching the first of the sun. Vael looked back once. She was standing on the milestone with her arms wrapped around herself, small and grey and straight-backed against the black cone, and she raised one hand. He raised his.

The ash took their footprints, one after another, and kept them. By the time they passed the Fourth Ring there was a long line of them stretching back along the road, four sets, wide and narrow, quick and slow. And beside the first milestone, where the road met the tower, a much smaller set stood a long while in the dust before they turned and went back the way they had come.

At the heart of the bowl, on the iron circle, in the red dark under the throne, something old opened one eye.

It had been lying quite still for six nights. It had listened to the machines hum, and to the thing beneath the machines, and to the long cold silence of a dead world. It had licked its wounds, which did not close. And now, on the wind that came across the bowl from the west, it caught a scent it knew. Warm blood. Salt on the skin. Iron and shadow, and a small, stubborn flame.

The black glass of its eyelid slid back.

Come, it thought, in the old tongue, in the slow burning dialect of volcanoes. Come, little ones. I am waiting.
