Chapter 7: What Floats and What Falls

Part 1 — First Ground

The elevator rose in slow, groaning increments, the ground falling away beneath the platform’s edge in a way that made the whole world feel like it was quietly reconsidering its opinion of them.

Trista noticed Figo first — the way his jaw had gone tight, the faint tremor working through his shoulders that he was trying, with visibly failing effort, to disguise as the cold. She’d seen this before, she realized, in smaller doses: a stiffness before certain jobs, a reluctance near cliff edges she’d never quite put together until now. When Figo starts shivering, she thought, watching him grip the platform railing hard enough to whiten his knuckles, it’s never actually the cold.

The higher they climbed, the quieter the workers’ call became below — “high-hooo” fading into something distant and thin — and the harder Figo’s composure cracked at the edges.

Everyone noticed eventually.

James, never a man to leave a vulnerability unpoked, clapped a hand on Figo’s shoulder with rather more enthusiasm than the moment called for.

Figo’s shiver became, instantly and comprehensively, a full-body shudder.

James grinned. “Guess big walls like you are built for the ground, huh. Unsurprising.” He hooked an arm around Figo’s elbow, dragging him gently but insistently toward the platform’s edge. “C’mon, it’s just a couple steps up. No big deal. See?”

Figo closed his eyes first. Then, against every survival instinct he apparently possessed, opened one to peek over the rail.

The sound that came out of him was not, strictly, a scream. It had the tonal quality of a boar in its final moments, a long, rising, undignified shriek that seemed to surprise even Figo.

Trista’s smile dropped at once and she half-rose, concern flickering across her face. But she stayed where she was, hands folded tight in her lap. Figo was a tough lad. He would face this, and he would do it on his own two feet.

Vael, however, felt the specific discomfort of watching someone he cared about being made into a joke, however affectionately, and stepped in. “James. Enough.” He pulled Figo firmly back toward the platform’s center. “Hey. Don’t look down. We’ll be on solid ground again soon enough.”

Figo’s breathing slowed, gradually, under the warmth of it. He gave Vael a short, grateful nod that said more than his voice currently could.

Trista watched the exchange with a small, private smile — the particular satisfaction of watching a good instinct confirmed. “James’s gut was right about you,” she said, half to herself, then louder, to Vael directly. “You were the piece we were missing. We can actually win this. We can kill that Dracol.” A beat. “Together,” she added, with the specific emphasis of someone trying hard to sound inspiring and landing somewhere closer to endearingly awkward.

“Thank you, Trista,” Vael said, and meant it. He gave her a small thumbs-up.

That was when she noticed the ring.

“Huh. Nice ring.” She tilted her head. “Where’d that come from?”

James’s eyes found it too, sharpening with sudden interest. “Oh, that. New toy?”

“Jade gave it to me,” Vael said. “A gift.”

“A gift, you say.” James’s tone stayed light, but something underneath it had gone very still. “Jadey didn’t give me anything.” He said it casually. It did not land casually.

“Gifts,” Trista said, entirely unbothered, “are for people who’ve earned them.”

James opened his mouth. Closed it. Decided, visibly, not to pursue that thread any further in present company.

“She told me about my mother,” Vael said, before the moment could curdle further. “What she wore. How she carried herself in the field.” He didn’t mention the rest — the Vigil, the deeper truth Jade was clearly saving to tell her brother herself, in her own time. That wasn’t his to give away. “It’s the closest I’ve felt to actually remembering her.”

James’s expression softened, some of the sting fading out of it. ”…Good,” he said, quieter. “That’s good, Vael.”

Atver had drifted to the platform’s edge, entirely unbothered by height, peering down at the underside of the drifting landmass ahead of them with open academic delight.

Vael joined him, curious despite himself. “Any idea how it actually floats?”

“Look there.” Atver pointed. Beneath the nearest island, barely visible against the dark stone, something glinted — a small, viscous orb, faintly pulsing, fixed to the rock like a growth that had decided to stay. “I’ve had my eye on that for some time now. It flickers, if you watch closely enough.”

He tapped the side of his brass monocle, and the lens shifted color, refracting the scene into something layered and strange — lines of faint light suddenly visible in the air around the orb, threading outward from it in slow, deliberate arcs.

“My analyzer confirms it,” Atver said. “Starfall debris. When a fragment that dense strikes something already unstable, it doesn’t just impact — it bonds. The orb down there isn’t holding the island up by force. It’s convinced the space around itself that gravity doesn’t apply here the way it should. A permanent, localized argument with the laws of the world, and so far, the world’s been losing.” He lowered the monocle. “Magic like that doesn’t come free. It’s bloomed everywhere the Starfall touched — new abilities, new growth, entire ecosystems accelerating past what they should be capable of in this short a time. It’s a miracle, in its way. But an argument with gravity doesn’t resolve cleanly. Whatever’s floating on borrowed physics can stop floating the same way it started — suddenly, and without warning. That’s the curse sitting under the miracle.”

“That’s insane,” Vael said, quietly, looking at the island with new eyes.

“Are we almost there,” Figo asked, in the specific tone of a man for whom “almost” could not arrive soon enough.

“Look!” Trista’s voice cut through, bright with relief.

The island rose to meet them.

It was, impossibly, beautiful — a month of isolation had let it become something the ground no longer had the luxury of being. Trees stood dense and green, canopies thick with birds that had never learned to fear a human silhouette and didn’t start now. Flowering vines threaded the rock in colors too saturated to be natural, and somewhere unseen, water ran steadily over stone.

“Woah,” Vael breathed, stepping off the platform onto solid, unmoving ground.

Atver was practically radiant with scholarly joy, already crouching to examine something growing at the treeline.

Figo dropped immediately to his knees and kissed the earth with the fervent gratitude of a man reunited with an old friend. Sheela, predictably, was set gently at his side throughout.

“Hey, Figo — not in front of Sheela!” James called out, grinning.

Trista couldn’t hold back a laugh. “Boys, am I right, Sheela?”

Figo, straightening with dignity, murmured a quiet apology to the axe and appeared to consult her expression for several seconds. “See,” he said finally, satisfied. “She doesn’t mind.”

“Sure she doesn’t,” James said.

A settlement had already taken root at the island’s edge — a handful of sturdy huts around a central storage structure where Skygate villagers sorted crates and bundles hauled up from below, with the brisk efficiency of people who had done this every day for weeks. Nobody stood idle.

A guard approached them — weathered, broad-shouldered, a long scar tracing one forearm, the kind of practical soldier who’d clearly done more building than fighting since arriving up here. “Welcome to Skygate’s first floating settlement,” he said. “Cleared and safe. Mostly.” He gave a short, tired smile. “Our Empress told us to expect you. Stay sharp out there.”

He held out a long, hollow pipe-like device, one end flared open. “Signal flare. Channel magic into it and it’ll fire a marked light, visible for miles. If you find trouble, or find our missing men, that’s how you tell us.”

“Thank you,” Vael said, taking it.

“No — thank you.” The guard’s expression turned briefly serious. “We’re stretched thin up here, and the ground still needs hands too. Every soul on a hunting party like yours is one less soul we have to spare ourselves.” He clapped Vael’s shoulder once. “Safe travels.”

He pointed toward two distant shapes on the horizon. “That’s the medium island, that’s the largest. Bridge connects the two. It’s not fully stable yet — mind your footing.”

He was gone before Vael could ask anything further.

The walk to the bridge took them through the island’s interior — dense undergrowth, sunlit clearings, luminous fungus at the base of old trees, a small herd of something deer-like grazing beside a stream that had no business being this clear this soon after the world had nearly ended. They crossed it in comfortable quiet.

The bridge itself, when they reached it, did not inspire confidence.

It was rope and salvaged plank, strung across a gap that dropped away into open sky and cloud, the wood weathered and creaking even in stillness, several boards visibly cracked.

“We’re here,” Trista said, already moving toward it.

Figo stopped walking.

“Hey, big man, what’s the holdup?” James breezed past him toward the bridge’s start. “We’ve got a Dracol to kill.” He put a foot on the first plank, testing it with theatrical unconcern. “C’mon, it’s safe enough.”

Trista’s smile faded into something gentler as she took in Figo’s face. “Hey. Don’t worry — I’ve got magic that’ll carry you if anything goes wrong.”

Vael studied the bridge — the fraying rope, the uneven planks, the sway even in the light wind. “We go across in pairs,” he said. “Figo goes alone. He’s basically worth two people’s weight anyway — no offense.”

Figo’s eyes, already wide with dread, widened further at the prospect of crossing entirely alone.

Trista and James went first, crossing without incident, the bridge groaning under their combined weight but holding. Atver and Vael followed, the small elf moving with the unbothered ease of someone who genuinely did not register the danger as significant.

Then it was Figo’s turn.

“You’ve got this, big man!” James called from the far side, in a tone that managed to be both encouraging and clearly enjoying itself.

“Just don’t look down,” Vael added.

“James,” Trista said, low and warning. “You should be encouraging him, not smirking.”

“He needs this,” James said. “Only way through a fear is straight through it.”

Figo took his first step onto the bridge like a man stepping onto the surface of a frozen lake he didn’t trust. The wood groaned beneath him. He pushed forward — one plank, then another, the whole structure swaying with each transfer of weight — until he’d made it, against every instinct in his body, to the bridge’s midpoint.

Then he looked down.

The distance yawned beneath him — sky and cloud and, impossibly far below, the vague suggestion of ground.

“Blughhh.” His whole body seemed to fold in on itself. “I — I can’t — go on without me—”

“You’re halfway there! You’ve got this!” Vael called, waving him forward.

The bridge’s creaking sharpened. Sharper. Sharper still.

It snapped.

“WOAHHHHH—”

The planks nearest Figo tore free of their moorings in a cascading collapse, rope whipping loose, and Figo’s weight, already unsteady, pitched forward into open air.

Vael was moving before his mind caught up to the decision. “Atver — Trista—”

“On it,” Trista said, all humor gone, hands already glowing as she touched his shoulder. “This’ll make you lighter.”

“Your turn, elf,” Vael added, and Atver’s voice rose in a short, precise chant: “Let gravity forget.”

Vael felt his own weight simply — leave. He summoned the Spirit Bird in the same motion he was already falling into, and it caught him, translucent wings beating hard against the thin air, propelling him forward and down toward the tumbling shape of Figo.

“TAKE MY HAND!”

Figo didn’t hesitate. His grip closed around Vael’s wrist like a vice, and the sudden weight destabilized the Bird’s flight instantly — both of them lurching, the ground and sky trading places in Vael’s vision.

He summoned the Flesh Toad on instinct, landing it hard on solid ground behind them, its tongue lashing out in a long, sticky arc that caught them both mid-fall.

“TRISTA, JAMES — PULL, LIKE A ROPE!”

“Grab the sticky tongue with my bare hands? Absolutely not—” Trista started, then caught herself, the urgency overriding the complaint entirely, and both she and James threw their weight into hauling the line hand over hand.

It held.

They came up short of the drop, dangling, sticky, breathing hard — but whole.

“Phew,” Vael managed, once his feet found solid ground again, his heart still hammering against his ribs.

Figo, deposited unceremoniously beside him, immediately turned and kissed the earth a second time with real feeling.

“Sorry, Sheela,” James muttered, red-faced and winded from the pulling. “That’s twice today.”

“James.” Trista was wiping toad saliva off her hands with an expression of profound distaste. “That was closer than I’d like.”

“No going back now, I suppose,” Vael said, looking at the wreckage of the bridge scattered across the gap below. “We owe Jade an apology. We just destroyed part of her infrastructure.”

“Eh.” James shrugged, already recovering his usual looseness. “Badly built creaky bridge. That’s on her engineers, not us.”

They pressed on to the second island — larger, the canopy denser, light falling in thick green shafts, roots thick as a man’s torso curling across the forest floor.

They had barely gotten their bearings when the sound reached them.

“HELP! CAN ANYONE HEAR ME?”

The voice was faint, ragged with exhaustion, coming from somewhere deep in the trees ahead.

“That could be them!” Trista was already moving, breaking into a run toward the sound before anyone could react.

“Trista — don’t go alone!” Vael called after her.

“Then let’s go,” he said, already breaking into a run himself. “We don’t split up.”

The others fell in behind him, and together, they ran toward the voice in the trees.

Part 2 — What Falls

Trista was fast. Nobody had ever quite put that on the list of things she was, because she spent so much of her time looking like a woman who would rather be reading, but with a voice calling for help ahead of her she ran like someone had cut a cord.

Vael kept her white robe in sight for the first hundred strides. Then the path bent around a wall of root and fern, and the robe bent with it, and when he came round the same curve there was only green. A trembling frond. A shaft of light with nobody standing in it.

“Trista!”

Nothing answered. Behind him, boots hammered the soil, Figo first, Sheela bouncing against his back, then James, already breathless and already complaining about it, and last of all Atver, who ran the way he did everything else, as though the ground had been informed in advance and had agreed to keep out of his way.

“She does this,” James panted. “She always does this. Runs off. Then gets annoyed that we’re not where she is.”

“The voice,” Figo said. “Listen.”

They listened. The voice had stopped calling. The forest had gone quiet around the place where it had been, and that was somehow worse.

It was Vael who noticed the silence properly. On the first island the canopy had been thick with birds that barely moved aside as the party passed. Here there was nothing. No wings in the branches, no scrabble in the undergrowth, not even the whine of insects. Only the sound of their own breathing, and somewhere ahead, very faintly, a voice that wasn’t calling anymore. It was talking.

They followed it.

She was kneeling at the foot of a fallen trunk, in a clearing where the light came down in long green shafts, and for a moment Vael’s mind refused to build the picture it was being shown.

There was a man in a blue-and-silver tabard, the colors of Jade’s banners, torn and gone dark with old blood. Trista had him propped against her, one arm braced behind his shoulders, her white robe soaked at the sleeves. He was sitting upright. He was sitting upright because there was nothing of him below the ribs to lie down with.

It was a clean cut. That was the thing Vael would remember afterwards, more than the blood: how clean it was. Whatever had done it had gone through leather and mail and bone like a shear going through cloth. Someone had tried to save him. Strips of torn tabard were knotted tight and layered around the wound, and there was a faint residue of healer’s light on them still, grey and spent, the ghost of a spell that had bought him hours and no more. Hours of lying here alone, calling.

He was breathing. Barely. Each breath was slow and shallow and took a long time to arrive, as if he had to go and fetch it.

Figo stopped at the edge of the clearing, and Vael watched his face change. The big man had seen what Vael was seeing, and he had understood what it meant in the way only someone who had stood over many such men could. There was no spell in the world that would put a body back where it had been taken from. Trista knew it too. Figo knew she knew.

“That’s…” James had stopped dead behind him. His eyes went to the man and fled, and his whole face tried to look anywhere else. “That’s an awful way to go,” he said, to a tree. His voice had lost its cask-warmed ease completely.

Atver, who had seen men die in every way the world offered, looked at the ground when he spoke, and his voice was quiet. “This is a tough way to go.”

Vael walked forward slowly, the way one approaches a sleeper. He had thought he knew every face Trista owned: the sharp one for arguing, the tired one for James, the bright one for a good idea. He had not seen this one. She wasn’t looking at the soldier the way a healer looks at a patient. She was looking at him the way you look at someone you have loved your whole life and are about to lose.

He crouched beside her. “Trista,” he said, gently. “He’s lost too much blood…”

She turned her head. Her eyes were full, and she was holding the tears back by main force. She didn’t need him to say it, and the look she gave him said so: I know.

“Tyra…”

The voice came from the man, and it was nothing, a thread of sound. He must have spent every scrap of strength on the calling. Vael forced himself to look, once, at the rest of him. The cut. The knotted cloth. The ground below it, dark and soaked. Then he looked away and did not look again.

“Tyra… is that you?” The soldier’s eyes had clouded, seeing someone who wasn’t there and someone who was, laid one over the other. His hand rose from his lap, trembling and slow, and found her cheek. “You’re as beautiful as ever…”

Trista didn’t flinch. Vael would think about that for a long time.

“I’m sorry,” the man said. “I’m sorry I left you down in the village. I promised you… your father… will be back.” A breath. A long one. “Here… I am…”

The hand began to fall. It hadn’t the strength to stay raised, and Trista caught it before it dropped, closed both of hers around it, and held on.

“Father!”

It was the happiest sound Vael had ever heard her make. Her whole face had lit up. She smiled the way a child smiles when the front door opens, and there was not a crack in it. “I knew you’d come back! You would never break a promise to me!”

The man’s eyes brightened. Even the dying can be given something to be glad of. “Ah… look at you. All grown up.” He tried to laugh and it became a cough, deep and wet, and the blood of it spattered across the front of her white robe. Trista didn’t look down. She didn’t let go. “How long… has it been…”

“I just grew quickly, Father.” Her voice didn’t shake. “That’s all. And now I can help you. Let’s go home together.”

Something moved across his face then, gentle and enormous, like a tide going out.

“Home,” he said. “Yes. Let’s go home.” His fingers pressed hers, once. “I love you, Tyra…”

He let out the breath he had been fetching. It didn’t come back.

For a moment the clearing held perfectly still. A shaft of light moved across the soldier’s face, and nothing in it moved.

Vael couldn’t have stopped the tears if he’d wanted to. Behind him James had turned fully away, one hand pressed over his mouth, and Figo had bowed his head. Atver stood with his hands folded and his eyes closed.

Trista laid the soldier’s hand on his chest, folded the other over it, and passed her palm gently over his eyes. When she spoke, it was so quiet that Vael had to lean toward her to catch it.

“May you find home.”

Then she looked up at Vael, and her composure came apart at the very edges. “If I’d been here sooner…” Her voice cracked clean down the middle. “If I’d been here sooner, maybe he could have seen his daughter.”

“It isn’t your fault, Trista.” Vael put his hand on her shoulder and left it there, and she let him. She breathed in, once, shakily, and then she stood, wiping her eyes hard with the heel of her hand, like someone shutting a door she meant to open again later.

“He wasn’t alone,” she said. “He had someone at the end.” Her eyes dropped to the knotted cloth, the spent grey light. “Someone healed him. Someone tried. That means there are others, and we need to find them.” And then the grief in her face, which had nowhere to go, turned and set into something harder. “And that Varkador…” Her voice dropped. “It will meet its end.”

James walked past her, quiet, and rested his shoulder against hers for a heartbeat as he did. Figo followed, and stopped at her other side. “Together,” he said, rumbling. “We make sure this doesn’t happen again. Right, James?”

James looked at Trista. He looked at Figo. Then he looked up into the sky, through the gaps in the canopy, as if the thing that had done this might be up there looking back. “Of course,” he said. His voice had no joke in it at all. “We’re hunters. That’s the job.”

Behind them, Vael found himself smiling. It was a small and sad smile, but it was real. Whatever else the road took from them, it was building something.

It did not last.

The shriek came from the left, close, so close the leaves rang with it. It was a high, raking, metallic scream, like a blade drawn across a plate, and every head in the clearing snapped toward it.

Something large dropped through the canopy.

It landed behind Vael and Atver with a sound like a felled tree, and the whole clearing shuddered. The Varkador stood almost as tall as a horse at the shoulder, slate-grey and iron-bright along the feathers, with a hooked beak the length of a man’s arm. It had the dull hunger of an animal that had eaten well for a long time and was now eating more. Along its crown ran a single feather of deep red, striped in black, standing proud above the rest.

“It smells our emotions,” Atver said, without turning his back on it. He raised his monocle, and the lens shifted color. “We’ve been lamenting at the top of our voices over a fresh kill in its hunting ground. It came for the grief the way it came for the blood. It’s a male. An alpha, most probably, and in prime condition. See the red-striped feather? That marks a male in his mating display. This is nesting season.” His voice sharpened with unwelcome interest. “He’s building. And a Varkador nest needs timber. And shiny things, for display. And fresh flesh, for the eggs.”

“Fresh flesh,” James repeated flatly.

“Everyone!” Vael’s voice came out louder than he’d meant it to. “Prepare to fight!”

The ring woke first.

Vael raised his right hand and felt the Ring of Eons answer. The dull stone at its center brightened from grey to ember to a white-hot, stuttering point, and the worn engravings along the band lit one by one like a fuse catching. The cost was immediate: a cold hollow opening behind his sternum as the ring drew on the well of power inside him, coiling it tighter, and tighter. The air around his hand began to hum.

Figo’s hand closed on the haft of his axe, and he took Sheela from his back the way another man might lift a sleeping child. “Sorry, my love,” he murmured, and shifted his weight down into the earth with the quiet stillness of a mountain deciding to move. Put an axe in his hands and a monster in front of him and the shiver went out of him entirely.

Trista’s palms came together and drew slowly apart. Between them a point of silver light bloomed, then thickened, then began to spin, throwing off tiny sparks like glitter caught in a draft. Pretty. Faintly rude. She tracked the Varkador’s head with narrowed eyes and rolled the growing sphere between her fingers.

James didn’t bother with a grin this time. He lifted both hands, and the shadows in the clearing drew toward him, pulled thin across the ground like cloth. They folded into the cup of his palms and compacted into a black sphere that drank the light from the air around it. From somewhere inside it, faint and thin and terrible, came the sound of distant screaming. The Varkador’s eye flicked to it and, for the first time, hesitated.

Atver simply stepped back to a safer distance and let his monocle spin through its colors, one hand raised in readiness.

The beast shrieked again, and then there was no more time.

It came forward in a single enormous lunge, wings half-open, beak leading. The wind of it flattened the ferns.

Vael thrust his hand out and released.

The blast left the ring in a white line, a lance of raw force that struck the Varkador in the chest-feathers and kicked. The beast’s lunge skewed sideways, wings flailing, bearing lost. It dropped lower than it had meant to, and Figo was already moving.

He ran three strides and jumped.

It was a ridiculous jump. A man of his size should not have been able to clear the beast’s shoulder, but he did, all of his weight coming down with Sheela raised overhead, and he landed square on the Varkador’s back and brought the axe down between its wings.

The shriek that answered was different from the others. It had pain in it.

“THAT’S where it hurts, huh?” Figo bellowed, clinging on with one arm, driving the axe down again. “HAHAHAHAAA—”

The Varkador, quite reasonably, decided it would like him off.

It threw itself up. One beat of its wings took it above the treeline, two took it higher, and Figo’s laughter went with it, stretched thinner and thinner as the canopy fell away beneath him. Then the first crack appeared in it. Then the second.

“…ha… ha—”

He looked down.

“WOAH WOAH WOAH, WOAH, THAT WAS NOT THE DEAL!” The voice of a man who had just discovered, at a considerable height, that the thing he had agreed to ride was not going to stay near the ground. “DOWN, BIRDIE! DOWN! DOWN!”

Vael had already seen the whites of his friend’s eyes. He knew what this height meant to Figo. He didn’t think at all.

His hands were already folded into the sign. The Spirit Bird poured out from behind him, cold and pale and sudden, and its claws closed on his shoulders.

“Vael!”

Trista had jumped. He felt her hands lock around his forearm just as the ground let go of his boots, and the bird lurched under the extra weight, then steadied. She didn’t waste breath on a plea or a complaint. The glitter bomb was already blooming in her free hand, and her eyes were fixed on the sky above them.

“Get me close to that wing,” she said.

He didn’t argue. He drove them up after the beast and its passenger in a long, climbing arc, and then the Varkador’s wing was beside them, enormous, iron-feathered, beating.

Trista flung her arm back and threw.

The glitter bomb struck the beast’s right wing at the joint and detonated in a silver sunburst, each spark a tiny hammer, and the wing buckled. Vael swung his hand out wide and let the ring speak again, its second blast cost twice what the first had, a deep dull ache where his strength had been, and the white lance punched through the left wing’s flight feathers in a spray of shattered quills.

The Varkador screamed once, a high, wild, broken sound. Both wings failed.

It began to fall.

And the Spirit Bird, which had carried more than it was ever asked to carry, and which had already given everything it had that day, simply came apart in Vael’s hands. A last cold breath, a scatter of pale light, nothing. It was gone. It wasn’t coming back for a while.

There was a very brief, very silent moment in which Vael and Trista understood their situation.

Then the air started rushing upward past their ears.

“AAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAA!” Figo was falling alongside them, still more or less attached to the Varkador, which was a very bad thing to be attached to. “WE ARE GONNA FALL! WE ARE GONNA FALL! WE ARE—”

“We’re already falling!” Trista screamed back.

From the ground, very small and very far away, one old elf drew a breath, thrust his open hand toward the sky, and spoke in a voice that carried far further than it should have.

“Let gravity forget.”

The rushing stopped. It stopped for the three of them, anyway, as a weight lifted from Vael’s chest and his stomach came back up out of his boots. They went from plummeting to drifting, fast still, but suddenly soft, like falling feathers.

The Varkador was not so lucky. Atver’s spell had not touched it. It dropped like a dead stone, wings flapping uselessly, and Figo, with the speed of a man who had just been returned to his senses, kicked free of its back with a bellow and let the spell have him.

It hit the earth hard.

The ground jumped. Dust and leaf-mold rolled outward in a ring, and in the center of it the Varkador lay twisted, one wing folded wrong under it, still alive, still shrieking, trying to heave itself upright.

James was waiting.

He hadn’t moved from where he’d stood. His orb hung over his hands like a held breath, the screaming inside it gone quiet. He let it go.

It wasn’t a single blow. It was a barrage, one dark sphere after another hurled in a ragged, furious rhythm, each one crushing inward on impact with a sound like wet cloth tearing. The Varkador bucked, twisted, shrieked. Another orb. And another. James’s face was set, white, all of the lazy humor burned out of it, and his lips were moving.

“Die,” he said. “Die. Die.”

The shrieking thinned. It cracked in the middle. It stopped.

When the dust cleared, the great bird lay still, one eye half open on the green light that had never done it any harm. The red-striped feather rose and fell once in the breeze. The clearing was silent again.

They came down in a heap, more or less. Vael landed on his feet, which surprised him, then discovered his knees weren’t going to hold, and sat down heavily in the leaf-mold. Trista stayed flat on her back, staring at the canopy. Figo lay full length on the ground, spread-eagled, with Sheela clutched to his chest, and breathed.

“Well done, everyone,” Vael said, and was surprised to hear that his voice shook a little. “We make a great team.”

Nobody argued. Nobody had the air.

James came over, slow, and crouched beside Figo. For a moment he didn’t say anything. Then, softly: “You did well, big man.” A hand on his back, steady. “You were brilliant.”

Figo’s breath hitched. He was still breathing hard, and Vael understood that the man had spent the last two minutes in the place he feared most in the world and was only now coming back. But he nodded against the earth, and the hand on his back stayed there.

Trista was already up. She’d brushed off her robe, which only made the blood on it more visible, and her face had resumed the shape of a decision. “We find its nest,” she said. “Now. If there are more people out there, they may still be in time.”

“I believe I can track it,” Atver said, tucking his monocle away. “A Varkador in season leaves a trail only a fool could miss. Follow me.”

“Wait.” Figo had pushed himself to his knees. “The soldier. We have to bury him first.”

“We’ll come back for him,” James said, a hand on his shoulder, not unkindly. “There could be someone alive out there.”

“James is right,” Vael said, and saw the big man’s mouth tighten. Figo said nothing for a moment. Then he got up, and slid Sheela back onto his shoulder.

“We’d better come back,” he said, to no one in particular. “A soldier deserves a proper burial.”

He did not look at the body again as they left the clearing. But Vael, glancing back, saw him raise two fingers to his brow, a salute, brief and awkward and sincere, and turn away before anyone could comment.

They followed Atver for an hour.

The old elf walked with his head slightly bowed, as though listening to the island, stopping now and then to crouch and touch something with two fingers: a crushed fern, a long scrape in the bark. And as they went, the island told its own story, if one knew how to read it.

“Look at the undergrowth,” Atver said quietly. “What do you see?”

Vael looked. The forest floor was a riot. Ferns grew head-high where there should have been trampled paths. Brambles had swallowed the base of the trees. Fallen fruit lay in drifts beneath the branches, bruised and splitting and swarming with flies. There were almost no tracks. Hardly anything had touched any of it.

“It’s too green,” Vael said slowly. “There’s too much of it. And hardly anything’s eating it.”

“Precisely.” Atver nodded. “On the first island, the growth was kept in check by those who lived there. Grazers, browsers, birds, scavengers. A living web. Here, the web has been pulled out thread by thread.” He stopped and laid his palm against a tree-trunk scored with deep, hooked scratches, higher than a man could reach. “Our friend has been sharpening his beak. And look there.” He pointed. In the crook of a root lay a tangle of small pale bones, cracked open at the ends for the marrow. “Browsers first. They’re easiest. Then the creatures that eat the browsers’ leavings. Then the ones that eat those. Whatever sat at the top of this island’s table has been eating down the rest of it for a long time.”

Further on, the stream that cut across their path was choked to a trickle with reeds that no grazer had thinned. A scatter of iron-grey feathers, longer than Vael’s forearm, hung in the thorns like torn banners.

Then Vael saw the one thing that did not belong. A bramble beside the stream had been trimmed. Not torn, not trampled: trimmed. A neat row of leaves had been bitten from its lowest stems, each bite no wider than his thumbnail, and the bush beside it showed the same, and the one beyond that, one careful mouthful from each plant and never two from the same.

“Atver,” he said. “Something’s been eating here.”

“So it has.” Atver crouched and ran a fingertip along the bitten edge, and for a moment something in his face eased. “Something small. Something with excellent manners, that takes just enough to live on and nothing more, and keeps well away from the open ground.” He rose, dusting off his knees. “The Varkador has not eaten everything on this island. Only everything it could see.”

“The Starfall made things quick,” Atver continued, almost to himself. “The magic that settled here lifted everything faster than it should have grown. The prey grew. But the hunter grew faster. A creature with no rival, no one to keep it in check, grows to meet its appetite, and appetite eats its way down to the foundations.” His voice was low. “Then it turns to anything with a pulse. Soldiers. Travelers. Us. It isn’t malice. It’s a mouth that has run out of other things to feed.”

“So it was starving,” Trista said, very quietly.

“It was thriving,” Atver said. “Until there was nothing left to thrive on.”

Nobody said anything after that.

The nest sat in the crown of a dead cedar, where the island’s ridge fell away to open sky, and Vael smelled it long before he saw it.

It was enormous. A great round platform of branches, woven tight, and into it, like trophies, everything the Varkador had thought worth keeping. Planks from the broken bridge, still bearing rope. A cart wheel. Splintered beams. Armor plate, helmets, belt buckles, bits of mirror, anything that caught the light, all jammed into the weave and glittering dully under the afternoon sun. Bones, old and white, in drifts.

And the rest of it. Vael looked once, and then looked at a branch.

“It’s worse than I thought,” Atver said, and for the first time his voice had gone dry. He stood at the base of the tree, studying the structure with his lens, though he did not seem to be enjoying it. “That Varkador was apex here for far too long. It grew too strong, and it broke the food chain. It had nothing left to eat but its own neighbors.” He lowered the monocle. “It’s a mercy we killed it.”

Vael turned to say something, and found that the person he’d meant to say it to was gone.

“Trista?” He looked around the base of the tree. “Trista!”

Faintly, from somewhere beyond the ridge, came a sound he knew he’d been dreading. It wasn’t a scream. It was a low, ragged sobbing, the sound of someone trying not to make a sound.

He ran. Figo and James were behind him within a heartbeat. Atver, apparently too absorbed in the nest to follow, stayed with his lens and his notes.

She was kneeling in a hollow beneath the ridge, among the roots, and around her, in the thin light, lay four bodies. Blue-and-silver tabards, shredded. They’d been dragged here and left, the way a predator leaves what it will come back to. There was nothing left to save in any of them. There had not been for days.

“No,” James said, softly, and the word came out like something breaking. “No, no, no…”

Figo said nothing at all. He stood at the edge of the hollow, his big hands hanging empty at his sides, and his face looked as though it had been struck.

Four. And the man in the clearing. Five, Vael thought, and the word fell through him like a stone down a well. Five again. He thought of a row of fresh graves outside a tavern, and of Ragnik’s hand on the nearest one, and of the weight of it. He had thought that he had left that behind him.

“We should have been faster,” James whispered.

“We were as fast as we could be,” Vael said, and the words felt thin even as he said them.

Trista wiped her face. It took her two tries. When she spoke her voice was steady, as steady as it had been at the fallen man’s side, and it was worse for that. “We use the flare,” she said. “Now. The people must know.”

Vael took out the hollow pipe the guard had given them at the settlement, fed a thread of his own magic into it, and lifted it to the sky. The flare left it with a hiss and a bright, climbing scream of red light, high over the dead cedar and the broken nest, and hung there, burning against the afternoon like a second sun.

Figo watched it climb. His hands had curled into fists.

“They’ll come,” Trista said, and turned to him, and her voice gentled. “Jade’s people. They’ll come and carry them home, Figo. They’ll bury every one of them properly. All five.” Her eyes held his. “We can’t be the ones who do it. But they will.”

Figo’s jaw worked. For a long moment he said nothing, and then he set Sheela down in the roots and went to one knee beside the nearest of the fallen. He laid his hand, very lightly, on a soldier’s shoulder. “Rest easy,” he said, rough and low. “The sky won’t have you.” He stayed there a breath longer. Then he rose, and took up his axe, and when he turned his face was wet and he made no attempt to hide it.

“And the Dracol?” James said. He sounded tired.

“The Dracol is still waiting for us.” Trista picked up the thread as if she were picking up her pack, and each word was a stone she was setting down in front of herself to walk on. “We keep going. We finish this. If we turn back now, there will be more of this, and no one to stop it.”

Vael looked at her, and had no idea what to say.

He had seen her angry. He had seen her frightened, and sarcastic, and exhausted. He had not seen this, the whole weight of a thing carried by someone who would not set it down. Words crowded behind his teeth, and none of them were good enough. In the end, he simply stepped up beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and stayed there.

She didn’t look at him. But after a moment, she leaned very slightly into him, a few degrees, no more. It was the closest thing to an answer she could give.

“I’m alright,” Trista said, to the sky, to the flare, to the five. “We have to make sure this Dracol is dealt with. To save everyone.”

Nobody told her she wasn’t.

They gathered themselves wordlessly, Atver coming down from the nest with his notebook closed. Behind them, the red flare burned on, thinning against the sky, a signal to anyone who would come.

They walked on toward the Molten Imperium, and the island gave them nothing but silence the whole way.

Part 3 — What Waits

Far to the east, past the last of the broken sky and the dead ranges that had once fed it, the Dracol came home.

It came slowly. It had been coming slowly for weeks, in short, grudging stages: a night’s flight, a day spent curled in some cold ravine where it scorched the stone black beneath it, another night. It was how a hurt thing crosses a long distance when it cannot afford to be seen and cannot afford to stop. The wings that carried it were not the wings it had been born with. The left one hung a shade paler than the right, its obsidian thinner along the leading edge, where something that was not quite a bird had once passed through it as though through smoke, and every downstroke on that side came a fraction late, so that the whole vast body flew with a faint, constant list, like a ship with a bad keel.

The rest of the damage was quieter, and worse. High on the left shoulder a seam of glassy stone ran crooked across the plates where a thrown axe had split them. The lava beneath had sealed the wound, but not truly, and a thread of orange still wept from the weld and crept down the black like a tear that had learned not to fall. Lower, between two plates of the chest, one vein of fire had gone dark. It had been dark since an arrow made of shadow found it on a frozen ridge, and the rest of the lattice pulsed unevenly around the dead channel, like a heart that had skipped a beat and never quite found the rhythm again. Its neck, once snapped back by a sphere of silver light, was carried an inch too low. And the glow of it all, the molten map that had once blazed along the whole length of its skeleton, had been banked to the dull, patient red of coals under ash, because everything the Dracol had left was going into the work of staying alive.

Its eyes were the one thing the weeks had not touched. Two wells of liquid fire, bulging in their sockets with the strain of something that had not stopped hurting since the day it was born, they swept the land below as it came. There was very little left to see.

The city lay open beneath it like something laid out for study.

It had been the largest thing that ever deserved the word. It filled a bowl of black ground a day’s walk across, ringed by the cones of old volcanoes whose rims were still collared in iron: enormous ribbed rings, rust-red against the dusk, from which vast pipes ran down the slopes like the roots of an inverted tree and sank into the plain. Through those pipes the heat of the mountains had once been drawn down to the forges, and the forges still stood, row upon row: chimneys without smoke, furnace-mouths without fire, blackened by six thousand years of work that had never once stopped for the night. The avenues between them were wider than rivers. They had not been built for people. They had been built to move engines.

And the engines were still there.

They stood in the avenues where they had stopped, in ranks, thousands upon thousands of them: great hulking shapes of dark iron with blank faces turned toward whatever order they had last been given. Haulers with their arms half-raised. Diggers caught mid-stride. Behind them, taller shapes of siege that stood higher than the rooftops. They had not fallen or been broken. They had simply stopped, all together, on the same morning, as though the world had blown out a lamp, and ash lay on their shoulders in a grey, undisturbed fur.

There was no smoke over the city but one thread, and the thread was the Dracol’s.

At the center of the bowl, where every avenue converged like the spokes of a wheel, lay a plaza of black stone as wide as a lake, and in the middle of it stood a throne. It had not been made for anyone to sit on. Carved from a single mass of volcanic rock, tall as a watchtower, its back rising in a jagged crown against the dusk, it had been raised as a promise: that the world would be brought to heel, and that this was the seat from which it would be done. Around its base the paving was set with a vast circle of iron plates, each wider than a barn, fitted so closely that no seam showed. They lay shut flush with the ground, unscarred by quake or fire, as though whatever lay beneath them had closed itself, deliberately, on the last morning.

The outer districts it had already worked. It had reached the city’s rim days ago and had been eating its way inward since, whole blocks of the outer ring run to black glass behind it, the iron of a hundred stopped engines melted down and pooled in channels that still glowed a sullen orange in the dusk. It fed on destruction, and had found a banquet. Tonight, for the first time, it meant to claim the center.

It circled the plaza three times, the way a hound turns before it lies down, then let itself fall the final ten feet, and the paving cracked beneath it. It coiled around the foot of the black throne, its length laid across the iron circle. The plates were warm. Deep beneath them some old fire had not entirely gone out, and a hurt thing goes toward warmth. It laid its chin on the stone. Its eyelids, two plates of black glass, began to lower.

Then it saw the red light.

It was far to the west, so far and so thin that anything less watchful would have taken it for a late star. High above the horizon, where the ruined ranges gave way to the floating stone, a speck of red hung in the dusk, unsteady and burning low. It did not twinkle. It did not drift. It had been put there on purpose.

The Dracol lifted its head.

It had been hunted before. It remembered a frozen ridge, and small warm things that had hurt it far more than anything so small had any right to, and it did not need the word signal to understand what a red light in the sky was for. Someone was coming.

The lava in its eyes turned once, slowly, and fixed on the west. For a long moment the whole dead city seemed to hold its breath with it.

Then it reared up against the black flank of the throne, to the full height of its ruined body, and roared.

It was not the roar from the ridge, not the territorial bellow, not the hunting cry. It was longer, and slower, and entirely deliberate, a sound that began in the deepest part of the chest and rolled across the bowl of the city like a wave crossing a pond. It was not a cry of pain, though the pain was in it. It said, in the only language the thing had ever needed: I am here. I am waiting. Come, if you dare.

Ash slid from the rooftops. Windows that had been dark for a month shuddered in their frames. And along the great avenues, row on row of motionless machines took the note into their iron chests and gave it back a half-tone lower, ten thousand dull bells struck once and ringing, until the last of the echo died among the ranks.

In the silence, the Dracol lowered its head onto the black stone and watched the red speck in the west go out. It did not sleep.

They had not walked a mile before Figo stopped.

He stood in the middle of the path with Sheela on his shoulder, looking back toward the ridge and the dead cedar and the hollow beneath it. Above the treeline the last of the flare was a thin red thread, dimming in the afternoon haze.

“We can’t,” he said at last. “I know what Trista said. I know they’ll come.” He shook his head slowly. “But that was a nest back there, and the scent’s still all over that place, and whatever else it drew in…” His jaw worked. “I can’t leave them lying out there for the next thing that wanders by.”

Nobody argued. Trista, who had every reason to keep walking, looked at him for a long moment and then simply turned around. “I wasn’t going to be able to leave them either,” she said quietly.

James opened his mouth, plainly intending to say something about the wisdom of putting distance between a person and a Varkador nest, looked at the other three faces, and closed it again. Atver gave a small nod, as if a question had been answered correctly. Vael said nothing. He just turned and walked back with them.

They sat on the ridge above the hollow while the light went gold and then amber, and they did not talk. It seemed wrong to. Once, Figo got up and moved a fallen branch away from the edge of the hollow, then sat down again. That was all that happened for the better part of two hours.

Dusk came early beneath the canopy. They heard the soldiers before they saw them: the groan of a hauling-cage somewhere beyond the trees, then boots on the trail, then voices pitched low in the way of people who already suspect what they have been sent to find.

A dozen soldiers in blue and silver came up the ridge path in a column, torches lit against the dark, hauling a two-wheeled cart between them. At their head walked the weathered, scarred guard who had pressed the flare into Vael’s hands. He looked at the hunters, and then at the hollow behind them, and did not need anyone to say anything at all.

He stopped at its edge. For a moment he simply stood there with the torch hanging from his hand, looking down.

Behind him, a young soldier took one look and turned away sharply, a hand over his mouth, and only just reached the nearest tree. Another, older, dropped to one knee in the leaf-mold. No one laughed. No one spoke. The torches hissed softly in the damp.

“Orrin,” the scarred guard said at last, very quietly, reading the braided blue-and-silver cord tied about a wrist. He moved to the next. “Pell. Tamsin.” Each name came out like something being set down with great care. He had to stop before the fourth. “Brenn.”

Vael held each name as it was spoken. He had stood before five fresh graves outside a tavern and had not learned one name in time. He learned these. He promised himself he would not lose them.

“There were five,” the guard said, and his eyes came up to find Trista’s white robe, stained brown to the elbows, the only white thing in the dark. “Sergeant Hollis led them. Where is Hollis?”

“In a clearing, about an hour back along the trail.” Trista’s voice was steady. “He was alive when we found him. He wasn’t alone when he died. I was with him.”

The guard closed his eyes briefly. “Then we’ll fetch him last,” he said, “and I’ll want you to tell me about it. But first these four.”

The soldiers brought pale sailcloth shrouds out of the cart, and then it was the work of hands. Vael and Figo went down into the hollow first. Nobody had asked them to, and nobody told them not to. They lifted Orrin between them, Vael at the shoulders and Figo at the knees, and Figo, who could have carried a horse across a river, carried the dead man as though the weight of him might still be hurt by clumsiness. “Easy, friend,” he murmured, to a man who could not hear him. “Easy. I’ve got you.” The scarred guard watched that for a moment, then looked away with his jaw tight and bent to the next shroud.

It took a long time. Nobody wanted it to take less.

When the four lay side by side in the cart, covered, the guard stood with his hand flat on the sailcloth over Brenn’s shoulder, and then turned to the hunters. “Thank you,” he said roughly. “Most would have gone on. And don’t give the bridge a thought. The engineers have been wanting an excuse to rebuild it properly.”

The clearing was an hour back by torchlight. Trista walked at the head of the column with the guard beside her, and the others followed the cart as it jolted over the roots.

The light was nearly gone when they reached it. The fallen trunk lay where it had lain all afternoon, and beyond it, sprawled across the ferns in an awful, quiet heap, lay the Varkador. In the torchlight it looked far larger dead than it had alive. Someone said “Gods,” under his breath, and no one answered. Vael, glancing at its head, felt something snag at the back of his mind — the long red-striped crest feather he had seen standing proud above the beast’s skull was gone, and the place where it had been was a neat, bare patch. He was too tired to wonder at it. He let the thought slide away.

Hollis lay where Trista had left him, his hands folded on his chest, the strips of knotted tabard still pale around the wound. She knelt beside him, and the scarred guard knelt on his other side, and the soldiers stood back in a half-circle with their torches held low.

“He asked for his daughter,” Trista said.

The guard looked up.

“At the end. He didn’t know where he was. He thought I was her.” She wasn’t looking at the guard. She was looking at the sergeant’s folded hands. “He called me Tyra. He said he was sorry he’d left her in the village, that he’d promised he would come back. He said, here I am.” Her voice caught, and she waited until it held. “I let him think I was her. I told him I’d grown up fast, and that we’d go home together. I didn’t know if it was right. I only knew he was frightened, and I had nothing else to give him.”

For a long moment the guard said nothing. Then he reached out, with great care, and touched the dead man’s sleeve.

“It was right,” he said. “It was the kindest thing anyone could have done.” He drew a long, unsteady breath. “Tyra is his girl. She’s seven. She and her mother have been staying with the cooks since the expedition went out. The girl asks every night when her father is coming home.”

Nobody moved. Behind them, one of the soldiers made a small broken sound and turned his face away.

“Tell her,” Trista said, and her voice finally broke, “tell her he came back. Tell her that he kept his promise, and that he wasn’t afraid at the end, and that he said he loved her.” She pressed the heel of her hand hard against her eyes. “Please. Tell her that, exactly.”

“I will.” The guard’s own voice was thick. “I swear it. She’ll hear it from me before anyone else says a word.” He wiped his face on the back of his scarred forearm, unashamed. “And she won’t be alone. None of them will. Every one of the five has people waiting in Skygate, and the Empress has already given the order: their families will be told by someone who knew them, not by a messenger, and housed and fed and kept for as long as they need it. Nobody Skygate loses in her service leaves someone behind to go hungry.” He looked at the sergeant’s face. “Hollis’s girl will be taken into the Empress’s own household, if she wants it. I’ll see to it myself.”

Trista nodded. She could not speak anymore. Vael, who was standing nearest, put a hand on her shoulder.

Figo stepped forward from the edge of the torchlight.

“May I?” he asked, very gently.

The guard looked at the huge man, at the scars, at Sheela, who for once was not on his shoulder but lying quietly on the ground by his feet, and he understood. He nodded.

Figo gathered Hollis up in his arms. He did not carry him as he had carried the others. He carried him the way a father carries a sleeping child up the stairs, one hand cradling the back of the head, and he walked every step of the hour back to the cart without letting anyone relieve him of it. Vael picked up Sheela, who was heavier than she looked, and followed behind with her, because someone had to.

The hauling line ran from a squat timber landing at the island’s edge out across the dark gap toward the first island, a single thick cable with a cage hung beneath it. The soldiers loaded it in near silence. Five shrouds, side by side. A bundle of torches, burning down.

The scarred guard shook hands with each of the four hunters in turn, and bowed to Atver, who returned it with exact courtesy. When he came to Figo, he held on a moment longer.

“He’ll be buried properly,” he said, quietly. “On the hill above the village, with the others. The whole town will come. That’s how the Empress wants it done.” He glanced at the shrouds, and back. “I thought you should know that, since I think you’d have wanted to do it yourself.”

“I would have,” Figo said. His eyes were wet. “Thank you.”

“No.” The guard shook his head. “Thank you. All of you. You found my people. That’s more than any of us could do.” He stepped back, drew himself up straight, and brought his fist to his chest in a salute that held every one of them in it. Behind him, the other soldiers did the same.

Then the cage swung out, and the cable took its weight, and the lights began to move.

The five of them stood at the landing’s edge and watched the little constellation of torches slide away across the black gap, smaller and smaller, until it was a single glow, then a spark, then nothing at all. A cold wind came up out of the dark. Somewhere far below, in a village none of them could see, a seven-year-old girl was asleep, and would wake in the morning to news she could not yet imagine.

Nobody said anything. After a while, the island closed in around them again, and the quiet came back.

Night came down on the ridge like a lid.

They did not go back to the nest. Nobody suggested it. They made camp instead in the lee of a shelf of grey rock a short walk from the landing, where the soldiers had left a ring of blackened stones and a stack of dry wood, and for a while the only sounds were small domestic ones: a pot of water set to heat, a bundle of dried meat passed from hand to hand and mostly passed on again. No one told a story. James, who could usually be relied upon to fill any silence with his own noise, said nothing at all. His cask sat unopened beside his knee, and now and then he looked at it the way a man looks at a letter he hasn’t decided to read.

Trista went down to the stream beyond the rocks and stayed there a long time, scrubbing at her robe with handfuls of wet sand. The stain at the hem was stubborn. When she came back it was still there, and she sat down and looked at it, and stopped trying.

Figo lay on his back with Sheela on his chest and both arms wrapped around her, staring up through the canopy at stars he could not see. He didn’t talk to her tonight. Vael noticed that. He only held on.

Atver sat at the edge of the firelight with his book open on his knee and did not turn a page for an hour.

“I’ll take the watch,” Vael said, when the fire had burned down to a bed of red. Nobody had asked him to. “All of it. I won’t sleep anyway.”

Trista looked at him across the coals. For a moment it seemed she might argue. Then she only said, “Wake someone if you need to,” and lay down with her staff beside her and one hand curled around it, as always.

He sat with his back against the grey rock and listened to the island.

It had no night sounds. That was the strangest part. Everywhere else he had camped there had been something: an owl, a rustle, the thin ticking of insects. Here there was only the soft collapse of a coal in the fire and the breathing of the people he trusted more than anyone alive. The Varkador’s long reign had left the dark empty, and the dark had not yet learned how to fill itself again.

Orrin, he thought. Pell. Tamsin. Brenn. Hollis.

He turned the names over like stones in a pocket. It was the one thing he had managed to do right today, and it did not feel like much. He thought of a morning in Skygate, and a gentle voice sitting down beside a small girl, and found he couldn’t think any further than that.

The Ring of Eons sat heavy on his finger. He turned it slowly, feeling the dull ache behind his sternum where the afternoon had been taken out of him. Two blasts. It had been worth it. It hadn’t been enough.

A month, he thought. A month since the sky fell and he woke without a name. He had gathered a great deal since: a mother’s voice in a dream, a row of graves, a ring, three people who argued like family, and a very old elf who never slept. He still did not know what any of it was for.

Sometime after the second hour, Atver closed his book, folded his cloak into a pillow, and lay down on his back with his hands crossed on his chest and his eyes shut. Vael stared. In all the days he had traveled beside the old elf, he had never once seen him do that. He was still staring when the slow, even rhythm of Atver’s breathing settled into the quiet like the ticking of a clock.

The fire went from red to dull orange. Weariness crept up through Vael’s legs and into his spine, patient as water. He straightened, shook his head, pinched the bridge of his nose, and looked up. Grey was beginning to seep into the black beyond the canopy. Soon. Only a little longer.

I’ll close my eyes, he thought. Just for a breath. Just to rest them.

His eyelids had other plans.

In the ferns beyond the firelight, a pair of very large, very dark eyes opened and watched the sleeping camp. Then a second pair. Then a great many.

The sound tore the dawn in half.

Vael was on his feet before he knew he had woken. The Ring of Eons was already burning on his hand, the cold hollow opening beneath his ribs as the ring drew on him. His heart was a hammer. The Dracol, he thought. The flare. It saw. It’s come for us. He swung toward the sky, searching for wings against the thin grey light.

The roar did not come from the sky. It came from close by. It came from the other side of the fire.

He looked around wildly. Trista was sitting bolt upright with her staff gripped in both hands and her hair flattened on one side, wearing the glazed expression of someone dragged out of the deepest sleep of her life. James had managed to lift exactly one eyelid, and was regarding the sky with faint malice. And Atver lay exactly as he had lain down, on his back with his hands crossed on his chest, breathing slowly and evenly, and did not stir. The roar had shaken needles from the trees. It had not so much as ruffled his hem.

The roar had come from Figo.

He stood in the middle of the camp with his shoulders heaving, and he was crying. Vael had seen the big man’s eyes go wet before, at the hollow and at the cart, but always quietly, turned away. This was something else. The tears ran freely down his scarred cheeks and he made no attempt to hide them. It was like watching a mountain weep.

Nobody spoke.

“Figo,” Trista said at last. She was plainly exhausted, and plainly annoyed, and not at all convinced this wasn’t an elaborate joke. “Is this a prank? Because if it is, it isn’t funny.”

Figo didn’t answer. He threw back his head and roared again, louder than before, and a shower of bark and needles drifted down from the branches overhead.

“Figo!” Vael let the ring’s light fade and came around the fire with his voice raised to carry over the echo. “We are exhausted. What was that for?” He put a hand on the big man’s back, between the shoulder-plates, and Figo turned.

Vael took a step back before he could stop himself.

Figo’s eyes were wide open, rimmed in red, the eyes of a man who had lost something so dear that there was no replacing it, and who had only just begun to understand the shape of the hole.

James had rolled upright by then, with enormous effort, and wandered over with the loose-limbed gait of a man still half in a dream. “Hey, big man,” he said. “What’s wrong?” He stopped. He cocked his head and studied Figo’s silhouette up and down with great seriousness, the way one studies a painting that has been hung crooked. “Hmm. Something’s missing.” He snapped his fingers. “Oh! I know! Where’s Sheela?” He looked around. “That thing never comes off you.”

Figo roared.

It was louder than any of the others, a bellow from the soles of his boots, and the trees moved. Somewhere in the grey, something small fell out of a tree with a thump.

Vael and Trista looked at each other. Neither of them had to say it.

“She’s gone.” The words came out of Figo in pieces. His knees gave way, and he went down in the middle of the camp with his arms still curled around nothing, a hollow the exact shape of an axe. He looked down at them as though they belonged to someone else. “I can’t find her. She’s gone. I woke up, and I was holding…”

He pointed, and Vael’s eyes followed the shaking finger to a short, fat log lying where it had been flung at the edge of the ashes. A few strands of fern were still wound around it like ribbon. A single small pink blossom had been tucked into its bark.

“A log,” Figo said. “I slept with a log.”

James’s eyes widened. They went unfocused, and then sharp, the way they did when he looked at a person and the world printed something in small type just beyond everyone else’s sight. Whatever it said, he didn’t like it. The color left his face. It took him two tries to find his voice, and when he did, it cracked on the first word. “Hey. Hey, big man. Don’t worry. We’ll find her.” It was meant to sound reassuring. It sounded, very clearly, like fear.

“What’s the doom and gloom for?”

Atver had finally got curious enough to get up. He came walking slowly into the circle of firelight with his staff in one hand, smoothing a crease from his robe, and regarded the assembled faces with mild interest. Nobody answered. He glanced at Figo, on his knees with his empty hands open in front of him, then at the log, and let out a small, dry laugh.

“Oh,” he said. “Your axe, was it? A great many little Champampas came and took it. I’m afraid I couldn’t stop them.”

Something in the way he said couldn’t suggested he hadn’t tried very hard.

He walked slowly to the edge of the firelight, and the others watched him go, because it seemed impossible not to. He lifted his staff and pointed into the dim green beyond the camp, east and a little north. “It went that way,” he said. “Though I wouldn’t get your hopes up. They are very good at hiding. They had to be. They survived the Varkador’s reign.”

Figo whimpered.

It was a tiny sound, from a very large man. Then he got to his feet, and Vael watched something in his face change from heartbreak to resolve with a speed that would have been alarming in anyone less fond of an axe.

“DAMN CHAMPAMPAS TOOK MY WIFE!”

He was gone before the last word had finished echoing, crashing off between the trees in a straight line, trampling ferns, bellowing.

James, in spite of himself, let out a snort of laughter. It was a small one. He tried to swallow it. It escaped anyway, in a cracked wheeze, and he pressed his sleeve over his mouth and failed to do anything about it. Trista, who had slept perhaps three hours in two days, gave him a long, flat look.

“Let’s follow him,” James managed, when he could, “before he makes the whole island fall.”

“Yes,” said Trista, with feeling. “Let’s.”

Vael was already pulling on his boots. As he did, the thought arrived, quite clear and quite cold, that this had happened on his watch. He had fallen asleep. Someone had walked into the middle of the camp and taken the thing Figo loved most in the world from between his arms, and Vael had slept through it. He tightened the last lace harder than he needed to and promised himself it would not happen again.

He glanced back once. Atver had settled onto his folded cloak again and opened his book to the page he had marked, and did not so much as look up. It was not his fight. Vael had the uneasy feeling that a man who had been sound asleep should not have known which way the thieves went, but there was no time to ask.

It wasn’t difficult to follow Figo. He was shouting the whole way.

“SHEELA! SHEELAAA… huh… huhuhu… SHEEEELAAAA!”

He called to her through the trees as though she could hear him, his voice cracking on the long vowels and ringing off the stones. No birds burst from the branches. There were none left to burst. The first grey light was seeping through the canopy, laying long pale bars across the forest floor. Vael ran. James ran beside him, swearing softly. Trista brought up the rear with her robes gathered in one fist, saying “Figo, slow down,” at intervals, to no one.

They found him a quarter of a mile on, and he was on the ground.

He had caught his boot against a rock the size of a loaf and gone down on his hands and knees in the ferns, and he had stayed there, exhausted and heartsick, hauling air back into his lungs with his forehead nearly in the dirt. Vael slowed, ready to help him up.

And then he saw why Figo had gone still.

There was a face a few inches from Figo’s own. It was small, and pink, and enormously interested.

“HUAH!” said Figo, with the full dignity of a man startled out of his own grief.

The face vanished. There was a flicker of rose-colored fur and a thrashing in the nearest fern, and then nothing.

Figo stared at the bush. A great change came over him, slowly, as the thought assembled in his head. A lead. This was a lead. This was the only lead he had.

He lowered himself onto his belly and crawled toward the fern on his elbows, with enormous care, in the way of a man who has decided to be as gentle as he possibly can, which is to say the way a landslide is gentle. It was at once the most and the least menacing thing Vael had ever seen. Behind him, James put his hand over his own mouth. Trista shut her eyes.

“Hey,” Figo whispered, in what he honestly believed was a whisper. “Hey. Little thing.”

The fern shivered. A pair of enormous dark eyes appeared between the fronds, took in the scarred face, the dented armor, and the large, unsteady hand held out flat in the dirt, and withdrew again. But not all the way. A small, round ear stayed visible. Something was willing to give him a chance.

“I’m… I’m looking for my wife,” Figo told the fern, his voice rough. “Have you seen her? I really need her back. I need to save the world, you see, and without my wife I’m nothing. She’s like… she’s the… the peanut and butter? No, no. There was a better word. Hold on…”

“HEY, FIGO! YOU ALRIGHT?!”

Vael hadn’t meant to shout. It was only that he’d been unable to hold in the relief any longer, and he wanted, badly, to be sure the big man could hear him. The effect, however, was instant: the ear vanished into the bushes with a rustle like a dropped handful of leaves, and Figo’s head whipped around with an expression of absolute betrayal.

“SHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHH!” said Figo, at a volume that could have stripped paint. He pressed a finger to his lips and glared at all of them in turn. “You guys ruin everything. I found a Champampa! It’s our lead! Now crouch down, all of you, and lower your voices!”

They crouched. There was a certain gravity to the way the three of them did it. In a moment of weakness, Trista even hid her staff behind her back.

It took a long time, in the silence, but at last the ferns parted again.

The creature came out slowly, one careful hand at a time, and in the grey light Vael got his first proper look at it. It was a little larger than a squirrel, and it was the pink of the inside of a seashell: soft rose fur over a cream belly, darker around the eyes in a smudged mask like a bandit’s, with ears so large and round they looked borrowed from something twice its size. Its eyes were enormous, wet and black as river stones, and a good deal too intelligent. Its tail was long and prehensile, carried over its back in a curl like a question mark, tufted white at the tip. But it was the hands that Vael kept looking at: small, four-fingered, the pads calloused and clever, the hands of something that had spent its whole life hauling loads alongside a hundred others. Around its middle was a belt of braided grass, and tucked into it, like a tool bag, were a thorn, a sliver of flint, and a tiny coil of cord.

It sat on a root and tilted its head at the four enormous strangers. Its nose twitched. And Vael had the distinct and uncomfortable sense that it was making a judgment, and that they were not winning.

“HEY, LITTLE GUY!” Figo said.

The creature was gone again before the word had finished.

“Aww, man,” said Figo.

“You’re too loud, Figo,” Trista said, and she said it gently. She came forward on her knees and laid a hand on his arm. “Take a deep breath. Let it out slowly. Then call it lightly. As if you were talking to a bird.”

Figo took the biggest breath Vael had ever seen a person take. His chest swelled until the straps of his armor creaked. He held it. He let it out in a long, shuddering, almost entirely silent exhale, and put his hand flat on the ground.

And the creature, miraculously, came back.

It stopped just out of reach. It looked at him for a long moment. And Figo, in a voice so strained from the effort of being quiet that his neck had gone red, said, “Oh. Hey. Hey, little buddy. Could you help me find my wife, please? She’s been missing all night. I heard your buddies took her from me.”

It was the quietest thing Vael had ever heard come out of him. The effort showed in every line of his body: a man who was simply not built for softness, and who would have moved the world to be soft for this. Behind him, James had lowered his hand from his mouth. Trista’s eyes were suspiciously bright.

The creature let out a small sigh, a breath through the nose, like someone resigned to a long afternoon. Then it climbed up Figo’s arm, scrambled to his shoulder, and settled against his ear.

“QUEEEEEEEE!”

The sound that came out of it was impossible. It was a little larger than a squirrel and it screamed like a kettle. Figo flinched so hard his teeth rattled. And then, after a stunned moment, he began to laugh, helplessly, in great wet gasps.

“THERE you are! There you are, little man! Okay. Okay.” He lifted a finger, as slowly as he could, and the creature allowed it to stroke the top of its head. “I’m gonna call you… Little.”

“Awww,” said Trista.

She hadn’t meant to. It came out of her before she could stop it, and she stopped, and thought about what she had just heard. ”…You really are bad with names, Figo,” she said, and for the first time in a very long while, she laughed. It was a short, startled thing, but it was real.

James had already got to his feet. He stood a little apart, wearing the expression of a man who had read something in the air and found it, for once, to his liking. “Our lead, lads,” he said, to nobody in particular.

“So.” Figo cleared his throat. He looked at the thing on his shoulder, then at the ground, then back at it. “Um. Uh. Little. Can you show us the way?”

“Que!” said Little. “Que que que, queeeeee!”

She leaped from his shoulder and shot away between the ferns, calling back over her shoulder in a stream of high, urgent squeaks, plainly telling them to hurry.

“WAIT UP!” Figo was already running.

“That thing is fast,” James panted, and the rest of them pelted after.

She led them at a pace that made no allowances.

Over roots, under a fallen trunk that Figo cleared by simply going through the lowest branches, across a stream that Little took in one leap and the rest of them took in a floundering scatter, up a bank of loose stone and down the other side. It was several minutes before Vael understood what he was following. The ferns were thick, but there was a way through them: a thin pale thread of packed earth no wider than his hand, winding between the trunks. At every turn stood a tiny cairn of stacked pebbles, each one topped with a single pink petal. It wasn’t a trail. It was a road. It had simply been built for a different size of traveler.

Every so often a flash of rose-colored fur and a curl of white-tipped tail would appear on the next rise, wait, flick once in plain exasperation, and vanish again.

By the time the trees thinned, the sun was fully up, and the four of them were breathing hard enough to have lost the right to complain. James had stopped talking altogether, which was how Vael knew he was suffering.

The road ended at the foot of a broken shelf of grey rock, where the island’s bones came up through its skin.

A cave mouth waited there, so low and so thickly curtained with moss and hanging roots that it might have been mistaken for a crack where two stones had failed to meet. Only when Vael came close did he see the work in it. A staircase climbed the left side of the arch, each step no bigger than a thumb, to a shelf where pebbles had been set in neat concentric rings. Pink petals lay scattered across the threshold, laid down rather than dropped. And out of the dark came a thin, steady breath of air that smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke and, strangely, of dried fruit.

Little did not so much as slow. She went through the arch like a coin through a slot.

“WAIT UP, LITTLE!”

Figo, who had never in his life learned to stop, hit the arch at full speed. It was waist-high. There was a scrape, a grunt, the long metallic shriek of a pauldron dragging along stone, and then he was inside, having apparently been poured through the gap like grain through a funnel.

James looked at the opening. He looked at his own shoulders. “I’m not saying we should hesitate,” he said, “but that is a hole.”

“It’s an invitation,” said Trista, and got down on her knees in her ruined white robe, and went in.

After a moment, James followed, muttering. Vael was last. He glanced once at the island’s golden light, and at the green, and at the quiet that was beginning, at last, to sound like a place that might fill itself again. Then he ducked into the dark.

The passage swallowed the daylight in ten strides. After that there was only the sound of Figo ahead of them, crashing from wall to wall like a bull in a sack, and the close warm air, and the smell of smoke.

Then, from somewhere ahead, small and polite and questioning: “Queee?”

Another answered it from the left. “Que?” A third, from above. “Que ueee.” Then they were everywhere at once, a rising, rippling chorus passed from one throat to the next like a message relayed down a line, queeue, queeue, que que que, until the dark itself seemed to be whispering.

“Figo,” Trista said, very quietly. “Don’t move.”

“I’m not,” said Figo, from somewhere in the black. “I’m really not.”

Then someone struck a light.

It was a spark first. Then a single flame no larger than a thumbnail, steady as a candle in a still room, cupped in a pair of small hands. A second bloomed beside it, then a third, then dozens, hundreds, spilling outward across the dark in a slow tide until the whole cavern stood revealed in a warm, trembling gold. They weren’t burning anything. The flames simply sat in the little palms as though they had grown there.

It was a hall. The roof was lost in shadow, and the walls climbed away in terraces cut, shaped, and stacked by countless small hands into something between a beehive and an amphitheater. Bridges of braided grass, fine as hair, spanned the gaps between ledges. Pulleys of knotted cord creaked above baskets of dried fruit and fungus and seed. Doors no taller than Vael’s forearm were set into the walls, and out of every one of them, and from every terrace, peered rose-colored faces with enormous dark eyes. Mothers with kits clinging to their fur. Elders with silvered muzzles. Young ones peeping from behind the legs of those older than they were.

Hundreds of them. More than Vael could count.

No one moved. A hundred flames and a thousand eyes were fixed on the four enormous strangers standing in their house.

“That’s…” James said slowly. “That’s a lot of them.”

“They were down here the whole time,” Trista murmured. “Hiding. From that bird.” She looked at a mother near the front, thin to the bone, holding a kit against her chest, and her face softened. “No wonder the island was so quiet. Everyone who could was underground.”

“We helped them,” said Figo.

He stood in the middle of the floor with his empty hands hanging at his sides, a picture of wounded bafflement. “We killed the thing. And they took my wife?!”

He stopped. His head came up. His nostrils flared once, twice, and then he began to sniff the air with the deep, wet, thorough snuffle of a hound on a scent. “Fmfff. Fmfffffff.” Vael watched the expression on his face change. “I can smell her,” Figo whispered. “Whetstone oil. Cold iron. The honey-wax I put on her haft. She’s here. She’s near.”

He turned in a slow circle, and the Champampas parted before him like water. Then he stopped.

On a shelf of rock at the far end of the cavern, higher than the rest, a ring of small flames burned around a heap of pink blossoms. And in the heap, laid carefully on her side with her haft toward the room, lay Sheela. Her blade gave back the firelight in a clean gold line. Someone had washed the Varkador from her edge. It was, Vael suspected, more care than she had received in weeks.

“SHEELAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAA!”

The whole cave shook.

A fine rain of grit fell from the roof. Along every terrace, a thousand small bodies flinched as one, and a stone the size of a fist cracked into the floor a stride from Trista’s boot.

“WOAH, WOAH, WOAH!” James had both hands up as though he could hold the ceiling in place. “Big man! Big man, we can’t be doing that here! This place could come down on top of us!”

Figo clapped both hands over his own mouth. His eyes, above them, were enormous.

In the settling dust, a single figure stepped forward from the crowd beside the shrine.

He was the biggest Champampa Vael had seen, which was to say about the size of a house cat, and he carried it the way a king carries a coronation. His rose fur had faded to silver at the muzzle and ruff. One ear was notched. His belt of braided grass was hung with more cord and flint and thorn than anyone else’s. And on his head, held in a circlet of woven stems, stood a single long feather of deep red, barred in black, taller than the creature who wore it.

Vael knew it at once. He had stood beneath that feather only the day before, when it crowned a beast the size of a horse. So that’s where it went. The tip of it swept the stones as the old Champampa bowed.

“Que,” he said.

It was a single syllable, and it was not like any other sound that had been made in that cave: low and measured and utterly self-possessed, a sound that had learned its manners in a court. It was, Vael thought, the most royal thing he had ever heard come out of a body that size.

Figo didn’t care. He took a long step toward the shrine, and then another, and he would have taken a third if Little hadn’t thrown herself between him and the dais.

She stood on the stone in front of his boot with her arms spread wide and her tail lashing, one very small creature in the path of one very large man, and shook her head so hard her ears flapped. “Que,” she said. “Que que.” She pointed with her whole arm at the old Champampa with the feather.

Figo stopped. He looked down at her. He looked at the king.

“Que,” the king said again, a single elegant word. Then he lifted his chin and said something longer. “Que que que… que!”

And all down the terraces, in a rustling wave that rolled from the highest ledge to the floor, the Champampas bowed.

Hundreds of them, in unison, small hands pressed to their chests, the flames in their palms dipping as one. Vael had spent a month learning that intelligence came in shapes that did not look like it. These were not animals gathered in a hole. They were a people, and the four of them were standing in their house, shouting.

“Bow,” he said quietly. “All of you. Maybe they’ll help us.”

They bowed. Trista with a gracious dip of her head, James with a flourish and a muttered “your lordship,” Vael from the waist. And Figo bowed the hardest of all, so low that his forehead nearly touched the stone, and when he spoke his voice was a thing Vael would not have believed he owned: small, steady, scraped almost raw with the effort of being gentle.

“Please,” he said to the king. “Give me my wife back.”

A silence. Then the king lifted one paw.

“Que…”

A dozen Champampas stepped forward from the shrine and hoisted Sheela between them, shoulders under her haft like pallbearers, and began to carry her across the floor in a slow, rocking procession, chanting a soft que, que, que in time with their steps. They brought her to the king. He considered her. He considered Figo.

“Que?” he said.

It was a question. It was a clear and elegant one.

“Yes,” said Figo. “Yes, yes, that’s her. That’s mine. Please. Thank you.” He raised his hand, palm up, to receive her.

The bearers, who had been carrying her very correctly, shifted the haft from their shoulders to their hands, lifted, and turned. The great flat of the axe-head came around in a long, smooth arc and met Figo squarely on the side of the head.

“TAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAA.”

It rang like an iron bucket dropped down a well. Dust shook from the ceiling. In the silence afterward, one of the bearers sank to her knees and covered her eyes.

Figo’s eyes crossed. He swayed. He sat down very slowly, with the careful motion of a man who had decided the floor would be safer, and he put out his hands, and the bearers, mortified, passed Sheela over haft-first. He gathered her in. He pressed his cheek to her flat, the same flat, and shut his eyes.

From the dais came a short string of sounds, grave and almost kindly. “Que. Que que.”

Figo nodded, with his eyes still closed.

“Alright,” he said after a while, thickly. “Alright, alright. I’ll take good care of her. I promise.”

He wasn’t talking to the room. He was answering someone. Vael felt the hair rise along his arms.

“Oh,” said Figo. His eyes opened. “You know a way out?”

Trista and James looked at each other. Vael looked at Figo.

“Oh, it’s… that way?” He twisted and pointed past the shrine to a dark opening in the far wall, half hidden by a curtain of woven grass. “Thank you. Thank you!” A pause. Figo cleared his throat, with the air of a man who has just been struck by his own wife and is determined not to let it show. “If I may ask, your majesty… why didn’t you just call us here?”

The king answered at some length. “Que que… que que que.” It had a rhythm to it, a patient, reasonable, faintly pained rhythm, the rhythm of someone explaining a thing that should not have needed explaining. One of the elders nodded gravely. A few of the younger ones put their paws over their mouths.

Figo thought about it.

“Good point,” he said.

He got to his feet, tucked Sheela against his shoulder, and turned round beaming to the others, and found three faces looking back at him in blank, polite bewilderment.

“What?” he said. “What’s wrong?”

“Figo,” said Trista. “Did you just understand that?”

“Of course I…” He stopped. He looked at the king. He looked at the hundreds of bright faces. “Wait. You can’t understand them?”

Nobody said anything. Somewhere on the third terrace, a very small Champampa sneezed.

“Oh,” said Figo. There was a long silence. “Oh.” The expression that crossed his face was a slow and deeply private wonder. “Huh.” Then he turned back to them, grinning. “Good news! There’s a way down. It’s just around the corner. Little’s going to take us. Apparently she’s their ambassador, to the, uh, big folk, which as far as I can tell is us.” Little drew herself up to her full, considerable height. “We should pack up and get Atver.”

Vael leaned toward James and lowered his voice. “That was a hard bonk. Is he all right?”

“Big man’s taken harder,” James murmured back. “He already had a few screws loose.”

“Guys,” said Trista, in a tone that stopped them both.

So the crew agreed to leave it there, and go back for Atver.

They found Atver exactly where they had left him, on his folded cloak with the book open on his knee, as though he hadn’t moved in the hour they had been gone. Perhaps he hadn’t. He looked up as they came crashing out of the ferns, took in the four of them and the small pink passenger riding Figo’s shoulder, and let his gaze settle on the axe.

“Ah,” he said. “The axe.”

“Her name is Sheela,” said Figo, with enormous dignity.

“Of course. Forgive me.” Atver inclined his head. “Her.”

They told him all of it, in the tumbling, overlapping way of people who each remember a different half: the road of pebbles, the dark, the hundred small lights, the king with the feather, the bonk. At the feather, Atver’s hand went still on his book. At the bonk, his mouth twitched. At a way down, he closed the book entirely, which was how Vael knew it was serious.

“A colony,” Atver said. “An organized, tool-using colony, with fire, and a hereditary authority, hidden beneath a Varkador’s nesting ground for the entire length of its reign. And a way down.” He rose, brushed off his robe, and for a moment looked less like a scholar than like a boy on the morning of a festival. “Child, do you have any idea how long I have wanted to see a Champampa court?”

“You said they were shy,” Trista said.

“I said they were said to be shy. Nobody had ever written down what they were like when they weren’t.” He shouldered his pack in a single motion. Vael had not seen him pack. Vael was fairly sure he had not packed.

Little, who had been regarding the old elf from Figo’s shoulder with an expression of profound suspicion, said, “Que.”

“She says she’s not a specimen, and you’re not to poke her,” Figo translated.

“Naturally,” said Atver. “Ambassador.” He bowed to her precisely as far as he would have to a head of state, and Little, after a moment’s consideration, bowed back.

This time the cavern was not dark with fear. It was a procession.

The Champampas lined the terraces, the bridges, the floor on either side of the path, and as the five giants passed, every one of them lifted a flame in cupped hands, so that the way ahead was a river of tiny lights. Little went first, tail up like a banner, and led them through the curtain of woven grass in the far wall.

The passage beyond was wider than the entrance had been, though still low enough that Figo walked bent double with Sheela carried like a sleeping child. It sloped down, and then down again, winding through the island’s flesh. The walls here were carved.

Not roughly. With small, deft, patient scratches, row on row, from the floor to as high as a Champampa could reach standing on another’s shoulders. Vael slowed to look. A star fell. A hand’s breadth on, the ground tore upward toward it, and little figures clung to the roots of a tree as the world rose beneath them. Then came a long stretch of wall with nothing on it but a single wide shadow wearing a crest, and under it a hundred tiny figures retreating, single file, into the dark. And at the end, at about the height of Vael’s shoulder, were the newest scratches of all, so fresh their edges were still white: four tall figures and one short robed one, standing in a clearing over a fallen bird. One of them carried an axe.

The axe was drawn in loving detail, down to the notch near the heel of its blade. It was much too exact to have been drawn from memory, in the dark, in a single night. Vael looked at it, and thought of a ring of small flames, and a bed of pink petals, and a blade washed clean, and felt something shift quietly in his understanding of the morning. He decided not to mention it. Figo was holding Sheela very close, and had enough to forgive.

Beyond the carvings the air thickened and warmed. The floor began to feel springy underfoot, as though each step were landing a little later than it should. Pebbles on the tunnel floor had lifted a hand’s width off the ground and hung there, turning gently. Dust floated in the lamplight in lazy gold ribbons. James tried to lean on the wall, missed it, and drifted three feet sideways before he found his feet again.

“Ah,” Atver said softly. His monocle had turned the color of a clear morning sky and was spinning. “We are close.”

The tunnel ended without warning, the way caves do: one moment a low rough roof, and the next, nothing above them but sky.

They stepped out onto a shelf of rock that jutted from the island’s flank like a lower lip, thirty feet across, ringed by a low wall of stacked stones. Beyond the wall there was nothing at all.

For a moment Vael simply stood there and let it have him.

The island’s side fell away beneath the ledge in a curtain of dark basalt hung with roots and ropes of flowering vine, and then the rock curved inward and was gone, and there was only air. Far below lay the floor of the clouds, a vast slow sea of white shot through with gold where the morning struck it, and through its tears he could see the land: patched, tiny, tilted, the color of old bruises. To the north a jet stream tore past at the height of the islands, a white river of cloud racing east. Other islands hung in the haze, each trailing thin waterfalls that turned to mist before they ever reached the ground.

And on the far horizon, east, where the morning was brightest, lay a long dark line that was not cloud.

Figo, who had walked out onto the ledge before he thought about where he was, took one look at the drop, went three paces backward very fast, and sat down against the cave wall with his eyes screwed shut.

Above the ledge, the island’s overhang leaned out like a roof. And from a crack in that roof, a pillar of light was falling.

There was no other word for it. It fell from the stone and out over the lip of the ledge in a column thirty feet across, pale and gold and faintly shimmering, like heat over a summer road, and it went on falling, down past the edge of the cloud-floor and through it, until the eye lost it somewhere far below. Motes drifted in it. Petals. A single grey feather, turning so slowly that it seemed to hang. It was the most beautiful thing Vael had ever seen, and he understood, with a sinking in his stomach, exactly what they were going to be asked to do.

“Oh,” James said faintly. “We’re jumping into that.”

“Do you remember what I told you about the orb beneath the first island?” Atver had not looked away from the roof. “That it has convinced the space around itself that gravity doesn’t apply?”

“An argument with the world,” said Vael. “And it’s winning.”

“For now.” Atver raised the monocle toward the crack, and the lens flushed a clear, running gold. Vael saw it then: a bead of dull light wedged in the stone, no bigger than a fist, and below it, pouring down from it, a thousand fine bright lines, drawn straight as rain. “This is its younger sibling. A smaller fragment, bonded to the rock above us by the same blow that raised the island. It cannot lift a mountain, so it does what it can. It has persuaded the air beneath it to forget. A pillar of forgetting, child, falling from this roof to, quite possibly, the ground.” He sounded delighted. “The Champampas have been using it as a stair.”

He bent, picked up a pebble, and tossed it at the edge of the column. It crossed the shimmering boundary and slowed as though the air had thickened to syrup, hung, turned gently, and began to sink, as lazily as a seed on a breeze. Atver tossed a second pebble a yard to the left. It dropped out of sight with a thin falling whistle, and did not, so far as anyone heard, land.

“Stay in the middle,” he said. “The edges are where the argument thins. Step out of the column and the world will remember you at once, and in some detail.”

“It’s your spell,” Trista said slowly. “Let gravity forget.”

“A distant cousin of it. Mine is a request. This is an argument that has already been won, which is why it holds so steadily, and why I would not trust it a day longer than I must.” He lowered the monocle. “It has held for a month. That is not a long time.”

“How much longer will it hold?” said James.

“Until it doesn’t.”

“Atver.”

“Longer than we need, in all likelihood.”

“In all likelihood,” James repeated, and then, without seeming to decide to, he looked at Figo.

His eyes went unfocused, the way they did, and for a long moment he stood very still. Then his shoulders came down a full inch. “You land fine, big man,” he said quietly. “All of you do. I’ve never seen it come out so clean.” It was, Vael realized, the first time he had heard James say something good about the future without a joke attached.

Figo did not open his eyes. “It’s all sky,” he said, very small. “James. It’s all sky.”

“It’s a floor made of light, big man.”

“It’s not a floor.”

“No,” James admitted. “It isn’t.”

Something tugged at Figo’s ear.

He opened one eye. Little was sitting on his shoulder, small and rose-colored against the enormous gold of the morning, holding a pinch of his ear in both hands, and she looked at him for a long moment. Then she hopped down onto his knee, and onto the stone beside it, and walked a little way along the ledge toward the lip and looked back, the way you might look back at a friend who is taking too long on a stair.

“This is where you go back,” Figo said. “Isn’t it.”

“Que.” It was softer than any sound she had made in the forest.

He nodded. He got to his feet in sections, the way a cliff might decide to stand, and walked to her with his eyes on the stone, well back from the edge, and knelt until his face was level with hers. The wind pulled at his hair. For a moment his mouth worked and nothing came out. When it did, it came out in the quietest voice Vael had ever heard from him, and this time there was no strain in it at all.

“I was loud,” he said. “I woke up your whole house. I shook your ceiling. I probably frightened the little ones.” He drew a long, unsteady breath. “And you still came and found me. You stood in front of me. You’re about the size of my boot, and a man the size of me was coming at your king, and you stood there with your arms out and said no.” His voice cracked, and he let it. “I’ve been afraid of big things my whole life, Little. Where we’re going, there’s a very big thing. I want to be like that, when I get down there. I want to stand in front of something and not move.”

Little looked at him for a long moment out of her enormous, wet, too-intelligent eyes. Then she stood up on the stone, put both arms up as high as they would go, and said, with great firmness: “Que.”

Figo didn’t need a translator for that one.

He held out his hand, palm up, and she climbed onto it, and up the scarred length of his forearm, and onto his shoulder, and then crossed the last few inches in a leap and landed against the side of his face, her arms flung around as much of his jaw as she could reach, her tail wrapped twice around his ear.

For a moment Figo didn’t move at all.

Then, very carefully, he curled one enormous hand up around her back, a hand that had held Sheela through a hundred fights, and held her the way you might hold a soap bubble. It was a hug in which one party was the size of a barn door and the other the size of a loaf of bread, and the barn door took extreme care. His face crumpled around her. Tears ran down the scars again, and they were not the ones from the morning.

“QUEEEEEE,” said Little, directly into his ear, and for once he didn’t flinch. He laughed instead, wetly, helplessly.

“I know,” he said. “I know. Thank you. Thank you for all of it.” He sniffed. “I’ll try to be quieter.” A pause. “No, I won’t. But I’ll try.”

She patted his cheek, twice, and slid down his arm. She trotted over to where Sheela leaned against the wall and did something Vael wouldn’t have thought a creature her size could do: she unwound a length of braided grass from her belt, climbed the haft, and tied it just below the axe-head in a small, neat, many-looped knot. “Que que,” she said, patting it.

“She says,” Figo translated, thickly, “it’s so we know the way back.”

One by one, the others said goodbye.

Trista knelt and touched two fingers to the top of Little’s head, and a thread of silver light, small and warm, went from her hand into the rose-colored fur. Little blinked and put a paw to her own head, wondering. “A blessing,” Trista said, smiling through tears. “For the road home.” James bowed so low his coat swept the stone. “Ambassador,” he said. “It was a pleasure doing business.” Atver inclined his head precisely as far as he would have for a head of state. “Tell His Majesty,” he said, “that he has my thanks, and a great deal of my professional interest.” And Vael, who did not know what to say and so said the true thing, knelt on the stone and said, “Thank you for trusting us.”

Little received each of them with a gravity that would have suited an envoy of any court. Then she went back to the mouth of the tunnel, where the king and a hundred small lit faces had gathered in the shadow of the rock, and turned, and put her hand to her chest.

All of them did.

It was Figo who stepped into the column first.

Nobody had asked him to. He tucked Sheela under one arm, shut his eyes, took a long breath, and stepped off the edge of the world.

He didn’t scream.

For a long moment nobody did anything. Then his voice came back up, thin and wondering, from just below the lip.

“Oh,” said Figo. “Oh, it’s soft.”

Trista went next, with her staff hooked through her elbow, and then James, muttering something about the cask, and then Atver, who stepped into the light as though entering a library. Vael was last. He looked back once. The ledge behind him was empty, and at the mouth of the tunnel, very small, a rose-colored figure had climbed onto the wall and raised one hand.

He raised his own.

Then he stepped off the edge.

It was like being lowered into warm water.

The first thing Vael felt was his stomach, which rose and settled again with a small, cheerful lurch. The second was the quiet. Inside the column the air was still, and faintly golden, and thick with slow-turning motes that hung about him like the first snow of a very gentle winter. He looked up once. The ledge was drifting upward and away, and on the wall of it a speck of pink still stood with one hand raised, a hundred small flames dipping behind her. Then the overhang slid across his sight, and they were gone.

They fell in a loose cluster, five people drifting down through a river of light at about the pace of a falling leaf. Trista let her arms drift out to either side, laughing under her breath. James lay flat on his back with his cask on his chest, like a man on a summer lake. Atver sat cross-legged in the air with his book open on his knee. And Figo had curled around Sheela into a tight, trembling ball and shut his eyes so hard that the lids creased.

“It’s all right, big man,” James said, drifting past him on his back. “Nothing’s going to happen. I’ve checked.”

“Don’t look at me,” Figo said through his teeth. “Talk about something. Anything. Please.”

Trista caught his hand and held it. “Atver,” she said. “Talk.”

“About what?”

“Anything. Something long.”

Atver turned a page. “I rarely have an audience that asks.”

“Then I’m asking,” said Vael, and found, as he said it, that he had been wanting to for days. “I don’t know anything about the Imperium. Everyone talks about it as if it were the weather. Tell us what we’re walking into.”

Atver closed the book with visible regret. “You’ll want to look, then,” he said. “It will make more sense with the view.”

The island’s belly slid past on their left, vast and slow and dripping.

It was a country of its own, an upside-down land of black stone and tangled root, hung with waterfalls. And set deep into the rock at its heart, like a stone in a ring, pulsed the orb. It was larger than a cart, a fat, slow, faintly flickering bead of pale light, with the threads of the field that held the whole mountain up streaming away from it in every direction. It looked like a growth that had decided to stay.

“There,” Atver breathed, in the voice of a man greeting an old friend. “Do you see it flicker?”

“Please don’t remind me what it’s doing,” said James.

Then the island was behind them, and the world opened.

Above, it dwindled to a hanging hill. Below lay the floor of the clouds, dazzling now. And ahead, far out across the white, where the morning was brightest, the long dark line on the horizon had grown teeth.

“There,” said Atver. “The Molten Imperium.”

The name came out of him strangely flat, like a word he had said many times and never liked.

“I have read everything about it that survives, which is less than you would think, and most of that was written by the people who ran it. So I will tell you what I know for certain. For more than seven thousand years, it was the largest thing in this half of the world. Not the oldest. Not the wisest, and I will happily argue the point with anyone who says otherwise. But the largest, and by a distance.”

“Seven thousand years,” James said, to the sky. “I can’t keep a tavern tab open seven days.”

“It began, as these things do, with a very good idea. The east is full of fire, child. Under its skin lie great ranges of old volcanoes, the whole interior of it. Somebody, long ago, worked out how to drink from them: the heat, the pressure, the slow endless pushing of the earth. They learned to pour it into iron, and the iron learned to move.” He turned a hand in the air, as though showing them something small. “Constructs. Engines that did not tire, did not ask for wages, did not ask questions. They built their cities around those engines, the way other people build cities around a river.”

“And then they conquered with them,” said Trista, her hand still wrapped around Figo’s.

“They advanced with them. There is a difference, to the men who kept the ledgers. The Imperium never wanted gold. It never wanted glory. The word its own scholars used was unification. It believed it had been charged to bring the whole world under a single roof, and it believed that, I am sorry to say, quite sincerely. It said the charge had come from Rhaegor, the warden of the western volcanoes. No record exists of Rhaegor saying any such thing, and its founders never set foot in his mountains.” Atver’s mouth thinned. “They built a faith on a sentence nobody can find.”

“That sounds,” said Trista slowly, “like the sort of thing people believe when they need to.”

“Mm,” said Atver. “Yes.”

The cloud floor rose to meet them. It took them in with a cold, wet hush, and the light went white, then pearl, then nothing at all, and from somewhere in the middle of it came a small strangled noise that was Figo.

“It’s only fog,” Trista said. “Only fog, Figo. Keep your eyes shut and keep listening.”

“I’m listening,” he whispered. “Atver. Keep talking.”

So Atver talked, and in the white, with nothing to look at, his dry unhurried voice was all there was.

The Imperium had marched south, he said, century on century, until it met a people who had no engines at all. No siege-works. No standing army. What they had was distance, and the willingness to retreat, and so they retreated: they gave up their lowlands, and then their foothills, and then their valleys, until there was ice at their backs and nothing else. The constructs that levelled their homeland had not even been built to destroy it. They were built to flatten ground for roads and supply lines, and the Tundra people happened to be standing on it.

“The Imperium’s administrators,” said Atver, “called it a logistical side effect.”

Nobody answered. Beside Vael, in the white, Figo’s breathing had slowed. He was listening the way a man listens to a story that is not about him, which is its own kind of mercy.

They came out of the bottom of the cloud all at once, into light so clear it hurt.

The ground below was grey.

It was not the grey of the dead forests behind them. This was a deeper, older grey: ash, lying in long smooth drifts across a plain so wide that the curve of the earth seemed to bend it, shaped by the wind into dunes like a frozen sea. Old riverbeds ran through it, pale scars with a line of rust at the bottom. Roads crossed it, black and straight and level, ruled across the ash like lines in a ledger. And at the edge of sight rose a ring of mountains, low, dark, perfectly round, each one wearing a collar of red iron that caught the sun and held it, so that the whole horizon seemed strung with a necklace of small fires.

From each collar, pipes as thick as towers ran down the slopes and into the plain, and from this height every mountain looked like a tree standing with its roots in the air.

“The forges drank from those,” Atver said. “A month ago, you could not have heard yourself think on that plain. It was the loudest place in the world.”

It was silent now. The silence came up off it like cold from a cellar. There was not a bird. There was not a blade of grass. Vael had thought the Varkador’s island was quiet, and he saw now that it had only been waiting. That silence had been the held breath before a story. This one was finished.

“It doesn’t look like much from here,” said James.

“That is the mercy of distance. Look at the middle of the ring.”

Vael looked. Where the haze thickened over the heart of the bowl, rising from a point he couldn’t make out, stood a single thread of smoke, thin as a hair at this distance, climbing straight into the still air and bending at the top where the wind took it.

“A land that once smoked from a thousand chimneys,” Atver said quietly. “And now that.”

“The Dracol,” said Trista.

“Its breath. Or the cooling of whatever it has set alight.”

“And how does something that size end?” Vael asked. He was still watching the thread. “Whatever it was, it’s dead.”

“Your sky fell.”

Vael turned his head.

“The Starfall, child. It did not strike them. It struck the world, and the world is all one body.” Atver was looking at the smoke, not at him. “For centuries the Imperium had been quietly drinking from something older than itself: a thread of power running beneath the world, a kind of relay, which carried its orders from city to city and from mind to mind, so that ten thousand machines could think one thought at the same moment. When the sky came down, the thread snapped. And every engine in the Imperium stopped, mid-stride, on the same morning. The forges went out. The vaults sealed themselves. Seven thousand years of the loudest thing in the world, and then nothing.”

“And the people?” Trista asked. “The ones who lived there?”

“Fewer than you would think. That was the cruelty of it. The Imperium never needed a great many. The machines did the work. The people it kept were its clerks and its overseers and its priests, and on the morning the machines stopped, they were the ones with nothing left to do. Some will have fled. Some will not have managed it.” He paused. “I would not expect to meet anyone.”

Vael thought of a vault beneath Skygate, and of a woman who had said our old ground as though she could still feel it under her boots. He kept it behind his teeth. It was not his to give away.

“I will not pretend I mourn it,” said Atver. “But I want you to understand what we are walking into, because it is not harmless. An empire is an appetite, child, like the bird. It grows to meet its hunger, and its hunger eats its way down to the foundations. It ate its own land to the bone, and then its neighbors’ land, and it was still hungry on the morning it stopped.” He looked at the thread of smoke. “And now something else has come to eat what is left.”

“Dracols feed on ruin,” Trista said quietly. “They’re born in dead volcanoes, and they live on destruction. Of course it went there.”

“It went to the largest feast in the world,” said Atver, “and found the table set.”

“But it’s not at the edges.” Vael frowned. “It’s in the middle.”

“A wounded thing that wishes to be left alone does not lie in the suburbs. It goes to the place that mattered.” Atver lifted a hand, and his finger drifted a little in the still air as he pointed at the root of the smoke. “The Ashen Seat. The capital. At its heart stands a throne of volcanic rock, tall as a watchtower. Nobody ever sat on it. It was made to be seen: a promise to the whole world that this was where it would be brought to heel.” He was quiet a moment. “There are vaults beneath it, too. Sealed. They closed themselves when the relay died. I should very much like them to stay that way.”

“You think it’s on the throne,” said James.

“I think,” said Atver, “that if I were hurt, and proud, and wished to be seen, that is where I would lie.”

“How tall is a watchtower?” Figo asked, to nobody, with his eyes still shut.

“Taller than you would like.”

“Hm,” said Figo. “Okay.”

It took the better part of an hour to reach the ground, and by the end of it they had stopped talking. The drift slowed as the plain came up, and for the last hundred feet they sank like seeds through a pale gold haze. Their boots touched the ash without a sound.

The world remembered them at once. Gravity came back like a hand on the shoulder, gentle and then not, and Vael’s knees took the weight with a small shock of surprise. Behind him, Figo had opened his eyes. He saw solid earth, and he fell on it with the fervent gratitude of a man reunited with an old friend, as he had on the first island, and kissed it.

It was ash. He spent the next minute spitting.

“Hey, Figo!” James called, with enormous relief. “Not in front of Sheela!”

“She understands,” said Figo, with dignity, wiping his tongue on his sleeve. Sheela, resting on his shoulder with a small braided knot under her blade, did not object.

The ground was ash, grey and fine as flour, lying in drifts to the ankle, soft as snow and utterly without sound. It took the print of every boot and kept it. Nothing grew in it. Behind them the pillar of light ran back up through the clouds like a spider’s thread, and thinned, and was gone.

At the edge of the ash, half buried, stood a tiny cairn of stacked pebbles, no higher than Vael’s hand, topped with a single pink petal. It was the only color for miles. Figo knelt and touched it with one finger. He didn’t say anything. He didn’t have to.

The road began a few hundred paces beyond it, where the ash gave way to black fitted stone. It ran east, straight as a drawn thread, toward a gap in the distant ring where two of the great collared cones stood like gateposts: a day’s walk, perhaps two. At its mouth, beside the first milestone, stood an engine.

It was a hauler, twice Figo’s height, built like a barrel on four jointed legs, with its arms half-raised and a blank iron face turned toward the east. Ash lay on its shoulders like grey fur. It had simply stopped, in the middle of a gesture, as though it had been about to wave. Vael walked all the way around it. Nothing moved.

“Don’t touch it,” said Atver.

“Is it…” Trista began.

“Stopped. I prefer it that way.”

Then the wind turned.

It came from the east, out of the heart of the ring, slow and warm and enormous: a long exhalation that rolled across the plain from horizon to horizon. It smelled of hot iron and old ash and something sweetish underneath, like a forge-mouth opened after a very long time. It lifted the ash at their feet in a single long ripple and let it settle.

Then it was gone, and the plain was still.

Far off, at the heart of the ring, the thread of smoke leaned toward them, thickened, and held.

“It knows,” Trista said at last. “It knows we’re coming.”

“It knows that something is coming,” said Atver. “It does not know what.”

Nobody answered. Vael turned the Ring of Eons on his finger. It was quiet and cool and patient, and it would be needed.

It was Figo who stepped onto the road first. Nobody had asked him to.

He walked out with Sheela over his shoulder and her little grass knot trembling in the wind, and he did not look at the engine, or at the smoke, or at the sky. He looked straight ahead. His eyes were red and his face was streaked with ash and tears, and he was afraid, and he knew it, and he had decided to stand in front of it anyway.

The others followed.

They walked east into the Molten Imperium, and the ash took the print of every step, and kept it.
