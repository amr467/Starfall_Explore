Chapter 1: The Ash and the Echo

Part 1 — Room Ten

There was ash in his mouth, and fire falling out of a sky that had turned the wrong color. Somewhere inside the roar of it, a hand was slipping out of his.

“MOTHER! DON’T LEAVE ME—”

The cry tore out of his throat, raw and suffocating, and broke into a gasp. “M-Mo…”

He bolted upright with his chest heaving, as if he had surfaced from deep water. His heart hammered against his ribs. The air he dragged into his lungs tasted entirely wrong. It carried no choking ash and no burning stone, only stale ale, roasted meat, old timber, and the faint sourness of too many people living under one roof.

He blinked against the weak light until the grit cleared from his eyes. He wasn’t outside. He was lying in a narrow bed, under a blanket of coarse wool, in what looked like the upstairs room of an inn. The walls were rough-hewn planks, gapped in places and stuffed with rags against the draft. A single window let in a dull grey dawn. Through the floorboards came the low, restless murmur of a crowd.

“Where… where am I?” he whispered.

His voice sounded like it belonged to someone else. He pressed a hand to his forehead to hold himself still, and reached inward for an answer, any answer. His name. His mother’s face. The street he grew up on. The color of his own eyes.

There was nothing there. It wasn’t like a locked door. It was like a room with no walls at all, only open dark in every direction. Behind his eyelids there was just the faint, terrible memory of fire falling from the sky. A starfall. Everything else, his past, his family, his own face, had been swallowed by shadow.

Heavy, uneven footsteps came hurrying down the corridor. The door swung inward with a creak, and a burly man filled the frame and let out a long sigh of relief.

“You alright, lad?” he asked, wiping his brow with a rag. “Saints above, we thought you were a goner for sure.”

The young man gripped the blankets. “How long was I out? Where am I?”

The man gave a reassuring if tired smile. “Two weeks, give or take. We carried you in the day after the sky fell, and you never so much as twitched.” He shook his head slowly. “To be frank, we didn’t find many survivors out there. You were the only one left breathing in that crater.”

“Crater?” The word felt heavy and dangerous in his mouth. “What crater? Wait… what happened to me?”

The man’s smile faded into something quieter. He stepped closer and studied the blank horror in the youth’s face. “Hey, lad… do you remember your name?”

The survivor reached deep into the fog, feeling for any syllable that felt familiar. Something snagged, only a sliver, a single letter. “Uhh… V… I… I don’t remember.”

“Well, I’ll call you V for the time being,” the innkeeper said warmly, trying to ease the tension. “I’m Ragnik, owner of the Hunter’s Guild Inn. You’re free to stay as long as you need. After what happened, we’ve been gathering every soul who lost their home. It’s been a long, brutal two weeks for us, to be honest. Treat this place as your own. I’ll go fetch you some proper food. Maybe your memory will find its way back with time.”

“Thanks, Ragnik,” V managed, and forced a weak smile.

As the older man turned to go, V looked at him properly. Ragnik was in his late forties or early fifties, with a face that looked weathered by weather and by worse. A jagged scar ran vertically over his right eye, and his left sleeve was pinned neatly against his shoulder, empty. He had the build of a veteran mercenary and the hard, careful way of moving that went with it. But when he spoke, his voice sat low and gentle in the room, like a man talking to a skittish horse.

Then the door closed, and V was alone with the ceiling.

He stared at it, at the knots in the wood, at a long water stain shaped like a river, and tried to piece the fragments back together. Fire. A hand. A word he had screamed. Every time he reached for the next piece, it dissolved like smoke.

Ragnik came back with a bowl of thick stew and a heel of bread, and stood over him with arms folded until V had eaten every scrap. The stew was salty, plain and hot, and V thought it was the best thing he had ever tasted, though he had no way of checking that against anything.

A couple of hours later, with a full stomach and a restless, stubborn energy he couldn’t account for, V decided he couldn’t stay in that small room any longer. He swung his legs out of bed and stood.

His muscles protested at once. Every joint felt stiff and rusted, as if his body hadn’t moved in far longer than his mind remembered. He braced a hand against the wall until the room stopped tilting.

It feels like I haven’t walked in ages, he thought. Like I’ve been hit by something massive. A rock, maybe. He frowned at his own hands. They were clean, unscarred and entirely unfamiliar. But how could anyone survive a direct hit from a falling star?

Someone had dressed him in borrowed clothes, a shirt too wide in the shoulders, trousers rolled twice at the ankle. He tugged the collar into place, drew a steadying breath, and opened the door.

The corridor was alive. He had expected quiet and found a river. Bedrolls lined the walls where the floor had been claimed inch by inch. A woman rocked a toddler in a doorway while an old man snored beside her, his boots still on. Somebody was frying onions below, and the smell crept up the stairwell and made the whole building feel almost like a home.

He rounded a corner and nearly collided with a young woman carrying a tall stack of fresh linens.

“Oh!” she gasped, stepping back with an apologetic laugh and hugging the pile against her chest. “You’re that sleeper from room ten! How are you feeling? You’ve been out cold for ages. Two whole weeks, can you believe it?” She paused, shaking her head, and then slapped her forehead lightly with her free hand. “Oh, where are my manners? Hi, I’m Rebecca. I’m the head of the front house here. If you need anything at all, just call for me and I’ll be right there.”

V rubbed the back of his neck, offering a sheepish smile. “Hi, Rebecca. I’m… V. Well, I don’t really remember my actual name, so just V for now.”

“A pleasure to properly meet you, V.” Her warm nod took in the borrowed clothes, the pallor, the unsteady hand on the wall, and she was kind enough not to mention any of it. “Take it easy out there.”

Then she was gone again, hurrying off with her linens toward the next crisis.

V followed the sound of voices down the stairs, and with every step the scale of the thing outside his room became harder to ignore.

The main hall was filled to the rafters. It was a vast, smoky room of dark beams and long tables, built for a hundred drinkers and now holding at least three times that many people. The atmosphere was a heavy, jarring mix of tension and despair. Along one wall sat the fighters: warriors in battered plate, mages in layered robes stitched with silver thread, hunters with weapons across their knees. Their faces were dark, tired and watchful. Along the other wall sat the refugees, haggard families with hollow eyes and nothing in their hands, who had lost everything to the cataclysm and come here because it was the only door still open.

A boy of about five sat on the floor beside the hearth, staring into the fire without blinking. A woman near the window was sorting a pile of children’s shoes, over and over, into pairs that no longer had owners. Someone had hung a blanket across a corner to make a room out of nothing, and from behind it came the quiet, steady sound of someone trying not to cry. The cold had crept in through every gap in the old timber. The fire fought it bravely, but there was only one fire.

V stood on the bottom step and felt very small.

This is what it looks like, he thought. After.

Then a clash of voices rose from the center of the room, and he was grateful for the interruption.

Part 2 — The Hunters’ Table

At the great wooden table in the middle of the hall, two groups were locked in a heated argument, and a crowd had gathered around them the way a crowd gathers around a fire. V, unable to help himself, drifted closer and let the bodies close around him.

“Can’t you see we are running perilously low on men?” The speaker was a heavily armored knight, broad as a door, and his gauntlets slammed down against the table hard enough to make the tankards jump. His eyes were wide and haunted, the eyes of a man who had seen a horror and was still seeing it. “It’s only a matter of time before that creature clears us all out, well before another starfall hits!”

“We don’t even know where it is currently,” a young woman countered sharply. Judging by the pristine white of her robes and the staff resting against her shoulder, she was a healer. Her voice was quick and clear, the voice of someone who had been right too many times to enjoy it. “Hunting it down blindly will cost us time we don’t have. Let’s not forget about the people right here in this room! They need our protection. Going on a suicide mission right now is a terrible idea.”

Between them, in a chair tipped so far back that it was practically horizontal, lounged a third man. He had a lazy, lopsided grin, a tankard balanced on his stomach, and the look of someone entirely unbothered by the looming threat of death.

“Fighting right now isn’t a good idea, friends,” he drawled, to nobody in particular and everybody at once. “We are from the Hunter’s Guild, after all. We need a proper plan. Minimize our losses, maximize our profit.”

V blinked. The man swayed slightly as he spoke, and the chair swayed with him.

He’s entirely too relaxed, V thought. Maybe he’s high? Or drunk… actually, looking at him, probably both.

He didn’t decide to move. His legs simply took him forward through the crowd, driven by a sudden, inexplicable urge he couldn’t have explained to anyone. He couldn’t sit on the sidelines while the world was ending around him. He stepped into the light at the center of the tavern.

“Excuse me,” V said. He kept his voice steady despite his pounding heart. “What looming threat are we talking about here?”

The three hunters stopped mid-argument and turned to look at him. Their eyes raked over his pale face, his trembling hands, his borrowed, ill-fitting clothes. Unimpressed, they collectively shrugged him off.

“None of your business, kid,” the heavily armored man grunted, turning his back.

The drunkard, however, let his chair thump forward onto four legs. His eyes gleamed with a lazy, chaotic light. He offered a sloppy wave. “Ah, nothing much, stranger. Just working through our options right now. Spoiler alert: it’s not looking good. Y’all might die soon, sadly. Such a beautiful tavern with a sad, tough fate.”

“JAMES!” the knight and the healer shouted in unison, and their voices rang off the rafters.

“What?” James asked, throwing up his hands in mock innocence. “I’m just stating the facts.”

Somewhere behind V, a child started to cry, and a mother’s hushed voice tried to soothe him. The healer heard it too. She rubbed her temples and let out a long, exhausted sigh. When she looked at V, some of the sharpness left her expression and pity took its place.

“There was a Dracol spotted near the tavern a couple of days ago,” she said. “Well, we didn’t see the beast itself, but we found fresh tracks. Our researchers are fairly certain it’s a Raging Variant.”

“Dracol?” V repeated. The word sounded foreign on his tongue, like a stone he had been handed in the dark.

The armored man whipped around, jaw dropping in sheer disbelief. “How the hell do you not know what a Dracol is? Where the hell are you from, space?!”

Before V could stammer out an excuse, the man called James leaned forward in his chair, narrowing his bloodshot eyes. He sniffed the air with a theatrical flourish, tracing V’s silhouette from head to toe. Then his gaze changed. It lost its focus, drifting a little to one side of V, as though he were reading small print written on the air around him. A wild, knowing grin crept across his face.

“Oh, now that is a surprise,” James purred, dropping his voice an octave. “I can tell you’re a hunter, kid. I can practically smell it on you. Mmm… powerful stuff.”

V instinctively took a step back, a chill running down his spine.

This guy is a freak, he thought.

“Don’t mind him,” the healer said quickly, waving a dismissive hand. “If you can’t tell, nothing he says makes sense. He’s a useless drunkard. Even on our guild missions, he barely does anything but—”

“Shhh!” James interrupted, laying a dramatic finger across his lips. “We don’t want to reveal our secret weapon to the stranger. Speaking of… who are you, stranger?”

Every eye in the circle was on him. V took a deep breath and steeled himself.

“My name is V. And I want to help you.” The words came out faster than he’d planned, and he let them. “Something inside me is screaming that if I do this, it will help me remember my past. Look, I’ve forgotten everything. I’ve been out cold for two weeks, I feel like I was crushed by a boulder, and everything is moving too fast. If I just sit in that room any longer, I’m going to go insane.”

For a heartbeat, nobody spoke.

James gasped dramatically and slammed his fist onto the table, rattling every tankard on it. “HAH! I love it! We need him with us. Y’all know my gut is never wrong!”

The knight crossed his massive, armored arms. He wasn’t buying it, and his scowl said so. The healer looked more worried than anything, her eyes sliding over V’s fragile frame, the thin wrists, the unsteady stance. Yet, for some reason V couldn’t read, a heavy silence fell over the pair of them. It was clear that despite James being a total mess, both of them deeply trusted his erratic judgment. Some old, settled thing lay underneath the bickering, and it said that his gut had been proven right before.

“I don’t mind, I suppose,” the healer muttered, sighing in defeat.

“If you fall behind, we aren’t stopping to save you,” the knight added, looking away with a dismissive shrug. He didn’t outright say no.

James pulled himself upright, puffing out his chest with ridiculous bravado. “The name is James. If you haven’t figured it out already, I’m the strongest, most handsome elite in this entire group!”

The knight let out a loud, hacking, sarcastic “COUGH!” into his gauntlet. The healer struggled, and mostly failed, to suppress a laugh.

“This grumpy, sick piece of armor over here is known as Figo,” James went on, entirely unfazed. “And the lovely mage lady is Trista.” He snatched up his tankard and took a massive, desperate gulp to finish the lukewarm ale, then slammed the empty wood down. “I have a plan, thanks to V! Woo! Our newest member! But first… LET’S CELEBRATE A NEW MEMBER ON OUR UNSTOPPABLE TEAM! HAHAAHAHAA—uh… oh… BLUGHEGHEEFHHGE!”

James instantly doubled over, and the whole contents of his stomach arrived on the tavern floor.

The crowd recoiled in a single groaning wave. Trista didn’t flinch. She just closed her eyes and shook her head, a woman greeting an old, familiar tragedy. “Oh, here he goes again. Guess I must heal him…” A white, soothing glow gathered around her fingertips as she stepped toward the retching man.

V stood frozen. Disgust and absolute bewilderment washed over him in equal measure. Then, despite the horror of it, something warm bubbled up out of his chest, a genuine laugh, the first he could remember, which wasn’t saying much, but still.

“Hah… well, glad to be here, I guess.”

Figo, looking like a man who had fully expected this exact sequence of events, let out a grim smile and turned to V. “Welcome to the team, lad. So, let’s get down to business… what’s your specialty? Swords? Magic? Scouting?”

V opened his mouth to answer.

The laughter died in his throat. He looked down at his hands, empty, unscarred, and entirely without answers.

“I… I don’t know.”

Trista, busy with a purification spell over a groaning James, froze mid-chant. Figo’s jaw dropped. For a moment the whole table, the tavern, and the ash-gray morning seemed to hold its breath, and then Figo’s voice cut through the ambient noise of the hall:

“WHAT?!”
