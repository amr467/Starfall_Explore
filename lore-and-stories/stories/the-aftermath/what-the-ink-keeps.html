Chapter 11: What the Ink Keeps

One year later.

The grass had come back over the plot behind the inn.

It lay thick across the five old mounds from the Dracol’s first winter, and thinner, brighter and more reluctant over the three beside them, which were a year old and still looked unfinished. The stakes at the head of the first row had weathered to silver and gone soft as driftwood. The newer three had stones. Vael had paid for them out of contracts he could barely remember signing.

He stood before them the way he stood before everything now, his weight on one leg, as though the other were already on the road. The shoulder guard strapped to his left side had been made for a man a head taller and half again as broad, and no number of new holes, punched through the leather with the point of a knife, had taught it to fit. It rode high and crooked, a slab of dented steel that pulled him a little toward it, like a tree grown up in a steady wind. His right shoulder was bare to the shirt. Only the one guard had come through whole.

At his belt, knotted twice through the loop, hung a scrap of white cloth as fine as paper, its hem the color of rust. Beside it swung a small oak cask, unscorched, the bung sealed over with fresh wax. It sloshed when he walked. He had never opened it.

People did not look at his eyes for long. They were not red; he had passed red months ago. They had gone dry and scoured, like a window cleaned so often that the glass had thinned, and the skin beneath them had turned the shade of a bruise that had made up its mind to stay. He took the next contract before the last was signed. He slept in the backs of carts and against walls, and once, memorably, bolt upright in a saddle. Rebecca said that one evening he would sit down to pull his boots off and wake up in the spring.

Figo’s stone was the widest, because Figo had been; Vael had made the mason cut it twice. Sheela leaned against it with her haft bound in strips of leather where it had split, and beneath her blade the knot of green grass was still tied, gone grey and dry as paper but whole. He had retied it twice when the cord frayed. Trista’s was narrower, kept bare but for a ring of white pebbles that had appeared the first week and been renewed ever since by people he never saw, travelers, he supposed, whom she had mended on some road. And on James’s stone stood a tin cup that was always full of something other than water. Vael had never caught who filled it. He suspected Ragnik. Ragnik, when asked, suspected him.

“North road,” he told them. “A Dagaruma pack under the old bridge. Eleven of them. Nobody got hurt.”

He waited, out of habit, for the correction. Figo would have called them small fry. James would have had something to say about the smell. Trista would have murmured well done in the tone she reserved for small children and nervous horses, and then looked him over for the injuries he hadn’t mentioned.

The wind moved through the grass. That was all that answered.

His mother had told him, in the one room where she could still be spoken to, that he must not hold it all up alone. He had nodded to her face. Then he had walked out and picked up everything within reach.

He let a breath go that lasted a long time, and turned toward the inn.

The path ran along the flank of the building, where the old grey planks of the winter had been patched with new yellow ones and nobody had troubled to hide the join. That was how the whole of Hunter’s Rest had been rebuilt: scar showing, working. Out front, where the road widened before the door, stood a fountain that had not existed a year ago.

The guild had paid for it out of its own purses, and the stonework had taken the better part of the year. The basin was wide enough to sit around, and people did: hunters with their boots off, children wet to the elbow, a dog drinking with enormous suspicion. At the center, four figures in pale ridge-stone stood shoulder to shoulder in a ring, each facing a different way, as hunters do when they sleep on open ground.

Figo had the east road. The mason had given him the whole of Sheela, unbroken, raised above his head in both fists, and a face caught in the first half-second of a roar. At his belt, small enough that you had to look for it, hung a carved fish. Only people who had known him found that funny, and so people kept finding it: a hunter would stop, squint, and burst out laughing alone in the road. James faced west with his arms flung wide and his head thrown back, coat flaring in a wind that had stopped blowing for him a year ago, a small stone cask at his hip and a grin the mason had needed three attempts to get right. Trista faced north. Her staff stood upright in her left hand, and her right was held out open, palm up, and from the palm a thin bright thread of water fell into the basin. Her expression was patient. The mason said she looked like someone waiting for another person to finish a sentence, and Vael had told him that was exactly right.

The fourth figure faced the door. It stood a little taller than the man it was cut after, and a good deal straighter, with its eyes closed and its hands pressed together before its chest in the middle of a sign. There was nothing in the stone face of the tiredness. It had been done out of kindness. Vael had never decided whether it was a kindness to him.

At their feet, curled in the water like a cat at a hearth, lay the tail of a Dracol, cut in the same pale stone and ending in a point of clear, faceted crystal that threw small rainbows over the wet. Vael had told the mason about the tail. The mason had said no one would believe it. Vael had said that was all right, and the mason had put it in anyway.

He looked at the three of them as he passed, and the corner of his mouth lifted of its own accord. It was the one smile he had that still worked, and he saved it for the fountain.

Two hunters sat on the rim of the basin with their feet in the water: a thickset man with a red beard braided into two ropes and a boar’s tusk on a thong at his throat, and beside him a wiry younger one with sandy hair and a guild badge so new it had not yet learned modesty. They were arguing about a knot. The younger one saw Vael first, and the argument went out of him like air from a bladder.

“Oh,” he said. Then, louder, driving an elbow into his friend’s ribs: “Oh! It’s the Dracol Slayer!”

The red-bearded man swung round so fast that water slopped over the rim. “The legendary Vael himself,” he announced, with the air of a man presenting a rare cheese. “How was the mission?”

Vael stopped. It took him a moment to find the thing his face did when it was addressed like this, and then he found it and put it on: a smile, tired and kind and worn thin as a coin. “It went all right.”

“Of course it did!” The red-bearded man heaved himself up, one foot still bare, and clapped Vael on the shoulder, the uncovered one, because the other would have broken his hand. “Welcome back, friend.”

Vael nodded to them both and walked on to the door.

It stood open on a roar of voices and a gold wash of firelight. It was busier than he had ever seen it. He went in.

The Hunter’s Guild Inn had not been this loud since before the Starfall, and Ragnik, who had spent the better part of a year listening to the lack of it, still had to remind himself on certain evenings that this was what he had wanted.

He stood where he always stood, behind the long counter at the back of the hall, filling cups. He filled them three at a time, the handles hooked through the fingers of the one hand he had left, a grip that had taken him half a year to learn and which Rebecca swore looked like a juggler’s mistake. She took them as fast as he could pour, a tray on her hip and a cloth over her shoulder, threading between the tables with the smile she had worn since the first refugee came through the door, and which had never once slipped in front of a customer.

The hall ran full to its walls. Three fires burned where there had been one. The cots and bedrolls were gone, and tables stood in their place, shoulder to shoulder, with benches already rubbed smooth by a season of sitting. Hunters in from the road with the dust still on them; merchants haggling over their ale as though it were cargo; two guild scribes in a corner who had been disagreeing about a sum since noon. A Dagaruma tail hung over the hearth, which Figo would have called a fine specimen and James a smell. The new planks were still yellow. Someone had strung dried apples from the rafters. By the contract board near the door, on a stool too high for him, Raul was explaining the guild’s rates to two travelers with a gravity far beyond his years. Twice this week Ragnik had caught the boy laughing. He had told no one, in case telling frightened it off.

It was getting better. Not evenly, and not with any manners, the way a bone knits; there would be a limp in it for years. But it was mending. Life, against all expectation, was healing.

The door swung wide on the noise of the road and let in a draft that bowed every flame in the hall, and Vael came through behind it, shoulder guard first.

Ragnik knew the walk before he saw the face. He had a talent for reading weather in a man’s gait, picked up over thirty years of guild halls, and this was the gait of someone carrying water in cupped hands across a very long room, careful not to spill it, long past remembering why. Then the face. Ragnik’s hand stopped on the spout, and a thread of cider ran over the rim of a cup and down his wrist.

He raised his voice over the din, which he seldom did. “Hey, lad!” The hall dropped half a tone to let it through. “How did the mission go?”

Vael’s head came around. He found Ragnik across the room, and the exhaustion rearranged itself, somewhere behind his eyes, into what looked very like gladness, and Ragnik’s chest did a thing it had no business doing at his age. The lad worked his way through the crowd, accepted three more claps on the shoulder, and took the stool at the end of the counter that had come to be called his without anyone ever saying so.

“Hi,” said Vael. “It went as usual.”

His voice sounded scraped. A year of it, Ragnik thought. A year, and not one night in all of it that the lad had lain down properly.

“Don’t push yourself to death, friend.” He slid a cup across the wood. It smelled of baked apple and clove and honey. “Your favorite. Nothing in it stronger than the honey. On the house.”

“Thanks, Ragnik.”

Ragnik leaned over the counter until his scarred brow was close to the lad’s ear, and dropped his voice to what, for him, passed as a whisper; it carried as far as the third table. “Right. Someone asked for you. Table by the stairs.” He tipped his chin at it, since the hand was full. “I don’t like the look of them, Vael. Never seen them before in my life. Came in at noon, paid ahead with a coin so old the face was worn smooth, and they haven’t touched a thing Rebecca brought. Be careful.”

Vael looked where the chin pointed. A table in the corner where the lamplight gave up, at the foot of the staircase: a figure in a long robe the color of a wet road, the hood drawn forward so far that nothing showed of them but the line of a bowed head. Vael nodded slowly, took his cup in both hands, and went.

Ragnik watched him cross the hall. He watched the cask knock softly at the lad’s hip with each step, and the white scrap stir, and the great borrowed shoulder tilt the whole of him to one side. Three people’s worth of weight, and he seemed to think nobody noticed.

On the road south, Ragnik had once told him what his wife’s last hour had bought. He had watched the words go in and be taken up as a debt, and he had spent the long time since wishing them back, and finding that the language had no word for the opposite of that. So he had settled on cups.

The noise of the hall reached the table by the stairs as if through a wall.

It was the one corner the lamps gave up on. At every other table the flames leaned and shivered whenever the door opened; the candle here stood as straight as a nail, and the air around it seemed to have been asked to stay still, and to have agreed. Salt came off the stranger, and cold tallow, and under that a great deal of old paper. A cup of tea sat at their elbow, long past steaming, as flat and still as a pond.

Vael pulled out the opposite chair, sat, and set his cup between them.

Their hands were all he could see: thin, long, resting on the table’s edge. The fingers were inked to the second knuckle, and perfectly steady, with the kind of steadiness that only comes from long practice at not shaking.

Then the head came up, and Vael flinched before he could stop himself.

Of the face he saw almost nothing, a chin, the corner of a mouth, skin the color of paper kept in a drawer. It was the eyes. They were dark, not in color but in shade, like a room where someone had turned a lamp down and then thought better of coming back. There were too many things in them. He had seen that look on the survivors who came down off the ridge that first winter, but it was older here, far older, as a coin is older than the hand that spends it.

“You asked for me?” His voice came out smaller than he had meant.

The stranger said nothing. A hand slid into the folds of the robe and came out with a book, bound in leather gone soft and dark as river-stone and tied shut with a strip of undyed cord. Two fingers freed the knot without looking at it. A pen followed, and the cover fell open on a page as white as the day it was made.

“Tell it all to me.”

The voice was low and level and wrong. The end of each phrase came again behind it, thinner, from farther off, as if the tavern were a larger room than it was and the words had been called across it. Me. And then, fainter, from nowhere: me.

“Tell what?”

“Your journey. The Dracol. All of it.”

Vael blinked. No greeting, no name, no build-up. In a year of strangers who wanted to shake his hand, or buy him a drink, or show him a sword they had made, nobody had opened with all of it.

“I’m sorry,” he said, after a pause in which he looked, quite casually, for the nearest door. “Who are you?”

The stranger flinched. It was a small thing, a hitch between the shoulder blades, and it was the first sign of anything human that Vael had seen, which did not help as much as it should have.

Now the head turned to him fully, and he saw the eyes in good light. Human. Certainly human. But at the edges of them, where the lid met the skin, a slow unraveling was at work: not a stain or a mark, but a page gone at its margin where damp has found it and the ink begins to lift. Something had been eating this person for a very long time, and had not yet decided to finish. He tried to say what, and could not get nearer than that.

“I am everything,” said the stranger. The flat voice gave it no weight, and so it had all of it. “And you, Slayer, have an important part in this world. It would be a shame for it to be forgotten.” The pen turned once between the long fingers. “Dracol Slayer. A title many here care about. For me it is something else.” The echo came and went. “Starfall Survivor.”

The words went through Vael and came out the other side as heat.

He was on his feet. He did not remember choosing it. His palm came down on the table hard enough to jump both cups, and the nearest tables glanced over, found nothing worth the trouble, and went back to their ale.

“You know who caused the Starfall.” It came out cracked. “Is it my father?”

The stranger smiled. It was not unkind, and it was not kind; it was the smile of a person asked this question many times, by many people, who had learned to find it touching. “Your father has nothing to do with it,” they said. “But I will offer you a bargain, Dracol Slayer. His name, in exchange for the story. Everything you remember. The road, the hunters beside you, everyone you met along the way. When you are done, I will tell you what you want to know.”

For a while he did not sit. He stood with one hand flat on the wood, listening to the blood in his ears and to the part of him that had been a year-long bruise and wanted to overturn the table. He thought of Jade’s voice, telling him of a woman who had known no other Dragodor and of a man whom nobody named. He thought of a room with pale boards and a lullaby, a little flat in the third bar.

Then he sat, and the chair took his weight with a sigh louder than his own.

“I need to know why,” he said. “Why it happened. I’ve tried to build it out of the pieces, and they won’t fit.”

“It is beyond what you can hold,” said the stranger. “For now.” And then, as though the sentence had cost something and needed another to pay for it: “But I can tell you this much. Your story may be a key to stopping it. I cannot confirm that yet.”

Stopping. He turned the word over. In a year of stone and statues and signed contracts, nobody had once said it to him. They said survived, and endured, and rebuilt. Not one of them said stopped. Stopped from happening again, he supposed. He did not suppose that was all the stranger meant, and a cold touch settled on the nape of his neck, and he could not have said why.

He looked at them again. They looked at the book. At the candle, at the staircase beside them, at the cold tea, at Vael’s hands. At his face only in glances, quick as a finger brushing a hot kettle and withdrawing, as though a face held for longer than a breath might have to be answered.

“All right,” said Vael.

So he told it.

He began at the wrong end, with the grey ceiling over a narrow bed in room ten, and the stranger said nothing, only wrote. The pen kept pace with him word for word, in a hand that slanted hard to the right and put a small hook on the tail of every g. The crater came out of him, and the weeks without a name. Figo came next, and the correct way to speak of Sheela, and the fishing stories, which grew longer with each fish and resembled fewer of them. James: the nicknames, the cask, the thing he saw when he looked at a person and never said aloud. Trista, who complained about everyone and went through fire for all of them, and had. The Framedious. The Davouras and the honey. The islands, the long fall, the Champampa king in his crown of one red feather.

“Barred in black,” murmured the stranger, to the page.

Vael stopped. He had said only red. He had not said the rest to anyone.

“Go on,” the stranger said.

Somewhere in the second hour he came to James and the name he had given Jade, and he laughed. It came out cracked and rusted, the laugh of a man finding a room in his own house that he had forgotten was there. He stopped, startled. And across the table the stranger, who had not been looking at him, made a sound too: a short dry breath that began as a laugh and collapsed halfway, followed by a hand pressed across the mouth. For one moment the figure at the table looked a good deal less like a figure out of an old story, and a good deal more like a person who had not laughed in so long that the sound was frightening.

Neither of them mentioned it. He went on.

The hall emptied around them by stages. Rebecca banked the fires. The scribes took their quarrel up the stairs. Ragnik put out the lamps one by one, and left without a word the one above their table, and the candle, which did not seem to have noticed the hour. The dead city came out under its ash, and Aava with her moss, and the plan that had gone wrong, and the plaza, and the beam. Jack. What his mother had said at the window. What it had felt like to bring his hands together he kept, and the stranger, for once, did not ask.

When he stopped, the pen stopped a breath later. The silence that followed was not empty. It had the weight of a very long sentence just completed.

“Perfect,” the stranger breathed. And again, with plain hunger: “Perfect… This will indeed be useful.”

The book closed. The cord was tied without looking. It went back into the robe, and the stranger rose, and Vael rose with them, stiff in every joint.

“Your turn,” he said. “Who is my father?”

The stranger looked at him, not in a glance this time, but fully, and it was the longest he had been looked at in a year. Something cold set a foot down at the top of his back and began to walk. Whoever this was, they had seen too much, and had been alive for every bit of it.

“You remind me of him,” said the stranger, “back when he was young and naive. Asterion is the name you are looking for.” A pause, in which the echo did what it did. “He is alive.”

The name went into Vael and did not come out. It meant nothing to him, and it should have meant everything, and somewhere under his ribs a latch lifted.

The stranger started for the door. Vael stepped into the way.

“Where is he?”

“I promised a name, did I not?” The head tilted. “The rest is for you to work out. You will find him. I am sure of it. Eventually.”

They stepped around him as one steps around a chair. Beside the stairs they paused, no longer than a breath, and bowed their head toward the lowest step and said something to it that Vael caught only the end of, …not long now. Thank you, and the old wood answered with a creak, though nothing stood on it. Then the long robe crossed the hall, and Vael watched every step. He had learned to watch walkers, and this walk was strange: it did not hurry, and it left no sound upon the boards. When the hem crossed the threshold he was already moving.

The road outside was empty.

Moonlight lay over the cobbles and the dark basin, and the fountain ran its thin thread from Trista’s palm, and the four stone figures faced their four directions. Nothing moved on the road in either direction as far as the last lamp. He ran to the corner of the inn wall and looked down the lane, and round the back to where the graves lay in their row. Nothing. A road a thousand footsteps long, and the person who had been on it a breath ago was not.

His own stone face looked back at him from the middle of the fountain, with its eyes shut.

He stood there until the cold worked its way into his hands. Then he went back in.

The hall was nearly dark. Rebecca had gone up. The three fires had sunk to red, and he stood in the middle of the floor with the whole of the night still running through his head, trying to make it hold still long enough to mean something, when a voice reached him through the noise.

“Vael. Is everything all right?”

Aava sat at the far side of the long counter under a single lamp, with her ledger open, and she had been waiting for him; he could tell by the way she was pretending not to.

She had changed more than any of them, as people do who are finally allowed to. The girl who had counted bricks in a tower of ash was a woman now, steadier in the shoulders, with a pencil pushed through the knot of her hair and ink along the side of one hand. A handler’s brass pin sat on her collar. A year of regular meals had put color in her face; a year of regular company had taken the wall out of her voice and left only the habit of care that the wall had been built around. By her elbow, in a cracked teacup, the moss she had once watered by the spoonful had swollen into a green cushion the size of a fist, and was spreading over the rim.

“You went to see them again, didn’t you?”

It took him a second to understand that she meant the graves. “Yeah.” It was true, and it was not what she had asked about, and he let it stand. “Yeah, I did. What’s our next mission?”

She looked at him a moment longer than the reading of a face required, and read it. “Nothing else today.” She closed the ledger on her pencil. Under his name, the column for tomorrow was bare. It was never bare, and he noticed it the way a tongue notices a missing tooth, briefly, and without drawing any conclusion. “Go and rest, Vael.”

“I…” The words arrived in the wrong order, and he started over. “I got a lead, Aava. On my father.”

Her whole careful face came open at once. “You did? Vael, that’s wonderful—”

“Asterion,” he said. “His name is Asterion.”

The brightness did not leave her face, but something moved behind it. “I’ve heard that name before.” She rose and took a few slow steps along the counter, one finger trailing the edge of the wood, and stopped, and turned, and the smile that came was small and real. “Tomorrow’s job is going to be an interesting one. I’ll need to gather some information first.” She slid a clay cup toward him, full to the brim, though he had not asked for one. “Use the time to rest.”

He meant to thank her. The sentence got as far as his teeth and gave up. He nodded instead, and she nodded back, as though that had been a sentence too.

The lowest stair creaked under his boot. He stopped with his weight on it, and listened, like a fool, for an answer.

None came.

Room ten had a real mattress now, and a window with glass in it. He lay down in his clothes and found the shoulder guard digging into his ribs, and lay there resenting it for as long as a minute before he sat up and worked at the buckles, which took a while, given his hands. He set it on the chair. Then the white cloth, folded in four. Then the cask. They sat in a row in the moonlight, and it looked to him like a table laid for guests who weren’t coming.

He lay back with one hand flung above his head. Down in the road the fountain was still running, thin and steady, like someone pouring and pouring from a cup that never emptied.

“Asterion,” he told the ceiling. His voice had almost gone. “Where are you?”

Sleep took him the way it always did when it came: all at once, without apology.

Starwatch Keep. The reading room. The candle is where it has always been.

I have it. Not all of it: most of it, and the outline of the rest, and that will have to do for tonight, because the pen has gone heavy and my nose has started up again, there, a small red hour at the corner of the page. I won’t blot it. It belongs to the account now.

He sat down. I want that entered somewhere before I lose it. He did not trust me, and he was right not to; I would have thought less of him otherwise. But of all the witnesses I have sat across from, he is the first to stay in the chair after hearing my voice come back at him off the walls. I had a speech ready. I have a feeling the other time went worse.

Why this account, of all the shelves? Because of the sky. I have read the night of the Starfall in nine hundred hands, and in every one of them the light comes down and the world goes under it and nobody does anything but run or die. In his, a woman stood in the road with her arms out, and something white held the sky back for him. Not for long, not well, but it held, and in nine hundred hands that is the only time anything has been seen to push back. He calls it Jack. I have turned that over for three days. A thing that can be pushed against has an edge, and a thing with an edge can be turned, or stopped, or—

Or. I had the word a moment ago. It was a good word.

The second thing is smaller and colder. A hum under the dead city stopped when the beast died, and something below answered the silence. I felt it here, across all this water and all these years, like a thumb laid on the back of my neck. For the new era, it said. Whose, I can’t yet say.

By morning I will begin to write it all out in order, in my own hand, with the rooms he was not in filled from what I have seen. I am good at rooms.

I should set down the cost, since I keep writing round it. The crossing takes a toll on the far side, and the toll is never the same. At first, only hours: a sleepless morning. Then a week. Lately it takes what it likes. The color of the sea from the window. A song I used to hum on the stairs. The count of my crossings, which I kept so carefully, and which now stops at a number I cannot have reached, because I remember crossings after it. The thing that takes the toll waits nearer the door each time; on the last one I could hear it breathing. This morning I set out two cups for tea and stood a long while trying to think who the second was for. It has been taken, whoever it was. Or I am making it up. I am told the difference matters.

[margin, unsteady] The tea is cold. I did not drink it. I never do.

And the road ahead. I have walked farther down it than is wise, and I will say only this. The stars come down on a schedule there, like rain in a season, and the people have given up on leaving; they have learned to pick up what falls and burn it for light. They build their cities in the wound and are proud of the glow. They have forgotten what the light costs, and that anything else was ever needed. I stood in a street there that hummed under my feet, among signs I could not read, and when I turned to look for the door I had come through, I could not find it. I do not know whether the city moved, or my memory of the door.

I need more of them. More hands, more stories, more rooms I was not in. The Slayer’s is the first account with a door in it, but one door is not a house. I believe there is a seam through all of them: a single stitch run through every sheet, and I almost have my finger on it. The accounts do not simply sit beside one another. They lean. Each is turned a few degrees toward the next, and the next, if I follow the lean, is not near. It lies far down the road, past anything I have taught my feet to reach: an age where light is sold, and someone is standing at the edge of it, looking back.

I will keep my eye on the Slayer. Not close; I can only be close at the wrong moment. When the old man finds him, or he finds the old man, I mean to be in the next room with the door open a hand’s width. I have never managed that. We will manage it.

I wrote we again. There is only the one of me. I went to the stairs and asked, and they said nothing either.

The candle has not changed. I decided long ago not to think about it, and the decision has held better than most.

I remember when this map was whole.

Which map?

I remember. I do.

There is a line at the bottom of the page where my name goes, and I have held the pen over it for some time, and I cannot think what belongs there. I will leave it. The title has always been enough.
