Chapter 9: What the Numbers Hide

Part 1 — The Zero

By the time the sun began to sink, the engines had stopped being a surprise.

They stood in their ranks along both sides of the avenue, haulers and diggers and, behind them, the tall blind shapes of siege, each in its coat of grey ash. Vael had learned to walk between them the way one walks through a crowd at a funeral, eyes forward, voice low. The light came in long and copper from behind and laid the party’s shadows out on the road ahead: four tall figures walking toward the thread of smoke, which had gone from grey to the dull red of a coal as the day failed.

Nobody was talking, and for once it was not only tiredness. Figo walked in front with Sheela held in both hands instead of slung on his back, Little’s grass knot swinging under her blade with every stride. Somewhere around the Fourth Ring he had stopped shivering at the word Dracol. Vael had noticed it the way you notice a clock that has stopped ticking. Trista had tied back her hair and cinched every pouch on her belt, and the stain on the hem of her white robe, the one she had scrubbed at in a stream and given up on, had dried to the color of rust. She wore it like a rank. She walked a half-step behind the others, where she could see all their feet, and every so often her eyes passed over them like a hand checking for fever.

James had not made a joke since the second milestone. His cask rode at his hip, unopened, and he walked with his chin tucked and his eyes a little out of focus, the way they went when the world printed something in small type just beyond everyone else’s sight. His lips moved now and then, on numbers.

Vael kept the Ring of Eons turned so its stone sat in his palm, where it was warm. The wolf padded at his heel with its head low. Nobody had told any of them to be ready. Nobody had needed to.

The heat had begun to come up through their boots, faint and living, the warmth of a stone that has remembered it was once a hearth. And beneath the silence, so low that it was less a sound than a pressure behind the teeth, the ground hummed.

“Friends,” said James, “let’s call it a day and rest.”

He turned and smiled at them all, bright and easy, the old smile, the one that had talked its way into a hundred taverns. It lasted about as long as the sentence did. Then it went out of his face like a lamp being turned down, and he looked at Vael, and only at Vael.

“We need to form a plan,” he said. “And now is the right time.”

Nobody laughed. Vael had known James long enough to tell the two of him apart. The loud one could be teased, talked over, ignored. The quiet one could not. When James went quiet, it meant he had seen something, a fork in the road, a number out of place, that needed turning before it was too late to turn.

No one argued. They were tired in the way that settles under the skin after a day of walking toward something, and tomorrow would take whatever they had left.

A quarter-mile on, they found somewhere to hide. A siege-hauler lay on its side across the verge, one of the few engines on the avenue that was not standing. Someone had been at it long before the Dracol, with cutting tools and patience, the same work that had armed the Gatecut: the core was gone from its chest, and the plates hung open like the shell of something cracked and emptied.

Figo went in first, sideways, pauldrons first, and for once nothing screamed against the stone. “It’s like a little room in here,” his voice boomed back out of the iron. And then, pleased with it: “Heh.”

Trista was right behind him, with a candle stub Aava had slipped in with the rations. A spark of silver at her fingertip lit it, and the hull filled with small gold light. She smiled at Figo, a tired, real smile. “This is the perfect place to recharge,” she said. “Big day tomorrow.”

She took the far corner and lay down with her staff beside her, one hand curled around it, as always.

Of all of them, the journey had been hardest on her. Two days of cracked lips and blistered feet and every small hurt anyone had been too proud to mention had passed through her hands, a little at a time, and she had not once asked anyone to carry some of it. Vael looked at the shadows under her eyes and thought, not for the first time, that she was the closest thing to a sister he had.

“You deserve some rest, Trista,” he said, and sat down in the opposite corner.

“I know,” she murmured, with her eyes already closed.

James, who had never let a doorway go by without making an entrance of it, came in last. He tossed the cask from hand to hand, swept under the sprung plate in one smooth bow, and spread his arms to the hull at large. “Well, well, well! If it ain’t the Dracol Slayers!”

“We haven’t killed one, James,” said Trista, in a voice with no energy left to be irritated.

“Not yet.” He winked.

Figo let out a rumble of a chuckle he could not seem to help. Vael smiled through all of it. It was the first time anyone had laughed all day, and he was not going to be the one to point that out.

James folded himself down against the far wall, the candle between them making a small gold room inside the iron.

“We rest tonight,” Vael said. “No watch. We’re well hidden in here, once we cover the entrance.” He had not forgotten the log wrapped in fern. He had only decided that a sealed door made a better sentry than a tired boy. He and the wolf dragged a sheet of hull plate across the opening, the wolf hauling at its edge with its teeth, until the last of the copper light was gone. “Tomorrow, we listen to your plan, James.”

“All right, all right.” James pillowed his head on the cask. “Sleep well, everyone. Don’t let the spiders bite!”

Trista opened one eye and gave him a face. It was a very good face, with a long history behind it. Figo was already asleep, snoring around Sheela’s haft.

“Good night,” said Vael, and the wolf came apart into his shadow, and the candle burned down.

Morning found them through the seams.

It came in as a hundred thin threads of gold where the plates did not quite meet, and lay across the sleepers one at a time until it found its way past their eyelids. For once, everyone had slept. Properly, deeply, without a cold stone in the ribs or a dream worth remembering. Vael could not recall the last time he had woken feeling like a person.

James was already up. He sat cross-legged in a slant of light with his cask forgotten at his knee, murmuring. He wasn’t talking to himself, Vael realized after a moment. He was reading. His gaze moved from one sleeper to the next, and his lips shaped numbers too quiet to hear, the way a man goes back over a sum he has already checked a dozen times, hoping that this time it will come out different. A spider no bigger than a thumbnail had strung a web across the vent above his head in the night. He had not noticed. Vael decided not to tell him.

Trista woke next, with a yawn so enormous it seemed to take her a moment to find her way back out of it. “One more minute,” said a voice from underneath an axe, and said it again, at intervals, with decreasing conviction, until the smell of breakfast reached Figo and he sat up all at once.

Vael hauled the plate aside and let the morning in.

The avenue lay in blue shadow, the dead engines stretching away on both sides with the first sun catching only their shoulders, so that each wore a rim of copper over its grey fur of ash. A thin mist lifted off the road where the cool of the night met the warmth rising from below, and went up into the light like the breath of something sleeping. Far ahead, the collared cones of the inner ring had begun to glow along their iron rims, one after another, a necklace of small fires being lit. On the flank of the gutted hauler, a patch of grey-green lichen no bigger than a hand had taken hold on the iron, the first living thing Vael had seen growing anywhere on the plain. He looked at it for longer than he meant to. Then the thread of smoke at the heart of the bowl drew his eyes back where they belonged.

They ate in the mouth of the wreck with their boots in the ash: Aava’s bricks of pressed grain, her dried apple rings, a flask of cistern water she had filtered twice. She had weighed out every portion herself and then counted them again, as though the count might be argued with. Vael ate slowly, and remembered a girl in a patchwork vest counting rations into a sack, and smiled. It was the plain, uncomplicated gratitude of a man who had been thirsty for two days and then was not. That was all it was.

James waited until the last brick was gone. Vael saw him do it, saw him make himself wait, as though bad news ought at least to be delivered to people with full stomachs.

“So,” said James at last. “Here is the situation.”

He looked around at the three of them, rested and fed, and something in his face changed. Not much. The way a room changes when someone closes the window.

“I don’t usually do this,” he said. “But the situation asks for it. This Dracol fight needs a unique plan.”

“What kind of unique are we talking about?” Trista asked.

James looked at her, and then at Vael. “When I look at your probabilities, Vael,” he said, “I see a zero. For the very first time.”

“A zero?” Vael said. “What do you mean, a zero?”

“A zero is a probability that’s there, on the board, and will never happen. A door painted on a wall.”

“I don’t follow,” said Trista. “What does a thing that never happens have to do with the plan?”

“I was hoping to see it go up.” James rubbed his face. “All the way here, I kept hoping. I’ve watched you every day, and in all that time it hasn’t moved once.”

“What happens in that probability?” Vael asked.

James paused. He looked at each of them, and then at Vael, and set his cask down on the floor beside him, which, for James, was the equivalent of taking off a hat.

“You summon a fourth summon,” he said. “A white figure. I can’t see it clearly. But I know this much for a fact: it turns the tide of this fight, and without it, we don’t have a chance.”

Vael looked to the side. Something moved in him, less a memory than a reflex, as though his body had spoken before his mind had caught up.

“Jack,” he said.

“Jack?” Figo’s hands tightened on Sheela.

“So that’s its name,” said James softly.

“Do you know anything about it?” Trista asked.

Vael shook his head. “No. Only the name.” He let out a long breath, and looked up. “How do we raise the probability?”

James smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. “I’ve never tried it before,” he said. “But given the situation we’re in…” He stood. His hands spread wide, and his voice took on a joy that sat badly in that small iron room. “A shock! A shock to the system is all we need!”

“A shock?” said Trista, more lost than before.

“We need to reroll the probabilities. Numbers settle, but a big enough shock shakes them loose. For a breath, everything’s up for grabs again.” He sat back down and took a breath of his own. “There are two ways to do it. The first one is…” His eyes dropped to the floor. Then he lifted them, and said it to each of them in turn. “Our deaths. Mine, Trista’s, Figo’s.”

Figo flinched. It was not that he was afraid of dying. It was that in all the years he had known James, James had never once spoken of his curse so plainly.

Trista looked at James, and then at the floor. She did not look surprised. For her a sacrifice was a plausible answer; she would sooner die than watch anyone else do it, and Vael could see her weighing it.

“No way in hell.”

Vael was on his feet. Something had come up in him that he had not known he owned, and his eyes had gone hard with it. “There has to be another way, James!” He paced the three strides the hull allowed. “You think I’d come all this way, after everything, and let you die? We’re a team.” His voice cracked on the last of it. “You’re my only family.”

Trista looked up at him. She saw the fire in him, the stubborn, unshakable thing that had walked out of a tavern with no name and kept walking, and she felt something answer it in her own chest. She wanted to match it.

Figo shot to his feet with a roar of pure frustration that shook dust from the ceiling. “AAAAAAAH! To think we even thought about dying, all to beat this thing! There has to be another way.” He planted himself beside Vael and gripped his shoulder, hard. “We won’t leave you, brother. Not as long as these legs stand and this hand holds Sheela.”

James let them have the moment. Then he said, quietly, “Sit down, friends. I’m not done. There’s one more way.”

He laid it out plainly, and it settled over the hull slowly, like ash. Vael would die in the fight. Not all the way. Far enough that his heart stopped and his breath went out of him, clinically dead, James said, picking the word up carefully, like something that might go off in his hand. And then, before he crossed the line that does not uncross, Trista would bring him back.

“It counts as a shock too,” said James. “Death is death, as far as the numbers care. Another reroll.”

“Then why don’t we just kill him now,” Figo said, with great reasonableness, “and keep rerolling until it works?”

“I’m right here,” said Vael. “And I’m not a pair of dice.”

“It has to have stakes,” said James. “The system isn’t that easy to fool. A staged death in a cave won’t shake anything loose. It has to be real, in the fight, with the Dracol standing over him and everything on the table.” He turned to Trista. “Trista is a strong healer. I believe she can do this.”

Everyone looked at her. She folded her arms. “You’re trusting me too much,” she said, and looked away, and for a moment her eyes went to nothing: a clearing, a fallen trunk, a pair of folded hands. “I can’t do it. Let’s think of something else.”

Vael did not answer at once. He was thinking of everything she had put her hands on since the tavern. Figo, grey and shaking with venom. A dying soldier she had given a daughter to, at the end. The road behind them, mile on mile of small hurts closed without complaint. She had not saved everyone. Nobody could. But she had never once stopped trying.

“I believe you can do it, Trista,” he said. “This is our best bet.”

She looked at him. She looked at Figo, and at James. ”…I can try,” she said at last. “No promises.”

“Why did you never tell us your numbers?” Vael asked James. “In all this time, I never knew.”

James smiled, small and crooked. “Telling people their probabilities ruins them. Tell a man his odds and he starts living inside them.”

“Then why tell us now?”

“What’s worse than zero?” said James.

Vael thought about it. ”…Fair point.”

He put a hand to his chin, and the others went quiet the way people do when somebody else has started thinking out loud. “All right,” he said. “Here’s the plan.”

They went through it until the sun had cleared the iron collars of the eastern cones. Then they went through it again. Figo could recite his part with his eyes closed, which was how he did most things anyway.

“This is our best bet,” said James, with a smile.

“We’re all going to make it,” said Figo, getting to his feet to prepare for the walk.

Trista rose after him. “This is it.”

“Indeed,” said James, and began gathering his things.

Vael was the last to stand. He took his time over the straps of his pack, because it gave his hands something to do while he found the words.

“People are counting on us,” he said. “Ragnik’s graves. The five in the cart. Everyone who ran out of road before they got this far.” He looked at the three of them in turn, the way you check a rope before you put your weight on it. “If we do this right, someone gets to start over afterward. That’s all I want out of it. That, and the four of us walking back out.”

“All four?” said James.

“All four. That part isn’t a hope. It’s in the plan.”

Nobody answered. It did not need one.

“That wasn’t much of a speech,” said James, after a moment.

“It was the shortest one I had.”

“It was better than the long one,” said Trista, and shouldered her staff.

Figo set his shoulder to the plate and rolled it fully aside, the way another man opens a door he has already decided to walk through, and the morning came in.

Part 2 — The Four Seconds

The avenue ended all at once.

At the last cross-street James stopped, with his hand flat on the nearest engine as if he were taking its pulse. Ahead, between the final ranks, a wedge of white daylight showed where the walls fell away. The cross-street curved off behind the engines, in a long ring around the plaza.

“This is my stop,” he said. “The ring road will take me round to the east wall without once showing me to the plaza. It’s a long walk. Don’t start without me.”

He had a tin lid in his hand. It was the lid of one of Aava’s tins, which she had given him at last on the grounds that it would stop him asking about the others, and he had spent the walk scouring it bright with sand and his sleeve. He turned it once to the sun and a coin of light slid across the engines.

“Once for left. Twice for right. One long flash means stay exactly where you are and don’t be clever. I’ll do the turning around in my head.” He nodded toward the wedge of daylight. “And keep it off the plates. A hurt thing goes toward warmth, so it will keep trying to go home. The longer it takes us, the worse the numbers get.”

“Anything else?” said Vael.

James looked at each of them in turn, the way a man looks at a bridge he has already decided to cross. Then he smiled, and it was nearly the old smile. “Don’t die early, Vael.”

“Don’t blink.”

“I haven’t blinked since breakfast.”

He went off along the ring road at a trot, with the cask banging at his hip, and the ranks of the dead engines closed behind him.

The three of them crouched in the last of the shadows and waited, for longer than any of them liked. Figo counted his own breaths. Trista re-tied every pouch on her belt. Nobody said anything that mattered. Then, high on the far wall, a coin of light winked once, small as a star, and James was there.

They went on to the last engine and looked out.

The plaza lay open under the sun, a field of black stone as wide as a lake, and the heat came off it in slow ripples. At the middle stood the throne, tall as a watchtower, with its jagged crown against the blue. Around its foot ran the circle of iron plates, each wider than a barn, shut flush with the ground so closely that no seam showed. The Dracol lay coiled across them. One thread of smoke went up from it, straight as a plumb line.

Between the circle and the avenue, a crooked scar ran across the paving where the thing had landed, a black crack as wide as a man’s arm. Ants had already found it. They crossed it in a dark thread, going about their day.

The Dracol lifted its head.

Nobody spoke. Its neck rose and settled, carried an inch too low, and the lava in its eyes turned once, slowly, toward the mouth of the avenue. It did not move. It was only listening.

“Stay off them if you can,” said Trista, softly, to the plates. It was Aava’s voice, cool and exact.

“Pray do not fight it upon the iron,” said Figo, in what was meant to be Atver’s, and which came out as a bear reading poetry.

“It’s lying on them now,” said Vael. “So the first thing we do is make it get up.”

“And then keep it up,” said Trista. “Away from them. For as long as we can.”

“And after that?”

Nobody had an answer for that. Vael looked at them both. “Everyone remember the plan?”

“We said it eleven times,” said Figo.

“Twelve is lucky.”

It was a plan with a gentle beginning and a terrible end. Vael would go out alone, on foot, and bring the Dracol off the iron after him. The wolf would slow it. The bird would move him. James would mark the stone from the wall, the one stone in that whole plaza where the tip of the tail could reach and the thick of it could not. And then Vael would stand on it and shout, and let the tail take him.

Four seconds from the moment his heart stopped, Trista had said, and she had said it the way a person says a thing they have checked three times and still do not believe. Four seconds was the window in which she could bring him back. After that she could not. Figo’s task was the other half of the same sentence: take the Dracol’s attention off a girl kneeling over a dead man, and keep it for four seconds.

“Four seconds,” said Figo. “That’s nothing. I once held my breath for longer than that.”

“Figo.”

“I’m going. I’m going.” He went down on one knee behind a toppled hauler’s great wheel at the mouth of the avenue, with Sheela across his thighs. “Come on, my love,” he whispered to her. “Together.”

Trista went down beside him, flat to the stone. Her hand closed around the rust-stained hem of her robe, and she held it there.

Vael stepped out from the shadow of the last engine onto the warm black stone, and he did not look back.

He walked out until the Dracol could see him properly, and stopped.

The Dracol rose. It came up off the plates one limb at a time, and the iron rang under its claws like a bell the size of a barn being struck from underneath. Its left wing, paler than the right and thinner along the edge, unfolded a fraction late. High on the left shoulder a crooked seam of glassy stone wept a thread of orange. Between two plates of its chest, one channel of the glowing lattice stayed dark, like a lamp nobody had lit. It looked at the small figure with a ring on its hand, and then it stepped down off the circle onto the paving, and Vael let out a breath he had been saving for it.

Off the iron. That part, at least, was done.

It came the way the avenue had come at them, dead straight and without hurry. Then it saw him properly, and it stopped being unhurried.

It charged.

The plaza shook. Vael counted the strides, because it was something to do, and at the sixth he opened his hand.

“Wolf.”

The shadow came up out of his own shadow and went across the stone low and fast, a shape of darkness with only its eyes left. It was half the Dracol’s height and it did not try to hold it. It went for the place where the great legs met the ground and tangled there, in the dark between the claws, and the creature’s stride came apart. It stumbled, caught itself, and slowed to a furious crawl, tearing at a thing it could not bite. Behind it, a long way back, the circle lay empty and warm in the sun.

“Good boy,” Vael said. His voice shook only a little. “Bird.”

The spirit bird dropped out of the sky and passed straight through his chest like a cold breath, and for a moment he was not running anymore. He was moving, and the plaza was sliding under him, and he came to rest well to the left of where he had been, out on the open black, with the Dracol swinging its long head round to follow him.

On the east wall, a coin of light flashed. Once. Vael moved left. Twice. Right, a pace. Once, once, and he slid his foot along the paving like a man feeling for a stair in the dark.

Then it held. One long flash, steady, a small white star on the far wall, and he looked down and saw where he was standing: at the very end of the scar, where the crack in the paving ran out into nothing. A black thread of ants went across the toe of his boot.

Plan where to stand, he thought, in a voice that was not his. Then stand there.

It was twenty paces from the wheel. He did not look back. Behind him, he knew, Figo had gone very still on one knee, and Trista had stopped breathing.

The Dracol wrenched one leg free of the wolf.

“Wolf,” said Vael. “Let go.”

The shadow thinned and was gone, and the Dracol, suddenly unhindered, threw its whole weight toward him. It was the ugliest sound he had ever heard: the dragging of that vast body over the stone, and under it a breath like a forge being opened.

He planted his feet. He took a lungful of the hot air.

“I’M HERE!”

It was a stupid thing to shout. It was a small voice in a very large place. But the Dracol heard it, and swung its long neck low, and the whole length of it came around behind, and what came across the stone toward him was the last yards of the tail, the thin whip-end of it, moving faster than anything that size had a right to move.

Vael had time to think, quite clearly: Oh, it’s very fast.

There was no pain at first. There was a sound, a short, flat sound like a door closing in another house, and then he was on his back and the sky was a long way up. His chest was wrong. He tried to take a breath and found nothing there to take it with. Somewhere very far off, a woman was screaming his name.

One.

Figo was out from behind the wheel before the tail had finished its swing. He came past where Vael lay at a dead run, Sheela high in both hands, roaring something that was mostly her name, and went wide around the great body. He took the Dracol on its far flank with all the weight of a very large man who had been afraid for a very long time, and the creature rocked sideways and turned its head from the thing on the ground to the thing on its flank.

Trista was already running. She crossed the twenty paces with her robes snapping out behind her and slid the last few on her knees, and her hands were on Vael’s chest before the dust had settled.

Two.

“Stay,” she said, to no one. “Stay. Stay, you idiot.”

She said the rest in a language that was not a language. The air at her fingertips went the color of a lit lamp. On the far side of the great body a wing came round, flat as a door, and caught Figo across the chest. He took it on Sheela’s haft and slid back twenty feet with his boots cutting two grooves in the stone. He got up. He shouted something rude. It came again.

Three.

It was quiet in the dark. Then it wasn’t.

Pen on paper. A woman humming under her breath, a lullaby, a little flat in the third bar.

Sun lay across a floor of pale boards. There was a table under the window, crowded with charts held flat by river stones, and a notebook open to a page of six small dots with one of them crossed out. A pot of mint stood on the sill, gone leggy in the light. It was a room he knew. He had never been in it, and he knew it.

The woman at the table wore white. She had her back to him and a spare pen through the knot of her hair, and she did not look round. “Sit, Vael,” she said. “I want you to hear this while the light’s good.”

He knew the voice. He had carried it for weeks, in pieces, through every dream that ended too early. It had never come with a back before, or a hand moving across a page.

She set down the pen and turned in her chair, and he saw her face.

It was a careful face. That was the first thing. A long straight nose, a small crease between the brows from reading in bad light, a mouth that held back more than it said. Her fingertips were stained with ink. She looked at him the way she must have looked at every problem she had ever been fond of, steadily, with a tired amusement that said there you are. He knew the set of that mouth. It was the one he made in borrowed mirrors, when he was trying not to say something.

“Mother, I’m supposed to be at the—”

He heard himself say it. It was the voice of someone younger and more tired, and the word fit his mouth like a key in a lock he had forgotten was there.

“It can wait,” said his mother. “Sit.”

He sat. “Why now?”

“Because I have been reading the sky, child, and I do not like what it says.” She folded her ink-stained hands. “And because on the day you need this, I won’t be there to say it. So. Listen.”

On the stones, nothing seemed to be happening at all.

“We Dragodors have had this power since forever,” said Saela, softly, in the voice of a woman reading out a chart. “But only a few of us ever reached the jack of all trades. It begins with muscle. Don’t look for a beast. It’s not a beast. It’s built.”

Then the air above Vael’s open hand grew heavy, the way air does before weather, and something began to be laid there, strand by strand. White muscle, bare as if skin had never been invited, rising out of nothing in long coils. It climbed itself. It found its feet.

“It will be large, tall, and built like something that has never had to ask permission.”

The Dracol had Figo pinned under one claw, and it stopped. Its neck turned slowly toward something it could not place. Trista did not see. Her eyes were shut. Her hands burned.

“It has four eyes,” said Saela. “They open from the bottom up.”

The first opened, low in the white face. Then the second. The third.

“And it smiles. Always. Don’t mistake it for kindness.” Her eyes were very steady. “It has never once lost, Vael.”

The fourth eye opened.

The Dracol took one step back. For the first time in all its long life, it flinched. It let Figo fall. It looked at the thing that stood over the still shape on the stones with a low sound in its throat, half fear and half something close to wonder, as if somebody had shown it a mirror. Then it opened its jaws and roared, and the sound was small, and the thing only smiled.

“Don’t wait for the wolf, or the toad, or the bird,” Saela said. “It keeps them folded away inside. It fights on instinct, and what it does, it does well.”

The Dracol lunged.

Jack did not dodge. It caught the great jaw in one white hand and lifted, and the Dracol came off the stones and was thrown. It hit the plaza and skidded and rolled. Jack was there before it stopped rolling. It brought one fist down on the split in the left shoulder, and the seam went white and then wide, and the sound was a bell being broken. It brought the second fist down on the pale wing. It did not hurry. It did not stop smiling.

“The only way to truly control it,” said Saela, and her voice seemed to come from farther off, “is skill, and pure grief. You have the first.”

She rose from the table and went to the window and put her hand against the glass. Outside there was only summer: a meadow gone thick with clover, a boy waving at goats, a hawk wheeling over a hill. Nothing was coming. It was the loveliest afternoon in the world.

“Plan where to stand. Then stand there, even when the plan breaks. And keep going, Vael.” She turned and looked at him, and there was nothing in her face but love. “Because that’s what a Dragodor is, and always will be.”

Out on the stones, the Dracol lay across its own broken wing, one eye rolling. Jack stood over it, white and patient, and drew back its fist for the last blow.

Four.

The room went white.

Vael woke with a gasp.

It was a huge, ugly, whooping thing, the breath of a man hauled out of deep water, and his whole chest went with it, ribs and lung and heart, in one long ragged ache. Someone’s hands were hot on his sternum. Someone was sobbing his name over and over, and swearing, and laughing.

He opened his eyes. Trista’s face hung over him, wet and furious and ruined with crying. Her robe was torn at the shoulder, and there was rust on her cheek, and she was shaking so hard that the hands on his chest shook with her. He thought, absurdly, that he had never been so glad to be shouted at.

“Hi,” he said, in a croak.

“You idiot,” said Trista. Then, at the top of her lungs, to the whole empty city: “VAEL HAS WOKEN UP!!”

Behind her the great white shape brought its fist down, and the Dracol did not get up. Jack stood over the ruin a moment, patient and smiling. Then the edges of it began to thin, like a lamp whose oil is done. Vael tried to sit, and found that his legs had not come back with the rest of him. It seemed a small price.

On the east wall, a coin of light flashed. Once, twice, again and again and again, like a boy waving. A moment later James’s voice drifted down across half a mile of air, thin and cracked and entirely delighted.

“Look at that…”

He kept smiling. Then, quieter, to himself, as if testing the weight of it: “We won. Against fate.”

Across the broken thing, Figo was still standing where the Dracol had dropped him, with Sheela in one hand and the other braced on his knee. He did not come running. He seemed to have forgotten he had legs. He stared at the ruin on the stones, and then at the two of them, and then he lifted Sheela over his head like a man showing a baby to a crowd, threw back his own head, and bellowed at the sky until his voice broke.

“WE WON!”

It was a happy bellow. It had hardly any words in it. It went up and up over the dead engines and the warm black stone and the quiet, sleeping city, and no one stopped him.
