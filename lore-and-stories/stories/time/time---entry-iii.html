Approximately 3,350 BCE.

I scratched a line inside the cup. After five mornings of wondering whether I was imagining it, I marked the level of the tea with the point of my knife before bed, and when I came down it was a finger below the mark and cold all the way through. I wasn’t imagining it. Somebody drinks it, or something does, and whenever I’m awake the cup is empty and the tower is as empty as it ever was.

Two nights ago I left a note under the cup. Who are you? I wrote it in my own hand, which seemed the polite thing. The tea went down and the note went with it, and nothing came back. I stood over that table with both hands flat on the wood. Somebody in this tower is reading what I write to them, and I would like very much to know who gave them leave.

I’ve done what I said I would. I’m reading everything. It has taken most of the autumn, because I can’t open a page without wondering whether it’s the one the letter meant, the one that tells me who was warned and who wasn’t. The table isn’t big enough for the piles, so I sit on the floor, and I’ve slept there more nights than I’ve slept in the bed. A few days ago I woke up with a ledger on my face and a basket of fish at the foot of the stair, where Tam’s family has always left it. It had been there long enough that I threw it off the cliff. I’d forgotten about eating.

It was a name that stopped me. I was copying names onto the long wall, the only one without shelves, with a stick of charcoal, because that’s how I think: dates down the left and names down the right, as I would for a manifest. Late one night I wrote Asterion and found it was already there, three lines up, in my own writing.

I knew the name before I knew I knew it. It was at the bottom of the Concord charters that used to cross my desk with the trade papers. Asterion, founding authority. The old cartographer who drew the star lines. We all had the same joke about him, that he must have signed the first charter with a chisel, and nobody ever thought it was anything but a joke.

I had seen it the very first morning, in the funeral account. I saw it and I did nothing. I am extremely good at that. I told myself that names repeat, and that a man in a page eight thousand years old who happened to share one with a signature was no business of mine, and I filed him. When he turned up in the library account I put a line under the name and kept reading. It’s a talent. I had nine years of practice.

So I took the funeral account down and read it properly. The page says he is standing at the edge of a terrace with blue and violet paint on his fingers while they lower his best student into the ground. It says he taught her to see a sunrise eighty years before. Eighty years, and he hasn’t changed. Somebody behind him whispers that the master is a painting that refuses to dry.

The library account, eight thousand years later, has the same man holding the same brush.

I held my hands up to the lamp. They were the hands I’d had on the road.

I said his name out loud. It was the first name I’d said to the room since Tam’s, and I said it to the stairs, because they’re who I talk to. “He’s like me,” I said. The stairs had nothing to add.

I have lived in this tower a hundred and forty winters believing I was the only one. All that time he was across the water, losing students and burying them, and signing charters. And whoever is sending me these pages has known it, and put a funeral in my hands and not one line to say there is someone else. The letter told me to read everything. It never told me there was a man in it like me.

I was angrier than I’ve been since the gate.

The funeral page also mentions a star, low in the south, shivering. I realized I hadn’t looked at that corner of the sky once in a hundred and forty winters. I went out on the cliff with my coat held shut. It took me a while. Then I found it, and it was doing exactly what the page said, a slow even tremble that the wind didn’t explain. I stood there until my feet went numb. It was the first thing out of any of those pages that I had ever seen with my own eyes, and I don’t know what to do with the fact that it’s true. When I came in I barred the door twice.

Then three days of nothing, and a night with no wind. The feeling had been in my chest since supper, that homesickness for nowhere I’ve never got used to. In the morning the bundle was on the table with the bar still in its brackets. I knew before I touched it that I would bleed, and I did, onto my cuff, the sixth time this season. The cold went up past my elbow and my back teeth rang. I held a cloth to my nose until it stopped, and then I untied the cord.

It was a city so clean it hurt to read about. White stone and silver banners, and even the wind was arranged. The page says they have a name for how they do it, the Silent Veils, and I read that twice because it sounded like something my old employers would have been proud of. In the middle of the city there’s a fountain, and Asterion is standing at it beside the High Prelate, who tells him the world has finally been put in its proper place. No chaos. We have perfected the Reflection.

Asterion reaches out with his brush and touches the water, and the water pushes back. The page says it is hard, and that it shakes under the bristles. He tells the Prelate that he hasn’t perfected the world, he has silenced it. This water wants to be a river, not a monument. He says the city is running up a debt it can’t pay, and that one day the reflection will break and the dark will come for the pieces.

That is where the page ends. It doesn’t say what the Prelate answered.

I stood up and went to the window because my hands wouldn’t stay still.

It was the Prelate’s words that did it. I know that kind of talk. I spent nine years among people who spoke it, who believed a thing was safe once it had stopped moving. No chaos. We have perfected. I signed forms that said it in more words, and I said parts of it myself over dinner and was proud of how I sounded.

And Asterion, with his brush against the water, was telling a man like that that it would end. I don’t know what he meant by the dark. But the Prelate’s city has white stone and spires, and the library page has a white tower breaking apart. I laid the two pages side by side on the floor. The one says the reflection will break and the dark will come for the pieces. The other has the pieces, floating up. It could be the same city. It could be that every city in that country is made of white stone, and I’m a woman who has read too many pages in a row. But it is the first time in all those pages that anyone has said out loud that something was coming. I went through the shelves that afternoon looking for what happened next. There isn’t anything. The page stops on his line.

[margin] He told the Prelate something was coming. What? Page stops.

I wondered if the people who unwrote me had anything to do with a city like that. I don’t know. To me the Concord was only ever a name on a shipping form. But I’d bet this tower that someone in that white city keeps a drawer with a false bottom.

That night I understood the rest of it, with the lamp low. He’s alive. Right now, across the sea, there is a man who has buried everyone he ever cared for, and he is still out there at the edge of things with a brush in his hand. I don’t know what he says to people. I don’t know if anyone has ever listened.

I wanted to write to him so badly that it frightened me. I started four letters. I burned the first three. The one I kept says:

You don’t know me. I keep a tower on a cold coast and I have been reading you for months. I think you may be the only other one. I don’t know what to ask. I wanted you to know that someone has been paying attention.

I couldn’t sign it. I wrote the name the village uses and crossed it out, because I know what it is to have a name on a page that people aren’t allowed to read. It’s at the bottom of the desk drawer. I haven’t sent it. If anyone in that city knows my old employers, a letter from a woman who isn’t supposed to exist is the quickest way for them to find her.

The last one came three nights later, and it wasn’t like the others.

It was a single sheet, folded in four and tucked inside the cover of the book I’d been reading, where I would find it. No cord. I knew it was there because the cold bit into my hand when I opened the cover.

The right side of the page was gone in one smooth curve. There was no tear and no burn. When I held it to the lamp, the paper near the gap was colder than the rest.

The writing was mine, and it was the worst I’d seen. It sloped to the right and dug into the paper, which is what my hand does when I’m frightened. Every line stopped where the curve began. This is all there is:

In the three thousandth year since the Starfall

They have built a city in the hole. Rings of walls, and numbers on the doors. Nobody who lives in it has ever asked what it is a hole

They sell the light that comes up out of it. I watched them do it. It is the same light. It is the same

I had never seen the word. Starfall. I said it out loud once, to the room. A star falls. I thought of the library page, the ink rising and the white tower breaking and the ground opening with a violet light in it, and I didn’t need to think any further. The writer had put it first on the page, where you put a date everyone is expected to know, and there was only one day in all those pages that anyone would have a name for. The day in the library is the Starfall. Somebody has given it a name, and the name has a star in it.

I did the sum anyway. Three thousand years after.

So it has a name now, and I know no more than I did an hour ago. I know what it looks like from one balcony. I don’t know what causes it. I don’t know if it’s a war or a quake or something worse, or how far it reaches, or whether anyone besides one man in one library lives to write it down. I had thought of it as the end of the shelf. It had never crossed my mind that the pages would go on, that there would be years after it, and people living in them, and someone sitting in a room writing the date.

I laughed. It came up before I could stop it, and I put my hand over my mouth, and then the tears came too. The ground opens, and three thousand years later they build a city in it. I thought of every clerk I’ve ever known, every careful person with a stamp and a number, and I thought: of course they did.

Then I stopped laughing.

The Keep is still standing.

I read it until the words went strange. This tower, three thousand years after the Starfall, and somebody still living in it whom nobody remembers. If I’m the one writing these pages, then I’m still here, and I don’t know what happens to me in between. If I’m not, then someone else is sitting at this table with my handwriting, drinking my tea. I don’t know which is worse.

There was no room for the sheet on the wall. I taped a long strip of paper to the end of the last column and ruled it out past everything I’d written, and pinned the page at the very end, where the dates stop and the charcoal runs out. I can see it from the bed.

[margin, shaky] Every line stops. Whatever they saw, they never got to the end of it.

[margin, pressed hard] I’ll find out.

I put out a fresh cup before I went up. I marked the level.
