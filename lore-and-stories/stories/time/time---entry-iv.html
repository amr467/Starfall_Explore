Approximately 3,338 BCE.

Twelve winters since the page with the missing edge, and nothing happened. I’ve decided to be grateful.

Seven deliveries in all that time, fewer than I used to get in a single spring. A tax roll from a town that’s under the sea now. A recipe for fish stew in my own handwriting, with more salt in the margin. I’ve made it eleven times and it is better with the salt. The cold in my hand lasts an hour each time. I bleed onto a cuff twice a year. I’ve got used to it the way you get used to a door that sticks.

I stopped leaving notes under the cup. You can only ask a cup who it is so many times. The tea still goes down a finger every night and I still mark the level with my knife. I’m not angry anymore. Some nights I say good night to it. It’s quiet up here.

Tam’s granddaughter has a grey head now. She still nods at me like I’m a statue. Her daughter leaves the fish.

I wrote to Asterion eleven more times and burned ten of them. The one I kept is in the drawer on top of the first, and neither has a name at the bottom. I read everything again from the first shelf and made a list of everyone he warned. It’s longer than I thought it would be. Nobody on it listened. I don’t know what that means. I think it might only mean that.

Every night I read the page with the missing edge, the one pinned where the charcoal stops. I know it by heart. I say they have built a city in the hole to the stairs. The stairs say nothing back. I tell myself I’ve stopped hoping for the rest of it. I say so every night, in the same voice I use to say I’m not hungry.

It came last night, and it hurt more than all the others put together.

It started at dawn with the homesickness in my chest, the usual one, but wrong. It was heavy. It sat on my ribs all morning while I swept and hauled water, and I couldn’t tell you now what else I did. By afternoon I couldn’t finish a thought. At dusk I put the kettle on because my hands still knew how.

The cold hit my right hand with the kettle in it. All at once, like someone had dropped my hand in the river and left the rest of me up here. The kettle fell off the hook and boiling water went over my ankle and I felt nothing. I felt my teeth. Every tooth in my head was ringing. Then it was in my jaw and behind my eyes, and I was on the floor with my cheek on the stones and my hand curled against my chest like a dead bird.

I tried to say something to the stairs. It came out as a noise.

Then the blood. My nose first, and I think my ear. I lay there and couldn’t remember what order you breathe in. I thought, this is it, I’m going to die on the kitchen floor next to a kettle. I thought of the guard and the pear. Nobody would know for a week. Tam’s family would find the fish still sitting in the basket, and there would be no one by that name, anywhere, and I’d have done their work for them by being too stubborn to leave. It was a very neat thought. I hated how neat it was. I held onto it anyway.

I don’t know how long I was down. When I could see again the lamp was out and the room was grey, and my hair was stuck to the floor. My hand was still curled up. I opened it with the other one, a finger at a time, like it belonged to somebody else. The cold was gone. The hand was pink and numb, like something that had been boiled.

The bundle was on the table. Of course it was.

I didn’t go to it. I washed first. I knelt over the basin and scrubbed the blood out of my hair and from under my nails. I was shaking so hard I had to stop and hold the edge. I stayed there until it passed.

Then I thought about the cup.

I thought about the note I left under it. Who are you? In my own hand, because it seemed polite. I thought about telling the stairs that somebody was reading my words and I’d like to know who gave them leave. I thought about the night I shouted how dare you at the shelves, as if whoever sends these was sitting somewhere with a blanket and a plate, deciding how much I could take.

I was angry, and it was cheap.

I dried my hair and sat down at the table. The bundle was bigger than any I’ve had, a thick stack knotted three times in the cord, and in the middle of it, wrapped in a scrap of oiled cloth, something hard. The right hand wouldn’t close, so I untied the cord with my teeth. I said to the room, “All right. I’ll read every word. I won’t ask who gave you leave.” Then I said, “I’m sorry.” It needed saying.

The cup was down a whole finger when I looked.

The hard thing in the cloth is the first object any of them has ever brought me.

It’s a plate of grey metal about as long as my hand and wrist together, bent a little along its length like a piece of pipe split down the middle. The surface is ribbed, with close lines running down it like the gills of a fish. At one end there’s a narrow slit with a light in it, faintly violet, the colour of the page I keep pinned on the wall. The light gets brighter when I bring my thumb near and dimmer when I take it away. The metal is warm, the way something asleep is warm. On the underside there are stamped marks in the trade script I half know from the Concord’s shipping forms. One word, RENOVA, which means nothing to me. A long number. A small circle with a crown. Inside the bend there’s a row of tiny prongs, like teeth with nothing to bite.

I turned it over for a long time. I don’t know what it’s for. I put it face down on the shelf over my bed and it’s still glowing through the cloth, a little violet seam in the dark. I thought about carrying it to the edge of the cliff. I didn’t. I don’t like not knowing what a thing does.

Seventeen sheets, all in my hand, all in the first person. Somebody telling me where they went. This is how it starts.

It was afternoon, and I was standing on a road in a grey coat a size too big.

The road was black glass, cracked into plates. The ground on both sides was warm, and I could feel it through my boots. Out across the flat, columns of smoke went straight up, and at the foot of the nearest one a crowd of people in rags were picking through the dirt with crowbars and sacks, like hens at spilled grain. Whatever they were picking at was still glowing. A man pulled out a lump the size of a loaf, violet inside like a bruise, and three others jumped on him for it. The sky was far too big. There was no mountain in it and no tree, nothing at all to hold it down.

I had my right hand pressed against my chest. It hurt. I was frightened and I kept very still, the way you do when you’re frightened of a whole place. “I’m here,” I said, out loud, to the empty road.

A boy of eight or nine was watching me from a tower made of scrap, with a spyglass in his fist. “Get off the road!” he shouted. There was a lantern on top of the tower. He told me the lamp meant the sky was quiet. Then he told me the sky was never quiet, only less loud, and I was standing in a bad place to stand.

“Where is this?”

“Hollowfall,” he said. “Are you simple?”

I went on.

A man was sitting by the road with a cart of glass jars. Each one was stoppered and labelled in shaky letters: FRESH AIR. TWO COINS. He offered me a breath of last Tuesday. “The good stuff,” he said. “Take a deep one.” I asked what was wrong with the air here. He looked at me so tenderly that I bought one.

Further on there was a plank bridge over a dry ditch, and on the bridge stood a very small, very angry man in a helmet made from a kettle. He wanted a toll. For the ditch, he said. Out of respect for the ditch. I gave him a button. He weighed it in his palm and scowled and said he couldn’t make change from a button. He dug around in a sack and pushed a bundle into my hands, wrapped in oiled cloth. “Fell off a truck,” he said. “Fits anybody with a fitting. Not that you’ve got one.”

“What’s a fitting?”

He laughed so hard he had to sit down on the bridge. I left him there, wheezing.

I stopped here. I looked at the shelf above my bed. The cloth is still on the plate and there’s still a little violet showing through it. I didn’t do anything. I looked at it and I looked at the page, and then I picked the page back up.

At a crossroads two men sat by a fire with a pot on it. They were the politest people I met all day. They stood up when I came near. They called me ma’am and asked me to sit and said there was plenty. I said I was expected somewhere. I didn’t ask what was in the pot. There was a boot beside the fire. I looked at the sky until I was past it.

I went and drank a cup of water standing up.

There were harder faces. A woman sat against a wall with a long cut down her cheek, watching the road as if every person on it was a question. Where her left forearm should have been there was something grey and jointed like a crab’s leg, with a faint violet light in the joints. I had no word for it, so I looked away. At a squat concrete house, another woman with flour on her hands put a cup of water into mine without asking where I’d come from, and told me not to take the west road after dark. Everyone I passed looked up at the sky every few steps, the way you check a pot on a stove.

Toward evening I came to a line of tall poles across the land. There was one every hundred paces, and on top of each a violet lamp, humming. A man on a ladder was mending one. He said it was the last pylon, and that the sky was different past it. I looked up as I crossed the line, and it was. The sky went flat and faintly bright, like looking up through still water. Where the wind touched it I could see it ripple. “The Wall,” the man said. Everything inside was safe, he told me, and everything outside wasn’t, and it cost money to know which side you were on. I asked what held it up. “Magic,” he said, the way you’d say weather.

After the poles, the light was different.

I came into a place of half lit streets just as the lamps were coming on, and half of them didn’t. Little glass bulbs hung on hooks by every door, glowing violet like lanterns. I watched a woman take one down and press it to her wrist before going out. A man was crossing the street in front of me, in the middle of a sentence, one arm raised to make a point, and the arm stopped. The rest of him kept talking. The arm hung in the air like a puppet’s with the string cut. People stepped round him and nobody looked. After a minute it dropped, and he finished his sentence. I asked a woman beside me if he was ill. She looked at me as though I’d asked if the sun was warm.

“He’s behind,” she said. “On the arm.”

Behind on what? I’ve read that line eleven times. I wrote it in the second book. Behind, on the arm. I don’t know what it means.

At the end of that street was a wide road with a name chalked on a board at the corner. CONTRACT ROW. Every wall was a board and every board was covered in chalk. Jobs, written out in the open for anyone to read. HAUL THIS. FIND MY DOG. Some had prices next to them that I didn’t like. Halfway down was a shop with a painted teapot over the door, and there was no tea in it.

A truck stood at the curb, six wheels, old and dented, with one door bent and tied shut with wire. Down the side, in red paint that someone had done by hand and done badly, it said FRESH MEAT SQUAD.

Four people came out of the shop. A tall, tired man, laughing and crying at once with his hand over his face. A young man with a bandaged hand and a split eyebrow, shouting at the whole street, “Free as a bird!” and walking backwards in front of a narrow, quiet woman with a long gun on her back. “Don’t,” said the woman, flat as a table. Behind them a round man in a clean shirt waved from the doorway with a ledger in one hand and a box of matches in the other.

The young man talked the way a tray falls, loud and all at once. When he saw me he stopped talking, which seemed to cost him, and tipped an imaginary hat.

“Mornin’, ma’am.”

“It’s afternoon.”

“That too.” He squinted at me. “You’s lost, ain’t you? Everbody that stands like that’s lost. Center’s thataway. Ride the Ring Line till the lights git ugly.”

The quiet woman looked at me a beat too long. I think she was checking something she half remembered. Then she turned away, and the young man was talking again, and the truck coughed and pulled out. I watched it to the corner. I didn’t know what they’d won. Someone had been let out of a debt. I could tell from the way the tall man walked.

I liked them. I’ve known those four for one page and I wish I knew what they did next.

At the end of the Row there was a low stone house with a lantern over the door. Above the door, in neat white letters painted by a patient hand: THE VIGIL.

There was a line outside it. Mothers with children, and old men. A woman at the door gave every child a heel of bread and every grown person a paper bag of dull violet stones. “Cheap and clean enough,” she said. “Pay what you have.” Next to the house was a small square where people stopped to set a pebble on a worn paving stone and say a name softly. Jade. I asked who she had been. “Ask somebody else,” they told me, not unkindly.

I had to stop. I know that word.

It’s the word on the three who came into my house and sat in my chair and read my pages. They walked me out before dawn and gave me a coat that wasn’t mine. And on this page they hand out bread, and the children take it from them and aren’t afraid.

I don’t know if they’re the same people. Names outlive the people who carry them. I know that better than most. But I got up and walked out of the room and walked back in, and I couldn’t tell you whether the bread makes it better or worse.

I asked the woman at the door if she’d ever heard of a keep. A tower on a cold coast, far south, with a dead lens at the top. She said there was a story like that. A light in the south that somebody keeps. Nobody goes. Nobody remembers who. She said it the way you tell a child a story, and turned to the next in line.

I know that line. It’s on my wall. I looked at it from here and I didn’t need to read it.

The Ring Line was a train that ran in a circle, and I got on without a ticket because the crowd carried me.

The car was packed with tired people. A boy slept on his feet against a pole. A woman held a paper bag of violet stones to her chest. A man’s hand on the rail moved a little too smoothly, and I watched it for two stops before I understood what was wrong with it. Through the window the sky was the same flat shining sheet. The people in the Seam had looked up every few steps. Here they glanced and looked away.

The Market Belt was the most tired place I had ever seen. Housing towers climbed so high that the streets between them lay in permanent shade, with washing hung between the windows like flags of surrender. There were markets, huge ones, where food and tools and news changed hands, and everything was a little cheaper and a little worse than the thing beside it. There were bright doorways where people queued and held out a wrist, and a man in a clean apron counted the violet light into them from a glass tube, a full measure for some, a quarter for others. The ones who bought a quarter came out looking ill. Nobody in the queue said anything about it. They looked at each other’s wrists.

I crossed a square where nothing had been built. The streets bent around it. An old man crossing took the long way round the edge, and when I asked why he said only, “We remember.”

I climbed a long stair out of the Belt and told it I was going up. A man glanced at me and moved away. Then the world got clean.

The streets were wide and the stone was pale. The people on them were smooth. That was the only word I had. Every one of them was fitted with something fine at the wrist or the throat, a seam so thin you could hardly see it, and they moved without a hitch or a wasted step. They were the first people I’d seen all day who didn’t look tired. They looked at me the way you look at a stain on a good cloth, politely, and then they didn’t look at all. None of them looked up.

In the middle of the Terraces was a garden. A square of grass and a dozen trees under a roof of lamps. I walked to the nearest tree and took a leaf between my fingers. It was real. It was cool and a little damp. I stood there until a man in grey asked if I was quite well, and I found I was crying.

In the north, over the roofs, there were cables. A dozen of them, thin as hairs from where I stood, running up from towers into the evening sky. At the far end of them, high and small and dark, were islands. I said it out loud, twice. “Islands. In the sky.”

I cried at the leaf too, in my kitchen, with my hair still wet. The islands I don’t even want to think about.

The Heart was the brightest place I had ever been, and it hurt to look at.

The light came from everywhere, a steady violet glow that never flickered or dimmed, and I couldn’t find a lamp it came from. The people here were rich in a way I had no word for. They carried nothing. Their clothes looked as though nobody had ever sat in them. They didn’t hurry, and their faces were calm in a way that made the back of my neck go cold. None of them looked at the sky, and none of them looked at me. In the middle of it all stood a tower taller than any other, its head bent forward over the city like a man looking down a well. A man with a briefcase told me it was called the Spire, and that it looked straight down into the Scar, and that everything else in the city was measured against it. He told me the people who lived here never walked through the doors of the great Exchange. They bought the light through brokers, at the highest price in the city. I could see the doors, and the light behind them. It was the same as the stones in the paper bags, only purer.

I walked to the edge.

The Heart was a ring of towers standing shoulder to shoulder around an opening in the ground. It was smaller than I’d expected, two or three hundred paces across, like a square somebody forgot to pave. The rest of the old crater was built over, terrace on terrace, lamps everywhere, and this opening in the middle was the one piece they’d left bare. This was where the middle of the Concord had been. It went down further than the lamps reached. At the bottom there was light. It came up out of the ground in veins, violet, and the towers around the lip drank it. I stood at the rail with the wind in my coat and I knew that light. It was the light that came up out of the floor of the library when the white tower broke and the ink rose. It hung above the opening like breath on a cold morning. It was the same light. A whole city had been built on selling it.

It was very steady. I noticed that and didn’t like it. Every other light in that city flickered a little. This one held still, like a held breath.

Above the opening in the dusk something hung. It was white and gold, a ceiling of broken wall and tower held up by nothing, and the people below never looked up at it. I did. There was a gold frieze along the underside of one huge piece of wall, broken in three places. I knew that too. It was at the head of every charter I’d ever seen. The Concord, whole and proud and certain to last until the sea ran dry, hung over a city of people who had stopped noticing it, with the gold gone grey from the glow below.

Most of the old world had been built over. I found what was left of it in a gap in the ring of towers, where the buildings stopped and there were only stones. Old ones, laid out in the shapes of rooms with no walls. Faint lines in the floor where the shelves had stood. Beside them was a polished plaque with gold letters. MERIDIAN. Under that, the library. Under that, in smaller letters, in remembrance.

Somebody had named a whole city after a library.

I stood on the stones. They were warm underfoot. A man called Asterion had stood here once, finishing a map.

I know that floor. I’ve read that floor. I stood on it every time I read the library page, and now somebody has put a gold plaque on it and nobody can say what it’s for.

Not far away was a hall with its doors open, a memorial the corporations paid for. The inscriptions on the wall were polished and the names had been trimmed. I read three lines and asked a couple passing what day it was for. They looked at each other. The man said he thought it had something to do with the light.

Nobody could tell me. The opening was right there, and they had built a city around it, and nobody could say why it was a hole.

By then the sun was long gone. I had been standing at the rail a long time with my hand pressed to my coat, where the bundle from the bridge was warm, and the pain in my arm had come back, slow and deep. I knew I didn’t have long.

There was a streak overhead.

It started at the top of the sky as a thread of white and grew. A long bright fall with a tail like a torn banner, coming down toward the middle of the city. It hit the Wall. There was a sound like a bell cracking, and a ring of violet fire spread out across the whole sky from the place it struck, wide as the horizon, and slowly faded. Pieces of burning light drifted down like ash and went out before they reached the roofs. My hands were shaking. All around me people went on talking. One woman put a hand over her child’s ear without stopping her sentence.

The ring was fading when the light in the pit changed.

It had been steady all evening. Now it went still in a different way. The veins at the bottom stopped moving, all at once, as if something had stopped breathing to listen. The lamps around the ring of towers dimmed one after another, in a line that ran around toward where I was standing. The hum under the city dropped a note. Nobody else seemed to hear it. The man beside me kept talking about the price of light.

The bundle against my chest went hot. The cold in my arm came up through my teeth. I knew, the way you know someone has come into a dark room behind you, that I was being looked at. Me, out of everyone on that rim. I hadn’t thought anything in that place knew I was there.

I should have gone then. I leaned out over the rail instead, to see what was moving down there.

The sheet stops there. The right side is gone in one smooth curve, the same as the old one, and the paper by the gap is colder than the rest. The last two lines are word for word what I’ve had on my wall for twelve years.

I don’t know how long I sat here.

When I could move I got up and took the coat off the hook by the door. The grey one the three gave me at the gate, a size too big, never fitted, never thrown away. I held it. I put my hand in the pocket and found a button in there that isn’t on the coat. I stood with it a minute. Then I put it back where it was and hung the coat up again.

The writer says I’m here to an empty road when they arrive. I read it out loud to the kitchen and my mouth was already making the words, a half second ahead of the page.

It sounds like me.

It’s my hand. It says I. It isn’t me. I’ve never stood on that road. I’ve never had a boy shout at me from a tower. I’ve never walked through a city like that, and I would remember. Somebody in a grey coat stood at a rim and I’m sitting at a table. It sounds like me, but I would know. I would. If it were me I’d know, so it isn’t me, but it sounds like me, and I think I should lie down.

I opened the second book and wrote the date at the top of the page. I wrote the wrong year. I crossed it out and wrote the right one, and then I wasn’t sure which was right. I counted on my fingers like a child, like that first morning. Twelve winters since the last one. I had thirteen. I fixed it. I’m nearly sure I fixed it in the right direction.

I said to the stairs, “It’s not me.” They didn’t argue. They never do.

I took the old page down off the wall and laid it next to the new one on the table. I looked at them a long time. Both say I, both stop in the same place. Then I pinned the new pages up at the end of the chart, right after the old one, so the two broken lines run into each other and don’t join.

[margin, left hand] My hand. Whose I is it.

[margin, beneath it, pressed hard] I’m sorry. I didn’t know what it cost you.

I wrote you, and then I crossed it out and wrote me, and then I crossed that out too and left the line as it was.

I put out a fresh cup before I went up. I didn’t mark the level. Whoever drinks it has earned a full one.
