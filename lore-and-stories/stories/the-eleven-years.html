The Eleven Years

Properties2
TypeShort Story / Primary Document
Era~850 – 789 BCE (Late Era V into Era VI)

Oct 21, 202619 min read

What follows is a reconstruction from several sources: personal correspondence recovered from the outer archive, internal Vigil records recovered separately, and two accounts from individuals who served under Kael Sorveth during his tenure as First Administrator of the Eastern Directorate. The accounts were given to different people at different times and agree on the events while disagreeing on what the events meant.

Kael Sorveth is the only leader in the Imperium’s recorded history who the Witness Body never formally assessed. The trial record for his selection cycle is missing from the outer archive. The body’s private files have not been recovered. Whether the gap is administrative accident or something else is not established.

— The Archivist

I. The Man Who Won

Kael Sorveth passed the trials at thirty-one, which was young — most candidates who reached the final examination were closer to forty, carrying the accumulated weight of two decades of administrative experience and strategic positioning. He carried eleven years of frontier posting and a reputation, among the people who had worked under him, for being difficult to lie to.

He was not magnetic in the way that leaders in the records tend to be described. He was attentive. He remembered things people had said months earlier and referenced them accurately, which unsettled people who were used to being managed rather than heard. He asked questions that had obvious answers and waited to see if you gave the obvious answer or the true one, and he remembered which you had given.

He passed the Physical Trial with competence rather than distinction. He passed the Strategic Trial with something the examining records describe, in the only surviving notation, as an approach not previously observed. The Witness Examination is not on record.

He was appointed First Administrator of the Eastern Directorate at thirty-two and spent the next several years doing his job with the same attentiveness he had brought to everything else — listening to the district reports, walking the supply routes himself, learning the names of the forge workers in each complex he visited. Not because it was good for morale, though it was. Because he was interested. He had always been interested in how things actually worked rather than how they were described as working, and the gap between those two things, in the Eastern Directorate, was considerable.

He did not, in those early years, think much about the Witness Body. It was an institution. It had administered the trials for longer than anyone could remember and produced the leadership the Imperium required. He had passed through it and come out the other side and that was, as far as he understood, the extent of their relationship.

He was wrong about this, but it took him years to understand how wrong.

II. The First Thing He Noticed

It was a small thing, and he almost missed it.

He was reviewing the previous administrator’s resource allocation records — standard practice for a new posting — and found a pattern in the forge complex assignments that didn’t follow the stated logic. Certain production runs were allocated to specific complexes for reasons the paperwork described as efficiency, but the efficiency calculation didn’t hold up when he worked through it himself. Complex Seventeen, specifically, appeared in the records more than its production capacity warranted, and in the years where it appeared most frequently, its output records were either absent or unusually brief.

He made a note. He did not file a report. He had learned, in eleven years on the frontier, that filing reports about things you didn’t yet understand was how you ended up in conversations you weren’t ready for.

Over the following months he found three more things that didn’t follow the stated logic. Each was small. Each was deniable. Together they suggested that some portion of the Directorate’s resources were being allocated to purposes that the standard administrative record didn’t describe, authorized by an office whose documentation he could find referenced but whose actual files he could not locate.

He mentioned this to his deputy administrator, Eryn Voss, over a working dinner. Not as a formal concern — as a puzzle. He framed it as an administrative inefficiency he was trying to trace. She was a careful woman, twelve years his senior, and she listened without expression and said she would look into it.

She came back to him three days later and said she hadn’t been able to find anything either, and changed the subject, and he noted the way she changed the subject — a fraction too smoothly, a fraction too quickly — and filed that away alongside everything else.

He didn’t push. He had time.

III. Varek

The Witness Body member who broke first was not who anyone would have predicted.

His name was Varek — a quiet man, slight, who had been on the inner body for twenty-two years and was known among his colleagues, to the extent that anything personal was known among them, for the precision of his thinking and the absence of anything resembling doubt. He had been the one, in three separate deliberations, to articulate the third position — the argument that risk must be assessed not by what a person has done but by what they might do, the logic that had closed Beth’s variable and a dozen others before her. He had made the argument calmly and been agreed with and moved on.

He encountered Sorveth at a district review in the second year of the administrator’s tenure. These reviews were standard — the body maintained observers at significant administrative postings, a practice so embedded in the institution’s structure that it had stopped requiring justification. Varek was not the observer; he was there in an official capacity, representing the body at the quarterly resource assessment. Sorveth was running the meeting.

What happened at the meeting was unremarkable. What happened afterward, when Sorveth detained him briefly on his way out to ask about a resource allocation discrepancy he had noticed — the same discrepancy, Varek realized with a cold feeling, that the body had been quietly managing for eighteen months — was less unremarkable.

Sorveth didn’t know who Varek was, not really. He knew him as a body representative, a proceduralist, a man who had come to observe the quarterly review and was now being asked about a number that didn’t add up. He asked the question the way he asked all questions: directly, without apparent suspicion, as if the answer were simply something he needed to know.

Varek gave the standard deflection. The administrator nodded, thanked him, and let him go.

On the journey back, Varek thought about the way Sorveth had nodded. It was the nod of a man who had been given a deflection and had noted that it was a deflection and had decided, for now, to accept it. The man knew something was there. He didn’t know what. He was patient.

Varek had sat in judgment on a dozen people over twenty-two years. He knew what it looked like when someone was going to become a problem, and he knew what the body did about problems, and for the first time in twenty-two years he sat with that knowledge and felt something that was not the body’s assessment of a risk variable.

He felt something closer to dread.

IV. The Argument

It took Sorveth four more years to accumulate enough that he was certain something was there, and another year after that to find a thread he could pull without alerting whoever was on the other end.

The thread was a man named Dorath, a forge supervisor at Complex Seventeen, who had been transferred out of the complex seven years earlier and was now running a small operation on the eastern frontier. Sorveth found him through the transfer records — one of many anomalies in the Complex Seventeen documentation — and visited him under the pretext of a routine frontier inspection.

Dorath was fifty-three and careful and had been careful for seven years, which is to say he had been living with something he had decided not to talk about for seven years and was very tired of it. Sorveth had a gift for finding people who were tired of their own silence, and he sat with Dorath for an evening and let the silence do most of the work, and by the end of it he had a partial picture of what Complex Seventeen had been producing and for whom and where it had gone.

It was not the full picture. It was enough.

He went home and sat with it for three days and then wrote a letter to Varek. Not to the Witness Body — to Varek specifically, at the address he had found through administrative records that the body hadn’t thought to obscure because nobody was supposed to be looking.

The letter was three paragraphs. The first described what he knew. The second described what he suspected. The third said: I am not writing to you because I think you will help me. I am writing to you because I think you are the kind of person who, when you sit alone with what I have just described, will find it difficult to continue as you have been continuing. I may be wrong. If I am wrong, I expect I will know about it shortly.

He sealed the letter and sent it and then waited, and while he waited he worked, and he told no one what he had sent or why.

V. Varek Alone

The letter arrived on a Tuesday. Varek read it twice, burned it, and did not sleep.

He had been in the body for twenty-two years. He had joined because he believed what the body believed — that something was coming, that the work mattered, that the cost of the work was not greater than the cost of inaction. He still believed this. He had read the astronomical records. He had seen the models. He understood, with the same precision he brought to everything, what the sky was showing them and how much time remained.

What he had not done, in twenty-two years, was sit alone with the body’s decisions and ask whether the decisions were what the original purpose required or what the institution had become comfortable requiring. These were not the same question. He had treated them as the same question. He had been very good at treating them as the same question.

He thought about Beth, though he didn’t know her name. He thought about the case — Depot-14-South-Logistics-F, which he had been present for, which he had articulated the third position for, which had been decided unanimously and closed. He thought about the reasoning and found he could not fault it. The reasoning was still correct. The variable could not be left open. He had meant every word.

He thought about what it meant that the reasoning was correct and that he could not sleep.

He did not write back to Sorveth. He went to the next body meeting and sat in his usual place and said nothing about the letter, and afterward he went home and sat with the question for another two weeks, and at the end of two weeks he had not resolved it but he had decided that sitting with an unresolved question was not the same as acting on it, and the body needed him, and the work was real, and he went back to the work.

He went back to the work for eight more months. Then something happened that he had not anticipated.

The body assessed Sorveth.

VI. The Assessment

The risk assessment for Sorveth was twelve pages, which was longer than any the body had produced. This was because Sorveth was not a depot administrator or a trade liaison. He was the First Administrator of the Eastern Directorate, selected by the body’s own trial system, and closing his variable required a level of care that closing Beth’s had not.

Varek read the twelve pages and felt the reasoning close around him like something he had built himself and could no longer leave.

The assessment was thorough. It documented Sorveth’s movements, his contacts, his correspondences. It documented the meeting with Dorath. It documented, with a specificity that meant the body had known about the letter before Varek had burned it, the letter Sorveth had sent. It noted that Varek had not disclosed the letter to the body, which the assessment described as a monitoring anomaly requiring separate review.

The assessment’s conclusion was the same conclusion it always was. The variable could not be left open.

Varek read the final page twice, and then he did something he had never done in twenty-two years of body membership. He asked for a deliberation.

VII. The Deliberation

He made the argument carefully. He had always been careful.

He said: Sorveth was selected by our own system. If our system produces men who notice what he noticed and ask what he asked, then closing his variable does not solve our problem. It confirms that the problem is us.

He said: we have been managing the world for three thousand years on the basis that we know what is coming and nobody else does. We have killed people who noticed the edges of what we do. We have bent institutions that were working toward the same end we are working toward. At some point the question is not whether we are right about the threat. The question is whether what we have become is capable of addressing it, or whether we have spent so long managing variables that we have forgotten what the variables are for.

He said: Sorveth does not know what we are. He knows something is there. If we close his variable, we lose a leader the system correctly identified as extraordinary, and we confirm to ourselves that we can do this indefinitely. If we bring him in — not to the body, not fully, but enough — we gain someone who has already proven he can see what we do not want seen, which is exactly the capacity we need.

The body listened. The body deliberated. The body’s response was patient and thorough and engaged with every point he made, and at the end of it the body’s position was unchanged, and the reasoning — which Varek could not fault, which he had helped build over twenty-two years — closed around the question the same way it always had.

The variable could not be left open.

Varek was asked, at the end of the deliberation, whether his own variable required assessment.

He said no. He said he understood the body’s position and would continue his work.

He went home. He sat for a long time. Then he wrote a letter to a woman named Sela, who had been on the body for eleven years and whom Varek had recruited personally and who was, as far as Varek knew, the only other member who had ever shown anything resembling doubt in a closed session.

The letter was two sentences. They are going to close Sorveth. I think we both know what that means for the work.

VIII. Sela

Sela was younger than Varek by fifteen years and had joined the body for different reasons. She had not joined because of the astronomical data — she had joined because Varek had recruited her, and Varek had recruited her because she was brilliant and because her particular brilliance was the ability to hold two contradictory things simultaneously without resolving them prematurely, which was a capacity the body needed and rarely found.

She had been holding two contradictory things for eleven years. The body’s purpose, which she believed in, and the body’s methods, which she had been accommodating rather than believing in. She had told herself the accommodation was temporary — that she was new, that she would understand better when she had more context, that the cases that troubled her would trouble her less once she had the full picture. She had been waiting for the full picture for eleven years.

She read Varek’s letter and understood it was not about Sorveth. It was about whether she was still waiting.

She wrote back: What are you asking me to do?

He wrote back: Nothing yet. I am asking you to know.

She knew. She had known for a long time. She went to the next body meeting and said nothing, and she went to the meeting after that and said nothing, and she went home each time and sat with the two contradictory things and felt the weight of them differently now — less like a condition she was living with and more like a decision she was not yet making.

IX. Sorveth at the End

Sorveth was fifty-one when he understood what he had been dealing with.

He understood it not through a revelation but through an accumulation — a pattern that resolved, one evening in his study, from a collection of separate observations into a single coherent picture. The hidden allocations. The body’s unrecorded deliberations. The personnel transfers that had no administrative logic. The people who had asked too much and disappeared into postings that didn’t exist. He had been assembling the picture for years without seeing it whole, and then one evening he saw it whole.

He sat with it for a long time.

He was not afraid, exactly. He had been afraid before — on the frontier, in situations that had a logic to them, where the danger was external and the response was knowable. This was different. This was the understanding that the institution he had served for twenty years, that had selected him and trusted him with the Eastern Directorate, had been watching him for the entire duration of that service, had read his mail, had assessed his risk, and had — he understood this clearly — decided something about him that had not yet been carried out.

He thought about writing to Varek again. He decided against it. If Varek had received the first letter and not acted against him, something had shifted there, and pulling on that thread might resolve it in the wrong direction.

He thought about what he could do with what he knew, and concluded the answer was almost nothing. He had no proof of anything that would survive outside his own understanding. He had a pattern, a picture assembled from gaps and anomalies, and the people who would need to be convinced were the same people the body had been managing for centuries.

He wrote in his journal that evening — he had always kept a journal, openly, which in retrospect he understood was the reason he was still alive, because a man who writes openly is less dangerous than a man who hides things — and he wrote: I have been fighting something I couldn’t name for twenty years. I know its name now. I don’t think the name changes anything except how I feel about what I have been part of.

He went to work the next morning. He kept doing his job. He kept being extraordinary at it, which was the only defense he had left — a man doing his job so well that closing his variable carried a cost the body had to keep weighing.

He died eight years later of a fever. The body’s records note his death without assessment. Whether this was because he was no longer considered a risk, or because the fever was the assessment, is not established.

X. The Suspension

The trial system was suspended two years after Sorveth’s death.

The official record gives no reason. The inner body’s private deliberation files for this period have not been recovered. What is known from the outer archive is that three members of the body did not appear in records after the suspension, and four members who appear afterward had no prior documented history.

Varek is among the three who disappeared.

Sela is not among the four replacements.

Whether she survived the suspension in some form — reassigned, managed, accommodated in a way the record doesn’t show — is not established. Her name appears once in a document postdating the suspension: a resource allocation request for the observatory expansion, co-signed by an administrator whose posting matches the district where Sela had been stationed. The signature is not identified as hers. The handwriting has not been analyzed. It is a single co-signature on an unremarkable document.

It may mean nothing.

The suspension lasted eleven years. When the trial system resumed, the body was different in ways the outer record cannot fully describe. The Imperium continued. The mandate continued. The work continued.

What changed, in those eleven years, is something the body’s private records would show if they had been recovered. They have not been recovered. What the outer record shows is that in the two centuries following the suspension, the Imperium did not expand. Its administrative records are the most carefully maintained in the entire archive. Nothing appears to go wrong. Nothing appears to happen at all.

A body that had spent three thousand years managing the world had spent eleven years managing itself. What it found there, and what it decided about what it found, produced either a purified institution or a broken one — and the difference between those two outcomes is exactly the kind of thing that would only be visible from inside the private archive.

Kael Sorveth spent twenty years fighting something he couldn’t name. He named it eight years before he died and spent those eight years doing his job in the knowledge of what was watching. I find this harder to read about than almost anything else in this record. I am not certain what that says about me, or about the record.

— The Archivist
