Told by Spank, sky-reader and general nuisance to the Fresh Meat Squad.

You’s writin’ this down? Good. Name’s Spank. Not Spanky. Spank. Grandmaw give me that name on account of the ladle, and I ain’t tellin’ you about the ladle. This here’s the story of the night the star turned.

Rule about falls. Ever’body in the Drift’s got one. Mine is don’t stand under it. Grandmaw’s was don’t stand under it, and never say the sky’s quiet, cause it’s listenin’. I broke the first rule twice and the second one ever’ goddamn day, and I still got all ten fingers. So you’s tell me which one’s the good rule.

I got Grandmaw’s chart. Goat hide, black ink and red, older than dirt. Ever’ star that falls is gonna shiver first, a day or two ahead. Shiver like a wet dog. The chart tells ya which stars to watch and which way they lean, and the lean says where it’s fixin’ to land. Top corner there’s a name wrote down. Asterion.

Grandmaw said he drawed it before there was ever a Meridian. I said ain’t nobody lives that long. She hit me with the ladle. The suits down at the Seam gates say there never was no Asterion, the old families just made him up. The old families say he’s still out yonder somewheres, walkin’ round, drawin’ stuff. I say if he’s alive he kin come git his chart back, cause it’s heavy as a sumbitch.

The Vigil got lookouts on ever’ ward. Sweet folks. They see one comin’ two hours out and ring a bell. Two hours! You’s cain’t git a granny outta bed in two hours. I git a day. That’s why I got a seat in the truck.

The truck was the Biscuit. Six wheels, older than Deacon’s debts. Down the side it said FRESH MEAT SQUAD in red paint, my own handwritin’. Other crews call the new ones fresh meat cause we die first, so I figured if they’s gonna say it I’ll say it bigger. Deacon wanted it painted over. Gasket said it stayed. Gasket said ya cain’t make a thing worse by agreein’ with it.

Deacon ran us. Tall, tired fella who owed a man on Contract Row named Orrin more than the Biscuit was worth, and Orrin added to it ever’ week whether Deacon paid or not. Gasket drove. Old fella with a gray beard and hands black up to the elbow, and he loved that truck more than most men love their wives. Called her sweetheart. Called me worse. She treated him better than most wives treat their men, too, which ain’t hard, I met the wives.

Sara shot. Sara didn’t talk. One word at a time, like it cost her. She was my favorite, and she had the finest ass in the whole goddamn Drift, and I told her so to her face, once. She said “Noted.” That’s one word, and I lived off it for a month.

Night it started we was in a shed in Scrapmoor and I’m up on the Biscuit’s roof with the chart. There ain’t no real dark in the Drift. Just different oranges, cause the Scar light is on the clouds all night. I been watchin’ a star I call the Limper, cause it sits low and crooked. Round midnight it shivered.

I watched it three more times to be sure. Then I kicked the shed door in.

“Up! Fall’s comin’. Before sunup.”

Deacon didn’t move. “How sure?”

“Sure as the ladle.”

“That ain’t a number.”

“It’s a big one.”

Gasket’s voice come up from under the truck. “Where?”

“Hollowfall edge. Burnt flats past the old pylon.”

“How far?”

“Four hours if nobody stops to hit nobody.”

They all looked at me. Rude.

Sara stood up with the long gun. “Sure?”

“Limper’s leanin’ east and it shivered four times. I’m sure.”

She nodded once. That’s how ya know it’s real. Sara don’t move for maybes.

“Sara,” I says, “you gonna say somethin’ else or just stand there lookin’ hot and dangerous?”

“No.”

“I’m takin’ that as a yes.”

We stopped for fuel at Lanternreach. One pump works, and it’s got a nozzle on it so big the Cinder Boys had been sittin’ round admirin’ it half the night. They’d parked across it, lounged on their hoods like it was a porch. The big one slid down and read the side of the truck out loud.

“Fresh meat.”

He started it. I want that wrote down. I hit him with a wrench before he got to the second word. Not hard. He fell down anyway. His buddies come over, Sara put a round in the dirt by their feet, and suddenly ever’body had somewhere else to be. Deacon paid the pump man double for the trouble. Gasket drank tea out of a tin and watched it like a show.

“You could’ve just asked,” says Deacon.

“I did ask. I asked with the wrench.”

Past Lanternreach there’s a Vigil bunker with a green patch on the door. A gray lady with a braid was loadin’ little kids onto a cart. They run a drill ever’ week cause kids forget. Marit, her name. Ever’body knows her.

“No bell tonight,” she says.

“It’ll ring before mornin’. You’s won’t be on the ground.”

She looked at me long. “Yer grandmother said that. Never wrong neither.” Then, “Ground’s hot three hours after. Three, Spank. Not two and a half cause you’s in a hurry.”

“We know.”

“You don’t. It’s always you that don’t.” She shoved a flask at Sara. “Beds here if you’s come back short. We got room.”

I said thanks and meant about half of it.

We got to the ridge at four. Burnt glass all down the flats, flat and shiny like a plate nobody washed. I put my finger on the chart where it was gonna land. Gasket parked the Biscuit nose down so we could go fast. Sara went up on the roof. Deacon got the heat suits out. Two suits, one for him and one for me, cause I’m the only one stupid enough to run into it.

The suit was tight in all the wrong places. Deacon bought it for his own body type and I ain’t got it. Gasket said I looked like a sausage in a sock. Sara checked my straps herself, give ‘em a yank, said “Tight.” I said that’s what she said. She held on to the strap a second longer than she needed to. Maybe I’m lyin’. Maybe I ain’t.

Down the road three sets of headlights stopped. Other crews. Word gets round when somebody calls ‘em early, and I’m a bit famous, which is bad for business. Up over the flats a big grey thing hung a mile high on sacks of sky shard. Renova skiff. Renova don’t ring no bells. Renova just turns up, like a rash.

“Four minutes,” I says.

“You can’t know that,” says Deacon.

“My teeth hurt.”

They do, right before. Never told nobody. Grandmaw’s did too.

Sky opened at five past five. There’s a color it comes in, white and purple at once, and it come down out of the east tearin’ like a bedsheet. The whole ridge lit up. I had the tongs and the crate and I was already countin’ shards in my head.

Sara says, “It turned.”

“What?”

“It turned.”

I looked. She was right. It was comin’ down at my finger and then it bent. Bent like a stick in water. Went off northeast, a long way, and hit the ground seven miles from where I said.

That chart’s never been off more than a stone’s throw. Stars don’t turn.

“GO!” I says.

Gasket was already in the seat. “Hold on to somethin’,” he says, and put the Biscuit off the ridge so hard my crate flipped.

Other crews was runnin’ too. Gasket went straight across the flats, and halfway he took us through the Hollowfall pocket. Gravity’s busted there. Kids learn where it is before they learn their own street. Six seconds the Biscuit was light as a feather and my stomach went out the roof, and Gasket sung. The whole way through. Terrible voice. Hit ever’ note wrong and loved ever’ one.

The skiff beat us. Course it did. A dozen assholes in grey armor, big guns, bigger crates. We come over the rim a minute behind.

The star was in the bottom of a hole. Grey, veined purple, big as a house and cracked all to hell. Ever’ piece of it hummin’. Ya feel it in yer bones. Air tasted like a coin. My teeth was fixin’ to crack.

“Suits,” says Deacon.

We went down. Gasket stayed up on the rim with the engine runnin’, cause Gasket don’t leave the truck. Sara laid on the roof. I went down in the heat and got a shard in ten seconds. Fist-sized and hot, buzzin’ in the tongs like a wasp. Dropped it in the crate. Deacon got two. I got another. Then a dickhead in grey come out of the steam and put a rifle in my face.

I hit him with the shovel.

I enjoyed it. I been waitin’ since Lanternreach. He went down and his gun went off in the air and ever’body in that hole turned round. Then it was on. Two more crews come over the rim. A Renova prick with a net gun got me tangled. Sara shot the net gun. I got untangled. Sara put a round through some fucker’s head about an inch left of my ear and I said thanks and she said “Duck.” Bit late. I’m hittin’ ever’thing grey and yellin’ at Deacon to grab shards and yellin’ at myself to grab shards, and we got six. We got eight. I lost count.

Then a gunner on the skiff line put a burst in the Biscuit.

I seen it. Fifty feet off, crate on my back. The truck jerked. Engine screamed. And she started to roll, slow, then not slow, down the slope with nobody steerin’ right.

I dropped the shovel and ran.

She hit a rock big as a cow and spun once and stopped against the wall with a bang I felt in my feet. I got to the door. Stuck. I pulled it with both hands.

Gasket was still holdin’ the wheel. Like somebody told him not to let go. There was a lot of blood and I ain’t gonna say.

“Spank.” His voice was small. I never heard it small.

“I got ya. I got ya, come on, git out.”

“Nah.” He almost laughed. “Listen. Coupling’s cracked. Hit it left. Left, not right. Ya always go right.”

“Gasket.”

“Don’t let Deacon drive.”

“GASKET!”

He didn’t say nothin’ else. I said his name a few more times. Don’t know how many. Then Sara had me by the back of the suit pullin’ and I kept sayin’ it and she kept pullin’.

After that I don’t remember much. The star cracked again. A big piece split and the whole hole got hotter in a second and ever’body ran. Renova hooked up and went up the lines, cussin’. The other crews took what they had and scattered. Then it was us, three standin’ and one in the driver’s seat, in a hole full of hummin’.

Deacon was cryin’. He didn’t say. He just kept wipin’ his face on the inside of the suit. “We gotta move him.”

“We don’t leave our own on the ground.”

“I know.”

“The Vigil buries ‘em.”

“I know, Spank.”

We carried him round to the passenger side. Took all three of us. Buckled him in cause it was the only way he’d sit. Sara shut his eyes. I stood with my hand on the window till Deacon says we gotta go.

The Biscuit wouldn’t go. Coupling was cracked clean through, like he said. Deacon had Gasket’s little book, greasy, full of pencil drawings of parts. He read it by Sara’s torch and got half of it wrong. Sara held the light, and with her other hand she held on to my sleeve the whole time, and she never said why. They clamped it with a chain. Then it was me.

I ain’t a mechanic. I hit things. That’s my whole skill. But Gasket told me where, and I laid under the truck in the heat and hit the coupling left, left, left, till somethin’ clicked and the Biscuit shuddered like she’d been woke up.

“Did it work?”

“It worked.”

“Gasket’d be unbearable about this.”

“He would.”

Then who drives. Deacon’s a city boy and can’t work the stick. Sara said “No” so quiet I almost missed it. So it was me. The one fucker what’s been told four separate times never to touch the Biscuit. I got in the seat with the blood still on it and put my hands where his hands was.

Worst drivin’ ever. She’s got a hard third, ya gotta shoulder it, and I didn’t know. I ground her on the first rise and the whole truck bucked. Then I did it again.

“I know,” I says to the seat. “I know! She’s got a hard third. Ya told me. Sweetheart, don’t do this to me.”

He didn’t say nothin’. Cause he was dead. I knew he was dead. I kept talkin’ anyway. Told him sorry about the clutch. Told him the other crews was behind us and I’d lose ‘em at the pylon. Talked an hour. Maybe two. Somewhere past the Hollowfall pocket, where the Biscuit goes light and I got no stomach left, I stopped. I’d got to the part where he sings.

It was real quiet after that.

“Spank,” says Deacon.

“I know.”

“He’s been gone two hours.”

“I know.”

“Ya can stop.”

“I’ll stop in a bit.”

We got to the bunker near noon. Marit was at the door like she’d stood there all night. She looked at the Biscuit. She looked at the passenger side.

“Come in,” she says. “There’s room.”

They took him in on a cart. She asked his name and I told her, and she opened a fat book and wrote it down careful. Then she says, “Gasket what?”

I opened my mouth. Nothin’.

I looked at Deacon. He shook his head. Sara shook hers. Nine years he drove us and none of us knew his other name. He was just Gasket. She wrote it like that. One word. Big gap after.

“Fourth from yer end this month,” says Marit. Plain, like a number. “I’ll see he’s put somewhere dry.”

Sara took his boots, hers was burnt through. Deacon took the ammo. That’s how it’s done, nobody argues. I stood in the yard tryin’ to think what I was meant to take. Couldn’t think. Took the rag out his back pocket, the black one he wiped his hands on, and put it in mine.

We sold the shards that night. Deacon put the word out and three buyers come to a loadin’ yard by the Last Pylon Line where the lights go on and off. Eleven shards, grey and purple in the lead crate, still warm.

First was a DeltaPure broker, thin and polite, nice coat. Fair price, said it twice like we’d argue. Second was a prick from Lumen Heights, nicer coat, smilin’ with half his face. His wife’s leg had lapsed, he says, and he don’t wanna wait for no subscription office. Double the broker. I seen his wife once on a poster in the Seam. Good legs, both of ‘em, God rest the lapsed one. I wanted to hit him anyway. I didn’t. I want credit.

Third was a lady in a gray jacket with a little V sewed on the cuff. No name. Tall, sharp, a mouth like she knew somethin’ about ya and wasn’t gonna tell. I wasn’t lookin’. I was lookin’ a little. Sara elbowed me in the ribs so hard I coughed up a noise. I ain’t sayin’ it was jealous. I’m sayin’ it hurt.

The lady looked at the crate then at Deacon. “Triple. All of it. Cash now. No paper.”

“Varkatech,” says Sara. Longest thing she said all night.

“Take it,” says Deacon. He was lookin’ at the floor. Orrin’s due Friday.

Nobody argued. She counted the money out on a crate in neat stacks and I counted it after, cause somebody had to. Gasket’s share went in a jar. Don’t know who for. It’s still on the dash.

She picked up the crate and looked at me. “You’re the one calls ‘em early.”

“That’s me.”

“Did it land where ya said?”

“No.”

She nodded. Little nod. Like she’d been told what she already knew. I opened my mouth to ask how, and she says, “Keep readin’, kid,” and walked off between the trucks with eleven bits of the sky in her arms, and the lights over the yard went off and come back on.

I think about that. I don’t like it.

We drove back to Scrapmoor in the dark. Me drivin’, cause nobody wanted to say I couldn’t. Ground third twice. Seat next to me was empty and I kept talkin’ to it anyway. Told it about the money. Told it Sara said four whole words today, a record. Told it I’d learn the clutch.

Round the old pylon Sara climbed over from the back and sat down in it. She didn’t say nothin’. The seat he rode his last drive in, and she sat in it like it was hers. After a while she put her hand on top of mine on the stick. Third was hard right there. She shouldered it with me, and it went in.

I quit talkin’ to the seat.

When we got in I took the chart out and put it on the hood. Grandmaw’s marks in the corner, little neat ones. Under the old man’s name there’s a gap. I got Gasket’s pencil out of his rag and I wrote one word.

Turned.

Hey, Asterion. If you’s out there drawin’. Yer chart’s been wrong once. I wanna know why.
