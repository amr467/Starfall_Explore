Driver Wanted

Properties3
TypeShort Story
Era~3,000 AD (the Drift, outside Meridian)

Nov 14, 202626 min read

Told in turns, by Deacon, Spank and Sara. Some weeks after Contract Row.

Deacon

The debt’s been gone six weeks and I still check my pockets for it.

Nobody tells ya what a free man does on a Tuesday. Three years I paid Orrin and never once pictured the mornin’ after. Turns out it’s quiet. I eat breakfast. I count the money. There’s more of it than there’s ever been, and I count it anyway, in case it changes its mind. Sara says I count like a man defusing something. She’s not wrong.

Spank’s driven the Biscuit all six weeks. In that time she’s lost a mirror and most of her third gear, and he’s lost the right to call himself a driver. Sara’s started holdin’ the dashboard with both hands and says it’s for warmth. She’s wearin’ a coat made out of what I think used to be a tent. So I walked down to Contract Row and chalked it myself.

DRIVER WANTED. APPLY AT THE BISCUIT.

Seven came. In order.

Hobb drove in reverse only. Said it was a condition. I asked what condition and he says, “The kind where ya like to see where ya been.” I gave him points for that, and then he backed us through the shed wall. Shed’s fine. Wall’s a door now.

A woman in a shawl whispered to the truck for ten minutes and called it “him.” Spank, who’d been quiet all mornin’, said “Her” in a voice I ain’t heard since the funeral. The woman left. Fast.

Dobbs drove an ice cream wagon for thirty years. The horn plays a tune when he turns the key and he can’t shut it off, and the Biscuit played it for two days after, every time she hit a bump. Nobody had the heart to say which tune. It was cheerful. We all agreed it was the worst thing that ever happened to us.

A soft-spoken fella in a clean apron said he could drive and he could cook. Sara asked what he cooked. “Whatever comes in,” he says, and smiled, and I went back to the Row that night and added a line to the board. It now says NO CANNIBALS under the first one. Somebody underlined it twice. I’d like to meet the person who underlined it once.

A boy of fifteen with a forged licence drove like an angel, then fainted when Sara looked at him. She hadn’t done nothin’. She’d just looked. I’ve been on the receivin’ end. I don’t blame him.

A broad fella named Corvin watched Spank back the Biscuit into a barrel and said he was a mouth with a truck attached. Sara hit him. I don’t think she decided to. She was standin’, and then Corvin was sittin’, holdin’ a tooth. She said “Sorry” and went to wash her hand. Spank asked me who she was apologisin’ to. I said the tooth, probably.

That was six. The seventh never auditioned. She’d watched the whole circus from an upturned crate with a thermos, a woman past sixty with a face like a boot and forearms like fence posts. When the barrel business was done she stood up, took the keys out of Spank’s hand while he was mid-sentence, and drove the Biscuit out of the yard like she’d stolen it.

Gone four hours. I quit countin’ at two and started writin’ a eulogy at three. When she came back the Biscuit was the right way up, which I consider a miracle, with two chickens in the cab and a man’s hat on the dash. She didn’t say where she’d found ‘em. She climbed down and says, “Third’s hard till she’s warm. You shoulder it.”

Spank went white as a plate.

Gasket said that, word for word, a hundred times. Nobody’d told her. Any driver who spends five minutes behind that wheel would say the same. It didn’t help.

“Name’s Rook,” she says. “Ran buses for the Vigil. Evacuation runs. Never lost a kid.” She thought about it. “Lost two buses.”

I hired her on the spot. Nobody else was gonna apply after the tooth, and Spank had stopped talkin’, which scared me worse than the chickens.

Spank

Now I was sulkin’. A little. I had a seat, and it was a good seat, and I’d been in it forty one days. I counted. Don’t tell Sara I counted.

Mornin’ after, Rook’s standin’ at the driver’s door with her thermos. “Move over, boy.”

I didn’t.

“This here’s Gasket’s,” I says.

“Where’s he?”

“Dead.”

“Then it’s mine now.” Flat as a plank. She looked at the wheel, and at my hands on it, and she says, “I never met him. I’ll drive her like he’d want. I’ll cuss at you like he would. That’s all I got.”

Nobody’d said nothin’ nicer to me in six weeks and I wanted to hit her for it.

My hands wouldn’t come off the wheel. They’d learned where to sit. Sara come round to my side of the truck. She didn’t say it soft. She said “Spank,” flat, like always. But she’d walked round the whole truck to say it, and that’s the soft part. I let go.

The Biscuit’s got a bench up front, three across, and a back seat behind it that Deacon claims by lyin’ across it like he paid for it. I never thought about the front bench till then cause I’d had it to my own self and Gasket’s thermos. Rook on the wheel. Sara at the window, where she likes it, which she’ll say she don’t. Me in the middle, with the stick between my knees like a little fella nobody asked for.

Rook shifted. Her hand come down on my knee. “Ma’am.”

“That’s the stick, boy.”

“That’s my knee.”

“It’s both.”

On the other side, Sara’s shoulder was on mine. Had to be. Ain’t no room. “Don’t,” says Sara.

“I ain’t doin’ nothin’!”

“You’re breathin’ on me.”

“I’m alive, Sara! It’s a requirement!”

She smacked the dash. “It’s three across. Don’t make it weird.” And she didn’t move that shoulder, not one inch, the whole way to the Varkatech turn. I checked. Eight miles.

Rook went diggin’ in the glove box for a rag and come out with Gasket’s notebook. Greasy thing, black cover. She opened it on her knee, drivin’ with her elbow, which I did not love. Deacon leaned up between the seats to see.

“‘Property of Gasket,’” she reads. “‘Short for nothing. Do not ask.’”

I laughed so hard I got hiccups, and then I wasn’t laughin’ no more, and Sara put her hand on the back of my neck. Just for a second. Like she was checkin’ a hot pan.

Rook read the rest out loud like a will.

Third is hard until she’s warm. Shoulder it.

Talk to her. She likes it. Do not tell the boy.

The boy cannot be left alone with fuel.

The boy is not to name anything. (Too late. Biscuit. I have given up.)

If you are reading this I am dead or I have lent her to an idiot. Be kind to her either way.

She stopped. Then she turned the page and read the last line, quieter. “‘Those two. Dear God.’”

“Which two?” I says. “Deacon and Sara? Dear God, that’d be somethin’.”

Deacon coughed behind my ear. Sara looked out the window like the window owed her money. Rook shut the book and passed it across me to her. “That’s yours,” she says. Sara put it in her jacket and said nothin’. It’s in there still.

Sara

The place the Seam calls Varkatech is behind a butcher’s, under a hatch that smells like what the butcher doesn’t sell. Below it there’s a cellar the size of a church, lit violet, with racks of arms and legs and eyes in rows like a cold store. Men in aprons were fitting a hand onto a customer who cried politely the whole time. Cheap parts, cheap suits, and weapons with the numbers filed off. Most of it came off a Renova truck. I know what the truck paint looks like.

Miss V was waiting by a table with her sleeves rolled up.

“The violet stripe,” she said. “That’s the fifth in two years. Same thief, same road. It never sells. I’ve checked every broker in the Belt. Somebody is paying for the one crate nobody can buy.”

“So find the crate,” said Deacon.

“Find who it answers to.” She looked at me when she said it. She does that, and she does it a beat longer than she needs to. I’ve decided I’ll ask her why when I know what I’d do with the answer.

“Why’s she always lookin’ at Sara?” Spank asked, which was exactly the kind of thing Spank asks.

“She’s the only one who never asks what’s in the crate,” said Miss V.

Nobody answered that. She gave us a week and fifty in advance.

I’ll say this about Rook. She drives like a woman with an appointment, and the appointment is with whatever’s in front of her. Deacon sat in the back row and gave directions nobody needed. On the front bench, Spank sat in the middle with his knees jammed against the stick, and my shoulder was against his because there was nowhere else for it to go. He kept very still and breathed carefully, like he was holding something that might spill. I should have shifted. It was a long eight miles.

Deacon

Hollowfall first. Rumour was the lapsed one liked high ground.

Hollowfall’s got the most gravity pockets and the fewest folks who mind. A pocket’s where the Starfall left somethin’ undone. Step in and ya weigh nothin’, or double. Everybody in the ward knows where every one is, same way ya know which stair creaks in yer own house. Rook knew too. She drove the Biscuit into one on purpose, and we floated forty feet. Sara’s tea left her cup and hung there. “Better than a funeral bus,” says Rook. Spank cheered. Sara reached out and caught the tea with the cup, which I’d have paid a week’s wages to see her do on purpose. She’ll tell ya it was an accident. She’ll say it with the cup full.

On a plank bridge over a dry ditch, a very small man in a kettle for a helmet wanted a toll. “For the ditch,” he says. “Out of respect.” Spank started a speech. I paid in a button, ‘cause I’m a coward and it was the cheapest thing I had. The man bit it, scowled, and said, “Another one. Had a woman through a few weeks back paid in a button too. Grey coat, bit too big for her. Can’t tell ya what she looked like. Funny, that.” He scratched under the kettle. “Gave her somethin’ off a truck. She never said thank ya.”

I knew the coat. It wasn’t Miss V, who wears a jacket with a V on the cuff and I’d remember. It was the lost woman outside Orrin’s, the day Spank shouted “free as a bird” down the whole street. He’d tipped his hat to her and told her to ride the Ring Line.

“What woman?” says Spank.

“Outside Orrin’s,” says Sara. “You said good morning. She said it was afternoon.”

“I did not.”

“You did.”

I’d been standin’ eight feet away. I remembered a grey coat and an afternoon. I couldn’t have drawn her face for money. Sara had gone very still, and I know better than to ask her anything before lunch.

A bit further on, a man with a cart of glass jars sold Spank a jar of air for two coins. FRESH AIR, said the label in shaky letters. LAST TUESDAY. Spank asked if it was a good Tuesday. The man said it was the best they’d had, and cried a little. I’ve seen better business sense in a ditch. I let it go. A man gets one mistake a week, and Spank spent his on Monday.

Scrapmoor next, for parts. Scrapmoor’s the ward of what other wards threw away, and its mechanics will tell ya anything was built by Varkatech and then sell ya the part. We asked about a grey soldier with bad joints. Three mechanics said Varkatech built it. A fourth said it built itself. A fifth sold us a spanner.

Then Lanternreach, where Marit keeps the bunker. The Vigil digs ‘em, stocks ‘em with bread and bunks, and when a fall comes the children go down first. Bed’s free. Bread costs a little. I’ve never worked out who pays for the rest and I’ve worked out most things. Somebody does.

Spank gave Marit the first page of the notebook. She read it twice. Then she opened her fat book to the gap after the one word, wrote short for nothing in it in ink, and shut it. Spank said thank you in a voice I’d never heard him use. I counted the stew bowls so as to have somethin’ to do with my face.

“Lapsed come through,” Marit told us. “Four this year. One sat in the yard all afternoon and wouldn’t speak. We fed it. It left the cup washed and hung on the wall.” She tapped a nail by the door. The nail was empty. “Never came back for it.”

Spank

By Glasswick we had seven theories and nine different times of day.

Glasswick’s all glass, on account of the fall dust, which melts into windows if ya spit on it right. Houses is glass, walls is glass, the whole ward’s see through, so nobody lies, cause ever’body knows. Folk walk round in dark specs so they don’t have to look at each other’s business. Ask a Glasswick fella if he likes yer truck and he’ll say it’s ugly, ya paid too much, and the paint’s stole. I lasted forty minutes. I wanted to hit a window. Rook said it was the most honest town she ever drove through and she meant it as an insult.

A glass blower told us the grey soldier was Renova’s. “Old soldiers don’t retire,” he says. “They get filed.”

Tickfall’s the one where ever’ clock is wrong on purpose. A fella told me they figure a star cain’t find a place if the place don’t know what time it is. They ain’t never been hit. They ain’t never been on time, neither. Told us it was a quarter to Thursday. It was Monday. They told us the grey one was Varkatech’s, which the Scrapmoor fellas said too, so that’s two votes for Varkatech and a spanner.

Past that there’s a village with no name. Folks call it the Soup, cause the whole place eats from one pot, and I ain’t askin’ what goes in it. The cook says the soldier come through one night, ate a bowl, said “Post’s closed” to the pot, and left. Sara didn’t say nothin’. She went and stood by the truck for a while.

Rook honked at a fallin’ star. A little one, way off east. She honked, and cussed it, and floored it, and the star went out like it was embarrassed. I seen Sara turn her face to the window so nobody’d catch her smilin’. I caught it. I ain’t tellin’ her.

That night at the Soup, with ever’body else eatin’ from the pot, I got the jar out of my coat. I’d had it three days. I’d been tryin’ to think of a way to do it that wasn’t dumb, and I never did.

“Here,” I says.

“What is it?”

“Last Tuesday. The fella said it was the best one they had.” I couldn’t look at her. “I ain’t got much. I thought you could have a good day, for when you ain’t got one.”

She took it. She turned it over twice. “It’s a jar.”

“I know.”

“It’s empty.”

“It ain’t.”

She put it in her jacket and went back to the pot without a word. I thought I’d done it wrong. Then I seen her hand go to that pocket twice before she sat down, just to check it was there.

Sara

The jar was in my jacket. I’d had it two days. I’d taken it out three times to look at it, which is a stupid thing to do with a jar of nothing, and each time I’d put it back before anyone could see. Nobody had given me something because I might like it in a long time. Gasket did, with his tea tin. Now Spank had, and I didn’t have a way to say that, so I kept it where I could feel it.

Cinderside is built on a slag hill that’s still warm, and its bar, the Soft Landing, is dug into the crater of an old fall. You drink in a bowl. The crews come to lie about the week.

“You’re the sky-reader,” said a man named Brask to the whole room. Big, grinning, drink in hand. “The one who got the place wrong. I hear the ward wants to put it on a plaque. Here lies a boy who called it a day early and a mile out.”

Spank gave the laugh he uses when it hurts. “Day was right.”

I put my cup down.

I crossed the bar without a word. Brask was still grinning when I got to him. “Hey, swe-”

My fist went into his mouth before he finished. It landed square and it hurt my knuckles. I hit him again, in the ribs, and when he folded over I got a hand in his collar and drove his face into the table. The table broke. A cup of something bounced off my sleeve. He tried to get up and I put a knee in his stomach and put him back down, and when he reached for a bottle I stepped on the hand until he let it go.

By then there was a sound in the room like a lot of people not breathing.

I stood up. I pulled my sleeve straight. I was out of breath, and my hand was open and shaking, and I could feel Brask’s blood on my knuckles.

“Whoa!” said Spank. He’d come up behind me, eyes huge. “Whoa. Sara.” He took my wrist, turned my hand over and looked at it. “Are you okay? Is it broke?”

“It’s fine.”

“Did you just defend me?”

“No.”

“You put a fella through a table.”

“He was in my way.”

“He was clear across the room.”

“It’s a small room.” It isn’t. It’s a bowl the size of a barn. I picked up my cup anyway and drank, and wouldn’t look at him, because I knew what his face would be doing. I looked, and I was right. He looked like someone had handed him the sky and told him to keep it. I looked away first. I never look away first.

Rook slid a bag of ice across the bar without turning her head. I put it on my hand, and it wasn’t for the hand.

The barman, who had watched all this with enormous patience, said, “East tower. Foundry Road gate. Ask the man who sleeps.”

We drove out there in the dark. Spank sat in the middle the whole way and I let my shoulder stay where it was.

The Foundry Road gate was quiet. The two towers stood black against the sky, the rope across the road was down for the night, and Tobin was asleep in his chair under the awning, same as the day of the heist. I woke him with my boot.

“I don’t know anything,” he said. “That’s how I’m alive.”

“The east tower.”

He sighed. “Has someone on it. Was there before I got the job. I take a bucket of water up on Mondays and leave it on the landing. Bucket’s empty Tuesday. That’s the arrangement.” He looked at the sky. “It said thank you once.”

He showed us a side door and told us not to say he’d shown us.

At the top there was a guard room four paces wide. A cot, made so tight you could have bounced a coin off it. Boots in a line, toes square. A tin cup, washed and upside down on a nail, with a little lantern stamped on its side. On the wall, rows of chalk marks in fives, rubbed out and redrawn so often the wall had gone white in a square. At the bottom, in careful square writing: DAY 1,411. NOTHING TO REPORT.

The crate sat at the head of the cot where a pillow goes. The lid was off. The shards inside lit the little room like a lamp turned low. I held my hand over it. It was warm.

Nobody was in the room. Nobody had to be.

Spank read the wall twice. “Fourteen hundred and eleven,” he said. “That’s near four years.” He swallowed. “I counted forty one.”

I know this room. I slept in forty like it. I told him, “Four Seven was my post.”

He turned.

“At the Bureau. Skywatch. I had a desk with a screen, and the desk was Four Seven. Across the corridor was Four Six.” I looked at the cot. “I never met them. Opposite shifts. A coat on a chair, a mug on the desk, that was all I ever saw of them. It called me Four Seven at the gate, and nobody alive knew my desk but the one across from it.” My voice did something. I waited for it to stop. “It stood its post, and nobody told it the post closed.”

I had never said that much in a row to anyone. “That’s the bell,” I said. “Part of it. I’ll give you the rest when I can.”

He nodded. He didn’t push. He picked up the washed cup, turned it over, and set it back on the nail exactly the way it had been, with the same tilt. He’s careful with other people’s things. He’s never been careful with his own.

We didn’t take the crate. Four Six stands up straight next to it. Take it away and it’s in pieces by Friday, and I’ve seen what pieces look like on someone I shared a corridor with. I took the tea tin out of my jacket, the one from Gasket’s glove box, made a cup, and left it on the crate. I don’t have a reason.

Going down the stairs in the dark, Spank’s hand found mine on the rail. He didn’t say anything. I didn’t pull away. We got to the bottom and neither of us let go until we saw Tobin.

“Find anything?” Tobin asked.

“Nothing to report,” I said.

Spank

We told Miss V it works for nobody.

“That’s not an answer,” she says.

“It’s the truth,” says Sara.

Miss V looked at her a long while. “That’s worse,” she says, and paid us for the week.

Rook drove us home at a speed I ain’t gonna say out loud. Deacon braced both boots on the back of my seat and said a prayer in a language I don’t think exists. I was in the middle, with the stick at my knee, and Sara’s shoulder on mine, and I didn’t say a word the whole road, cause I’m tryin’ to learn.

Somewhere past Tickfall she says, “You talk too much even when you’re quiet.”

“I ain’t said nothin’.”

“I can hear it.”

Then she leaned over and put her head on my shoulder and stayed there. She was comfortable. I could tell, because she didn’t fidget once. I didn’t know what to do with my arms. My heart was goin’ like I’d run the whole way home, and I was scared to breathe wrong and have her move. I didn’t want her to move.

“Don’t get used to it,” she says, eyes shut. “I’m tired.”

“Yes, Sara.”

Rook looked over, saw, and turned her eyes back to the road with a face like she’d seen nothin’ and would swear to it in court.

Before she dropped off she dug the jar out of her jacket, looked at it a long minute, and set it on the dash next to Gasket’s money jar.

Two jars.

“Shade,” she mumbled. “Keeps the sun off the dash.”

“It’s a jar of Tuesday.”

“It’s glass.”

“You could just say you like it.”

She didn’t answer. She was out. I sat there with her breathin’ against my neck, and Deacon snored in the back, and I never moved my arm the rest of the way home.

She never opened the jar. She never would. Rook honked at the sky, and for once I didn’t mind the noise.

Sara

I woke up when the engine stopped.

It was dark, and the Biscuit ticked as it cooled. My face was in Spank’s collar. He smelled like dust and grease and the Soup, and he hadn’t moved. I could tell from how stiff his shoulder was that he hadn’t moved the whole way home, and my stomach did something I don’t have a name for.

“Is she up?” Deacon whispered from the back.

“No,” Spank whispered. “Leave her.”

I kept my eyes shut.

Rook got out, and then Deacon, and the doors closed one at a time, very softly, like people leaving a nursery. Somebody’s boots went off toward the shed. Then it was just the two of us and the engine ticking.

“Sara,” he said, barely a sound. “We’re home.”

I didn’t answer. I wanted to see what he’d do.

He sat there a long while. Then he slid out from under me an inch at a time, holding my head so it wouldn’t knock the window, and got the door open and came round to my side. He picked me up like it was nothing. He’s no giant, but there’s wire in him, and he carried me across the yard as easy as a sack of flour, which I’d never have told him I noticed.

He almost walked into the pump. He said a very small word to it.

My cot was by the shed wall, made, with Gasket’s blanket folded at the foot. He set me down and pulled the blanket up, and then he tugged it straight, and tugged it again, and I could have told him it was fine.

Something clinked. He’d gone back to the truck and come back with the jar. He set it on the crate beside the cot where I’d see it when I woke.

“Night, Sara,” he said.

He stood there. I thought he might say something else, and he did, so low I almost missed it.

“I’m glad you’re here,” he said. “Real glad.”

It wasn’t the voice he uses for the truck or the jokes or the crowd. There was nothing to hide behind in it. I had known him a long time and I had never heard that one, and it went straight through my ribs and sat there. I kept my eyes shut and didn’t move, and the dark came up warm around me, and I started to sink.

I felt his fingers in my hair.

They went slowly, like he was learning the way, and I knew without looking that he was holding his breath. I’d have told anyone that I hate being touched. I opened my eyes.

He was crouched by the cot with the moon on one side of his face. He froze, caught, his hand still tangled in me.

“You’re awake,” he whispered.

“Your hand’s cold.”

“Sorry.” He started to pull away.

“I didn’t say stop.”

He didn’t. We looked at each other for a long time, and neither of us said anything that mattered. The pump dripped across the yard. In the shed Deacon snored, and Rook told her chickens off in her sleep. His fingers moved again, and I closed my eyes.

After a while his hand came down from my hair and rested along my cheek. It stayed there, careful, like he was waiting to be told to go. I turned my face into it a little. I told myself it was to get comfortable.

His thumb moved once across my cheekbone. It was the gentlest thing anyone has ever done to me, and he did it like he’d been practicing for years and never had anywhere to put it.

“Go to bed, Spank,” I said. It came out wrong. It came out like a different thing.

He didn’t answer. His hand didn’t move. I could feel him standing at the edge of something, and I could hear him working out whether to say it, and he waited there for a long time, and so did I.

“Yes, Sara,” he said at last, very quietly.

Then he leaned down and pressed his mouth to my forehead, quick and light, like he’d decided and was scared to be told off for it. I kept my eyes shut. I didn’t breathe. I told myself I hadn’t felt it.

He went away across the yard, and I heard him tell the pump goodnight too.

I lay there in the dark with my cheek still warm and my forehead warmer.

I’d been on watch so long I’d stopped noticing it. Four years of keeping one eye on the door and my hand near the gun, a desk in my head that I never got up from. I’d thought that was just how I was made. I’d looked at Four Six’s cot that night and seen someone I knew, and I’d gone down those stairs holding a hand I hadn’t asked for, and now I was lying under Gasket’s blanket with nothing in the yard to watch, because someone else was out there.

Nobody ever told me the post closed. I’m not sure he’d have known to. But he’d come and stood beside it anyway, and put the jar where I’d find it.

The jar sat on the crate and caught a little of the moon. I picked it up and held it against my chest, and I slept like I hadn’t in four years.
