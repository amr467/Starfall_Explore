The Passenger Side

The Passenger Side

Properties3
TypeShort Story
Era~3,000 AD (the Drift, outside Meridian)
constellationmeridian

Nov 16, 202620 min read

Told in turns, by Sara and Spank. Two years before Fresh Falls, and the morning after Standing Order.

Sara

Two years ago.

I left the Bureau on foot, at night, the way you leave a room where somebody is sleeping, and I kept walking until the lights of the Seam were small enough to cover with my thumb. I had forty coins and a rifle. The rifle was the only thing I owned that Renova had ever handed me without a form attached. They gave it to me the day I finished training, with a certificate and a handshake, and the certificate was in a drawer in a room I would never see again. I took the rifle because it had never once lied to me. It hit what I pointed it at.

By the second morning I hadn’t slept, and I had mostly stopped being angry, which was worse, because what was left was flat and had no edges to hold on to. I came over a rise and found a ward with one street, a pump, and a bar called the Dry Well. I assume it was named for the well, and I assume the well had been dry for some time.

Inside it was a shed with a tin roof and one lamp. A dog slept under a table. The man behind the bar poured from an unlabelled jug into a cup that had been washed in something that wasn’t water, and I paid him and carried it to the back corner and sat with my back against the wall and the rifle across my knees. I didn’t drink it. I had nowhere to be, so I watched the door the way I had watched a screen for two years, as if something might come through it that I was supposed to warn somebody about.

Nothing came through for an hour. Then two men did, arguing, and one of them was the loudest person I had ever heard in my life.

Spank

Deacon was in a mood, and it’s the reason for most of what come after. Orrin had sent word that mornin’ that the Biscuit’s debt had gone up again, on account of interest on the interest, and I said ya cain’t charge interest on interest, and Deacon said ya can if yer Orrin, and he hadn’t spoke to me in forty miles except to tell me to shut up, which I took as talkin’.

We stopped at the Dry Well for water and for hot water for Gasket’s tea. Gasket won’t take tea from nobody’s kettle but a pump man’s, and he don’t leave the truck for nothin’, so he sat in the Biscuit with the door hangin’ open and the tin in his lap, and me and Deacon went in.

Deacon ordered a water and told it what he thought of it. I looked around. There was a dog and a barkeep with a face like a fist. In the back corner sat a gal with her back against the wall and a rifle longer than her table laid across her knees. She was starin’ at the door like it owed her an apology. She had a clean collar on, the kind ya get from workin’ indoors, and boots that hadn’t been broke in yet, and I thought, that gal walked a long way to get here and ain’t looked once at where she’s goin’.

“Deacon,” I says. “That lady’s lonesome.”

“That lady’s armed.”

“Same thing, out here.”

“Sit down, Spank.”

I went over anyhow. It’s one of my few talents.

“Evenin’!” I says. “Name’s Spank. That’s Deacon over yonder, he’s sad. We got a truck.”

“No,” she says.

“I ain’t asked nothin’ yet.”

“No.”

“That’s a awful big no for somethin’ I ain’t said.”

She looked at me the way ya look at weather. I sat down across from her anyway, cause I ain’t smart, and set my cup of water on the table, and she picked it up and drank the whole thing and put it back empty.

“Now go away,” she says.

“That was my water.”

“Then you’ll want to get more.”

Deacon barked a laugh across the room, the first one I’d heard out of him in a week. I’ll admit I was a little in love by then, in the way a man’s in love with the weather, which is to say I was soaked.

“Ya could smile,” I says. “Wouldn’t kill ya.”

“It might.”

“What’s yer name?”

“No.”

“I’ll call ya Lady, then.”

“Don’t.”

“Ya got a awful lot of rifle for one gal.”

“I have exactly enough.”

That’s when Deacon got up and took me by the back of the collar, which he’d done to me so many times that I’d quit takin’ it personal. “We’re leavin’,” he said. “Now. Before she shoots you and I get stuck with the funeral.”

“I’m only bein’ neighborly!”

“You’ve been neighborly to four women since spring and one of ‘em hit you with a chair.”

“It was a small chair.”

She’d stood up by then. She wasn’t lookin’ at us. She laid a coin on the table for the barkeep and slung the rifle over her shoulder and went for the door, and Deacon went for the door, and I got dragged for the door, so all three of us hit it at the same time and stuck in it like a cork.

Sara

A truck was parked in the dirt out front with the afternoon light on its side. It had six wheels, a cab that had been patched with at least four different colors of metal, and down the door in red paint, in letters a child could have done better, it said FRESH MEAT SQUAD.

In the driver’s seat sat an old man with a gray beard and black hands, drinking tea out of a tin. He looked up at the three of us wedged in the doorway and smiled like Christmas had come early.

“Well, look at that, sweetheart,” he said, and patted the dashboard. “The boy went in for water and come out with a lady. I told ya he had it in him.”

“She’s not with us,” said the tall one, who’d stopped looking like he wanted to die and started looking like he wanted to sit down.

“Evenin’, miss!” said the old man. “Don’t mind these two. One’s a bad mood and the other’s a loose screw, and I ain’t found where either one goes.”

I should have walked on. I had forty coins and a rifle, and nothing I owed anyone. But I stood there in the road looking at the paint, and the patched cab, and the man talking to his truck like it was a horse he was fond of, and something in me that had been flat for two days turned over once, like a coin that hasn’t decided.

“What are you doing?” I said.

They all looked at me. I could tell it was a longer sentence than they had expected.

“We chase falls!” said the loud one.

“Why?”

“Cause nobody else gets there on time.”

“Cause the shards pay Orrin,” said the tall one. “And Orrin eats the shards.”

“What is the Fresh Meat Squad?”

The loud one lit up like I had asked about his children. “Other crews call the new ones fresh meat, cause we die first. So I figured if they was gonna say it, I’d say it bigger. I painted it myself.”

“I said ya cain’t make a thing worse by agreein’ with it,” said the old man, with a great deal of pride. “She wears it good. Don’t ya, baby?” He stroked the wheel. “Boy named her Biscuit too. I’ve given up.”

“What’s your name?” I asked him.

“Gasket.”

“Short for what?”

“Short for nothin’. Don’t ask.”

I didn’t. I had a feeling that if I did, I’d be there all night. “And him?” I nodded at the loud one.

“Spank,” said the loud one. “Grandmaw named me. I ain’t tellin’ ya about the ladle.”

“What do you do, Spank?”

“I read the sky.”

“You read it.”

“I got a chart.” He pulled a roll of goat hide out of his coat and shook it open on the hood, black ink and red, older than anything I’d ever seen. “Ever’ star that’s fixin’ to fall shivers first, a day or two out. The chart tells which ones to watch and which way they lean, and the lean says where it lands. Right now there’s one called the Kettle, and she’s been shiverin’ since last night, and she’s comin’ down on Saltrow tomorrow before sunup.”

“That’s a goat,” I said.

“It’s a good goat.”

“The Vigil rings a bell two hours out. Nobody can know a day ahead.”

“Miss,” said Gasket gently, “I’ve driven this boy a year and a half now and I ain’t once seen him wrong on the day. Only on the hour.”

“I ain’t wrong on the hour neither,” said Spank.

“Ya were wrong on the hour three times last spring.”

“That’s a different thing!”

I looked at the chart. I knew what a screen looked like when it was right, and I knew what a lever felt like when it was the wrong thing to hold. This was a goat with ink on it, in the hands of a boy who talked too much, and I didn’t believe it for a second.

But I had nowhere to be, and I had two days of walking behind me and nothing in front of me except a dog under a table. And I wanted to see if he was right. I wanted it badly, and I couldn’t have said why.

“I’ll come,” I said. “To see.”

“See what?” said Deacon.

“If it falls.”

“Passenger side, miss,” said Gasket, and he pushed the door open with his boot. “She don’t bite. Much.”

The loud one looked at the seat. It was his, I understood, from the way he looked. Then he looked at me, and moved to the middle of the bench without a word, which was the first sensible thing he’d done all day.

Spank

Saltrow is white. That’s what ya notice. There’s salt pans for miles, flat as plates, and the houses stand up out of ‘em on stilts with wind chimes made of glass, and when the sun goes down the whole ward goes pink and then blue and then a color I ain’t got a name for. We got there at dusk. I’d never been prouder of anything than I was of that ward, and I’d never seen it before.

I went door to door. That’s what I do. I told ‘em the Kettle was comin’ tomorrow before sunup, down on the east pan, and they should take the kids up to the dry ridge and the chickens too. A lady laughed in my face. A man shut the door before I’d finished my first word. The Vigil lookout was a bored girl in a hut with a bell, and she says, “Bell says no.”

“The bell ain’t rung yet.”

“Then there ain’t no fall.”

“There will be.”

“Come back when it rings,” she says, and she shut the shutter.

Sara watched me do all this from the roof of the Biscuit with the long gun across her knees. She didn’t say a word the whole time. Deacon sat on the running board and told me what the debt was, to the penny, and what we could have made on the night if I’d just stayed in the truck. Gasket sang to the Biscuit in a voice like a saw hittin’ a nail, and the Biscuit, I swear, sounded pleased.

About midnight I had four families up on the dry ridge. Four. One had a baby that screamed at me. The ridge sits west of the ward, and the east pan where the Kettle was supposed to land is a mile off the other way, so the ridge was safe enough. The folks who’d laughed at me were asleep in their stilt houses, right at the edge of the east pan. I walked back down and stood at the foot of the ridge path with a lantern, in the cold, watchin’ the sky, and nobody came to stand with me.

Sara could see me from the roof of the Biscuit. I felt it, like when ya know somebody’s lookin’.

“It’s been six hours,” she called down.

“I know.”

“Nothing’s fallen.”

“It ain’t time.”

“You’re sure.”

“Lady,” I says, “I don’t know ya name. But I ain’t sure of much. I’m sure of this.”

She never answered. She just watched.

At twenty to five the girl in the hut came out, lookin’ at the sky. The east was goin’ a pale, wrong green. I put my hand up. “Now,” I says, to the whole empty pan, and I heard her bell start up, thin and scared, forty seconds behind me.

The bell woke the ward. People came out of the stilt houses in their nightclothes with the sky turnin’ green over their heads, and in all that dark there was only one light to run toward, which was mine. I stood at the bottom of the path and waved ‘em up it one after another, the same folks that had shut their doors on me, and I never said a word about it. They went past me up the ridge with kids on their hips and chickens under their arms. I counted heads as they went. Eleven kids, and a goat that wasn’t mine.

The Kettle came down at ten to five, a mile off on the east pan.

I’d forgot how pretty they are. It came down out of the green like somebody was lowerin’ a lamp, slow, and then not slow at all, and hit the salt with a hiss and a roar and a cloud of steam that went up white as a sheet and lit the whole ward from underneath. The wind chimes all rang at once. The ground jumped under my boots. By then everybody was on the ridge, and nobody had a hair out of place, except two chickens that was missin’ and that I ain’t sure were ever there.

When the steam cleared, the Kettle sat in a bowl of white salt, hummin’, big as a barn.

I looked back at the Biscuit.

Sara was standin’ on the roof with the rifle down at her side, and she wasn’t movin’. She looked at the hole, and the steam, and the folks, and then at me. Then she climbed down, slow, like her knees weren’t sure of the ground, and walked over the salt to where I stood at the foot of the path, and stopped about three feet away.

“You were right,” she said.

“Ya sound surprised.”

“I am.”

“Wanna see the chart now?”

“Yes.”

I got it out. She looked at it a long time. She traced the lean of the Kettle with one finger, and her finger was shakin’ a little.

“I can’t read this,” she said.

“Nobody can. It’s Asterion’s.”

“Then how did you know?”

“I just do.” I scratched my neck. “Grandmaw’s did too.”

She sat down on the salt. She didn’t ask first. She just sat, with her rifle across her knees, and after a minute I sat down beside her, and we watched the steam go up.

“I used to watch these from a desk,” she said.

“Watch ‘em how?”

“On a screen.”

“Was it any good?”

“No,” said Sara. She said it like she’d never said it before. “It was a number going down.”

I thought about it. I didn’t ask her what she’d been countin’. It wasn’t mine to ask. “Well,” I says. “This is better.”

She looked at the Kettle, humming in its bowl of salt, with the whole ward comin’ out onto the pan to touch it and the kids runnin’ round it screamin’.

“Yes,” she said. “It is.”

“I never got yer name,” I says.

“Sara.”

“Sara.” I tried it out loud. “That’s a good one. It’s short.”

“Yes.”

“I like short.”

She didn’t answer, but she didn’t move away either.

Gasket’s voice came across the salt, cracked and delighted. “Sweetheart! Ya see that? She held!”

I think he was talkin’ to the truck.

We took the front bench home. Gasket drove, Sara got the window, and I got the middle, and I’ve had it ever since. I wouldn’t trade it.

Sara

The morning after.

I woke to daylight coming through the gaps in the shed boards, and I lay there a minute trying to remember where I was. Then I felt the blanket. It had been pulled up to my chin and tucked down both sides, and the edge was folded square, the way I had only ever seen one person fold anything. The jar sat on the crate beside the cot. I remembered my head in his lap and his hand on my shoulder, and my face went hot all the way to my ears.

Somebody was talking outside.

“I ain’t done nothin’ wrong, Gasket,” Spank was saying, low and worried. “I swear to ya. I stayed till she was out and then I went to bed. That’s all. Ya’d have been proud. Or ya’d have said somethin’ rude, and I can hear ya sayin’ it right now.”

I went to the door as quietly as I could and looked out through the crack.

He was sitting on the ground with his back against the Biscuit’s front wheel, holding a tin cup in both hands, and he was looking up at the grille as if the truck had just asked him a hard question. There was dirt on his cheek. He had plainly spent the night on something that wasn’t a bed.

“I don’t know what I’m doin’,” he told it. “I never done this before. Ya’d know. Ya always knew what to do with a truck. What do ya do with a person?” He took a sip and made a face. “This is cold. It’s been cold since last night. I forgot to drink it.”

I could have gone back to the cot and waited until he was done. I had plenty of practice at that. But he had been talking to Gasket every day since the cracked coupling, and I had listened to every word of it from the passenger seat and never once answered, and it seemed like time.

I opened the door, and he nearly dropped the cup.

“Mornin’!” he said, in a voice about an octave higher than his own.

“Good morning.”

“Sleep good?”

“Yes. You?”

“Great. Real great. I slept on a crate.” He got up so fast that cold tea went down his sleeve. “It’s a good crate.”

We stood there while a chicken walked between us, looking at neither one of us, and left.

“So,” I said.

“So,” said Spank.

Neither of us went on. I walked over and sat on the ground against the wheel, and after a moment he sat down too, with a careful gap between us about the width of a rifle.

I put my hand flat on the hood. “Hello, Gasket,” I said.

Spank made a small sound.

“He says hi,” he managed. “He says ya’re late for tea.”

“I know. I’m sorry.” The metal was warm from the sun. “Thank you for the seat.”

For a while neither of us said anything. Over by the pump the chicken made a remark to itself.

“I told you on the tower that the rest wasn’t tonight,” I said at last, “and then it was. I don’t have a good way to say what that was to me.”

“Ya don’t gotta.”

“I want to. I’m not good at it.” I made myself look at him, and he was looking at his boots with his ears gone red. “You never needed me to answer. Everyone else I ever knew wanted an answer, and you just kept talking, and some days it was the only thing that kept me in the truck. I told myself it was your chart.”

“I thought it was the chart.”

“It was a little the chart.”

He laughed, a short surprised laugh, and put his hand over his mouth. I felt my own mouth give way before I could stop it.

“I’m scared I’ll mess it up,” he said, quieter, to his knees.

“You will.”

“Oh.”

“So will I. I don’t know how this works. I’ve never been any good at anything that didn’t have a number on it.”

“Me neither.”

“Then we’ll be bad at it together.”

He looked at me, finally. The sun was on one side of his face and there was straw in his hair. After a moment he took my hand off the hood, carefully, as if it might be hot, and held it on his knee. His palm was rough, and it was sweating. I let it stay.

“You said something last night,” I said.

He went completely still.

“Right at the end. I was almost asleep, but I thought I heard you.”

“I didn’t.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yeah.” His grip tightened. “Ya was dreamin’. I didn’t say a word.”

He was lying so badly that it was painful to watch, like a boy hiding something behind his back that was much too big to hide. I felt a smile start before I could do anything about it, and this time I let it.

“Okay,” I said. “I must have misheard.”

Boots crunched on the gravel at the gate.

We let go so fast that my elbow hit the wheel. Spank jumped to his feet like he’d been stung, and I got up and brushed off my knees with great care, and we ended up standing three feet apart, looking in different directions, both of us as red as brick.

Deacon came through the gate with a folded paper in his hand and a long night under his eyes. Rook followed him with her thermos. She took one look at the two of us and turned her face away, and I saw her shoulders start to shake.

“Don’t,” said Deacon, before either of us spoke. “Whatever that is, I ain’t seen it.”

“Seen what?” said Rook.

“Exactly.”

The chicken gave Spank a long look on its way past, and he went even redder. I found something very interesting about the shed roof.

“Four Six is fine,” Deacon went on, very firmly, to the middle distance. “Sleeps like a tank. Miss V’s cellar men say four days. And she sent this.” He held up the paper. “She laid the halves of that torn form next to the page you kept, Sara. They carry the same stamp in the corner, and it ain’t a Renova form number.”

He unfolded it. It was a small black circle, with a single numeral drawn inside it in a thin hand.

9.

Something cold settled in my stomach.

“What’s it mean?” said Spank. He had stopped being red.

“It’s a desk,” I said.

“A what?”

“Every desk on the floor at the Bureau had a number. Four Six. Four Seven. It’s how we knew each other.” I could hear my own voice, flat and steady, the voice I’d used for two years. “Nine was at the top of the building, past the end of the corridor, behind a door nobody ever opened.”

Nobody said anything. Rook put down her thermos.

“I never saw who sat there,” I said. “Nobody did.”
